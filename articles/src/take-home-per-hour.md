## How it's worked out

Your take-home pay for a year is the net pay on your pay stub times the number of paychecks: 52 if you're paid every week, 26 every two weeks, 24 twice a month and 12 once a month. The costs of getting to work, and any other costs you enter, come off that.

The hours are the hours you work in a typical week, paid or not, plus the time spent commuting, for each week you work. Paid weeks off count as pay with no hours.

The tool divides what's left by those hours. It's a way to see pay and time together, not a wage anyone pays. Some people use a commute for things they'd do anyway, and some costs of working would partly be spent anyway, so treat it as a rough guide.

If your pay changes from check to check, use a typical one. Or use the year-to-date net pay on your latest stub, divided by the number of checks so far.

The national figure is the Bureau of Labor Statistics' average hourly pay for production and nonsupervisory workers in private businesses, not seasonally adjusted: {{fig hour_pay else="the latest figure"}} in {{fig hour_pay_month else="the latest month"}}. It's pay before taxes for each hour paid. So it compares with your pay before taxes, not with your take-home.
