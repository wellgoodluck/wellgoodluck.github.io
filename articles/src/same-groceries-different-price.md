## Short version

Grocery delivery isn't a trap. For someone who can't get to the store, or whose time is worth more than the fee, it can be a good deal. The trap is not knowing what you're paying for it. On a delivery app, the store sets the item prices, and many stores charge more than they do on the shelf. That markup is folded into each item's price, so it never shows up as its own line, while the delivery fee, service fee and tip do. The markup can be the biggest of the four. And until December 2025, some Instacart shoppers were also seeing different prices from other shoppers for the same item at the same store at the same time. The fix is simple: check the store's pricing policy before you shop, and compare one order against the shelf once.

## The trap in one sentence

You see the fees and judge the order by them, and you miss the markup inside every item price, which can cost more than the delivery and service fees put together.

## How it works

**The app shows the store's price, and the store decides what it is.** Take Instacart. It says [retailers set the item prices on its platform](https://delivery.publix.com/help/section/pricing). Many charge their everyday store prices, but some set prices different from in-store. Under each store's logo, the app shows a pricing label, such as "Everyday Store Prices," "Higher than in-store prices" or "View pricing policy." Instacart says markups are the retailer's choice and that [it encourages retailers not to add them](https://company.instacart.com/updates/the-truth-about-pricing-tests-on-instacart), though some do to cover the cost of its technology and same-day delivery.

**The markup is spread across the cart, so it's easy to miss.** A delivery receipt lists a delivery fee, a service fee and a tip. A markup doesn't get a line. It's a few cents or dollars added to each item. A 2021 NBC Los Angeles check of one supermarket's prices on the app found [a 15% to 17% markup on every item it ordered](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/investigations/instacart-cost-delivery-fee/2524359/), and with fees and tip the order cost 35% more than buying the same things in person.

**"Free delivery" wasn't free either.** In December 2025, Instacart agreed to pay [$60 million in refunds to settle Federal Trade Commission charges](https://www.ftc.gov/node/327255). The FTC said the app advertised free delivery on first orders while charging a mandatory service fee that could add as much as 15% to an order. The FTC also said Instacart's "100% satisfaction guarantee" mostly gave small credits toward future orders, not refunds. [Instacart denied wrongdoing](https://ny1.com/nyc/all-boroughs/ap-top-news/2025/12/18/instacart-settles-with-ftc-over-deceptive-practices-but-faces-separate-investigation-into-prices).

**Some shoppers saw a different price than others.** In September 2025, Consumer Reports and two advocacy groups had more than 400 volunteers load the same items from the same stores into Instacart carts at the same moment. [About three-quarters of the items appeared at more than one price](https://groundworkcollaborative.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/Same-Cart-Different-Price.pdf), sometimes five. A dozen eggs at one Safeway ranged from $3.99 to $4.79. Identical baskets differed by about 7% on average. The researchers found no meaningful pattern by shoppers' income, ZIP code or other personal traits. Instacart said these were short, randomized price tests run by [about 10 retail partners that already marked up their prices](https://turnto10.com/news/nation-world/instacart-may-be-pricing-same-products-differently-consumer-reports-groundwork-collaborative-study-shows-shopping), and that it doesn't use shoppers' personal or demographic data to set prices. On December 22, 2025, [it ended all item price tests on its platform](https://company.instacart.com/updates/ending-item-price-tests-on-instacart). [Retailers can still test promotions and discounts there](https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/instacart-stops-ai-pricing-experiments-a1176475852/).

**Some of what you pay for is real.** Someone walks the aisles, picks the produce and drives it to you. The markup and fees pay for that. Loose produce and meat are weighed in the store, so the final price can differ from the estimate, and a substitute can cost more than what you asked for. None of that is a trick. The trap is only paying for it without knowing how much.

## A worked example

Casey buys about $150 of groceries a week at shelf prices. There are three ways to get them (the fees and the markup are made up for the example, but in the range news reports have found):

The delivery-app order looks like it has $12.62 in fees. It actually costs $45.12 more than shopping in person, and $25.17 more than the same delivery from the store's own app. The biggest piece is the markup: $22.50 a week, about $1,170 a year, and it's on no line of the receipt.

Over a year, the delivery-app order costs about $2,350 more than shopping in person, and about $1,310 more than the store's own app. The convenience may well be worth it. The point is to know the price.

## How to avoid it

**Read the pricing policy before you shop.** On Instacart it's under the store's logo. "Everyday Store Prices" means items should match the shelf, though some in-store sales may not apply. "Higher than in-store prices" means the store charges more online than on the shelf, sometimes a flat percentage on every item. "View pricing policy" means you need to tap it to see what that store charges.

**Try the store's own app.** Some chains charge shelf prices in their own app and put the cost of delivery in a fee you can see, which is easier to judge. Some don't. Check one order.

**Check one order against the shelf.** Pick five things you always buy, note the prices on your next store trip, and compare them with the app. One comparison tells you what that store charges online.

**Pickup can cost less than delivery.** It drops the delivery fee and the tip. It doesn't always drop the markup, so check the policy for pickup too.

**Treat "free delivery" as "no delivery fee."** Look for a service fee at checkout, and for a free trial that turns into a paid membership.

**Read the receipt.** Weighed items and substitutions can change the total. If something is wrong, ask for a refund, not just a credit ([reading your statements](reading-your-statements.html) covers the habit).

**Give convenience a number.** If the whole order runs $45 a week more and saves you two hours, that's $22.50 an hour. Sometimes that's a bargain. Decide on purpose.

## The bigger picture

The price tests stopped, but the argument they started didn't. It's over what governments call "personalized" or "surveillance" pricing: using what a business knows about you to set the price it shows you. The Instacart study didn't find that, but it showed how easily two shoppers can see different prices without knowing it.

Governments have moved toward disclosure and, in a few states, bans. New York has required businesses to say when an algorithm used personal data to set a price [since November 2025](https://www.jonesday.com/en/insights/2025/11/new-yorks-novel-algorithmic-pricing-disclosure-law-takes-effect), and as of early November 2026 a broader New York ban, [the One Fair Price Act, was waiting on the governor](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/11/heres-where-key-legislation-stands-gov-kathy-hochul/417125/). On November 12, 2026, the New York City Council [passed a citywide ban](https://council.nyc.gov/press/2026/11/12/3339/), [44 to 5](https://www.cityandstateny.com/policy/2026/11/julie-menins-surveillance-pricing-ban-passes-council/417196/); as of November 16 it was [on the mayor's desk](https://legistar.council.nyc.gov/LegislationDetail.aspx?GUID=A6815BBB-AC2B-487F-A27B-46449A644474&ID=8027985). {{due 2027-01-15 "New York's One Fair Price Act: signed, vetoed or amended by the governor? Update the sentence. Also: did the mayor sign NYC Int. 891-A?"}} [Maryland](https://skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/05/maryland-becomes-the-first-state-to-restrict-surveillance-pricing) and [New Jersey](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/07/new-jersey-enacts-first-in-the-nation) passed laws in 2026 barring grocery stores and delivery services from using shoppers' personal data to set their prices (Maryland's bars only higher prices, and exempts discounts and loyalty programs). [Connecticut passed a similar law](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/consumer-reports-statement-on-the-signing-of-connecticuts-surveillance-pricing-bill-into-law/) that covers retailers in general. Maryland's took effect October 1, 2026; the other two start in 2027.

At the federal level, the FTC [opened an investigation into Instacart's pricing tool](https://www.consumerreports.org/money/questionable-business-practices/instacart-stops-ai-pricing-experiments-a1176475852/) in December 2025. In August 2026 it [proposed a policy statement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/08/ftc-seeks-comment-enforcement-policy-statement-regarding-personalized-pricing) saying undisclosed personalized pricing may be deceptive or unfair under federal law. That's guidance, not a ban; the FTC says Congress hasn't given it power to [prohibit the practice in all circumstances](https://www.gtlaw.com/ar/insights/2026/8/ftc-seeks-public-comment-on-proposed-policy-statement-regarding-personalized-pricing). On October 28, 2026, the FTC [ordered nine companies](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-launches-personalized-pricing-compensation-inquiry), including Instacart, DoorDash, Walmart and Target, to say whether and how personal data shapes their prices and, for the ride-share and delivery companies, their workers' pay. That's a study, not an accusation. The next day [DoorDash agreed to buy Instacart for about $17 billion](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001792789/000114036126041111/ef20084547_ex99-1.htm), a deal still under review. {{due 2027-03-31 "Personalized pricing: has the FTC finalized its policy statement, released anything from the October 2026 6(b) orders, or ruled on DoorDash–Instacart? Check new state laws and California's 2027 session"}}

Not every state has gone along. Colorado's governor [vetoed a bill banning the use of personal data to set prices and wages](https://coloradosun.com/2026/06/03/polis-vetoes-three-progressive-bills/) in June 2026, saying it was too broad. California's ban [passed the state Senate 22 to 14](https://legiscan.com/CA/bill/AB2564/) on the last night of the session, then died without a final Assembly vote. The actions so far don't line up neatly by party: the FTC's 2026 moves came under a Republican chairman, the three state bans were signed by Democratic governors, and Colorado's veto came from a Democrat too.

**Supporters of the new rules** argue that hidden testing makes it impossible to comparison-shop or budget, the point Consumer Reports and Groundwork made when they released the study. Groundwork estimated the average differences it found [could have cost a family of four](https://www.consumerreports.org/media-room/press-releases/2025/12/new-report-exposes-instacarts-hidden-price-games/) that bought all its groceries on Instacart about $1,200 a year.

**Skeptics** argue that testing prices is how every store learns what customers will pay, online or on the shelf. Instacart says its tests were randomized, not aimed at particular shoppers. A Maryland retail group [said it supports barring the use of shopper data to raise prices](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/02/17/dynamic-pricing-bill-could-backfire-eliminate-targeted-consumer-discounts-merchants-warn/), but argued that dynamic pricing isn't inherently unfair and that the bill could wipe out data-driven discounts. [Grocery industry groups in New Jersey](https://njbia.org/business-coalition-seeks-to-protect-loyalty-programs-that-benefit-nj-consumers/) made a similar case: broad rules could end personalized discounts and loyalty deals. Howard Beales, a former head of the FTC's consumer protection bureau, told the agency in [a September 2026 comment](https://laweconcenter.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Beales-Personalized-Pricing-Comments-Final.pdf) that charging different customers different prices can help or hurt consumers depending on the case, and noted the FTC's own admission that little is known about how common personalized pricing is or what it does. Instacart also points to competition: it [told shareholders in 2025](https://investors.instacart.com/static-files/a3e266a1-2d27-412f-a640-ba581759b139) that stores charging in-store prices on its app grow faster than stores that mark up, and said that was pushing retailers toward matching the shelf.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 16, 2026.
