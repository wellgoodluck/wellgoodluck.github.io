## At a glance

- **Who:** grades 9–12 economics or personal finance; works as written for adult education.
- **Time:** 45 minutes (hook 5, chart 15, discussion 10, activity 15).
- **Standard:** Council for Economic Education, [National Content Standards in K–12 Economics, 3rd ed. (2025)](https://www.councilforeconed.org/wp-content/uploads/National-Content-Standards-in-K–12-Economics-3rd-Edition.pdf), Standard 15: Inflation.
- **Pages used:** the [Inflation chart](../chart.html?id=cpi), [What Inflation Is](what-is-inflation.html) and [Has It Kept Up With Prices?](/tools/kept-up.html).
- **Materials:** a projector or one device per pair, and the [student handout](lesson-inflation-handout.html), printed.
- **Students leave able to:** say what a year-over-year rate measures; place this month's number in its history; tell a price level from a rate of change; check whether a raise kept up.

The current readings on this page and the handout update themselves when new figures come out, so the lesson works any month. The activity's worked cases use fixed past months, so their answers don't change.

## Opening hook (5 minutes)

1. Ask: "Name something you bought in 2019 that costs more now. What did it cost then, and now?" Write three or four answers on the board.
2. Ask the class to guess: across everything households buy, how much more does a typical $100 of 2019 spending cost today? Take a show of hands for $105, $115, $130 and $150.
3. Reveal it: prices are up {{fig prices_since_2019}} since 2019, by the government's main measure, through {{v cpi period}}. The line under the inflation tile on the [almanac](/#i-cpi) gives the same answer in dollars.
4. Bridge: "Today we learn to read the inflation number: what it measures, whether it's high, and whether your pay kept up with it."

The board list usually overshoots. People remember the prices that jumped and forget the ones that barely moved. That gap is the point of an average, and question 6 comes back to it.

## Reading the chart together (15 minutes)

Project the [Inflation chart](../chart.html?id=cpi) and read it top to bottom. Each part answers a question students should ask of any economic number.

1. **What is it?** "Consumer prices, change from a year ago." Stress *change*: the number is how fast prices rose over the past twelve months, not how high they are. Use the analogy from [What Inflation Is](what-is-inflation.html): inflation is the car's speed, prices are the odometer. When inflation falls, the car slows; the odometer never runs backward.
2. **Is it moving?** Inflation was {{v cpi}} in {{v cpi period}}, against {{v cpi.previous}} the month before and {{v cpi.year_ago}} a year earlier. Ask: faster or slower than a year ago?
3. **Is it high?** Read the bar under the number: its middle is the median of every month since 1948, and the line under it says how this month ranks. Ask students to say it in their own words.
4. **Compared with what?** The 2015–19 average was {{v cpi.baseline}}. Have a student tick **Fed's 2% inflation target** under Reference lines and compare.
5. **The long view.** Click **All**. Students find the peak, {{v cpi.max}} in {{v cpi.max period}}, and the low, {{v cpi.min}} in {{v cpi.min period}}, when prices actually fell. Then find 2022. Ask which era today looks more like.
6. **Where does it come from?** Find the credit line inside the chart and the source link under it: the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Consumer Price Index, through the St. Louis Fed's FRED database. Students should find this line before trusting any number.

Short on time: do steps 1, 3 and 4.

## Discussion questions (10 minutes)

Pick three or four. The questions climb from reading the page to weighing a trade-off; model answers are below.

1. *Reading it.* In one sentence, what does this month's inflation number mean?
2. Inflation was {{v cpi.max}} in {{v cpi.max period}} and is {{v cpi}} in {{v cpi period}}. Are prices lower now than then? Why or why not?
3. *Placing it.* The chart ranks this month against every month since 1948. Is the rank a reason to worry, or just context? What else would you want to know?
4. Why might the site compare today with 2015–19, not just with last year?
5. *Judging it.* Who comes out ahead when prices rise faster than expected, and who falls behind? Think about a borrower with a fixed-rate loan, a saver, and a retiree on a fixed pension.
6. Your board list from the hook probably averaged more than the official figure. Why might the official index differ from what people feel?
7. The Fed aims for 2% a year, not zero. What could be the case for a little inflation rather than none?

## Activity: Has pay kept up? (15 minutes)

In pairs, using [Has It Kept Up With Prices?](/tools/kept-up.html):

1. **The worked case.** Choose **Pay**. Then: $15.00, September 2025. Later: $15.50, September 2026. The tool gives the change in pay, the change in prices and the pay that would have matched prices.
2. **Read the verdict.** The tool calls a gap of a few tenths of a percent "about the same." Is it? Have pairs work out the difference per hour, then for a full-time year of about 2,080 hours.
3. **A second case.** Choose **Rent**: $1,400 a month in September 2025, $1,470 in September 2026. Who is ahead, and who is behind?
4. **Their own number.** Choose **Something else** and enter a price or wage students care about: a first-job hourly rate, a game, a phone plan.

**Exit ticket:** "Did a raise from $15.00 to $15.50 keep up with prices? Give the number that proves it."

## Answer key

1. Prices across the basket were {{v cpi}} higher in {{v cpi period}} than a year earlier.
2. No. Prices are higher than they were then. Inflation falling means prices rise more slowly, not that they fall. (Speed, not odometer.)
3. Context. Good follow-ups: is it rising or falling, is pay keeping up (the activity), and what's driving it ([Four Prices Inside Inflation](four-prices-inside-inflation.html) breaks it down).
4. Last year can be unusual too. 2015–19 is a recent stretch of steady inflation, so it shows what "normal" looked like before the pandemic.
5. Unexpected inflation moves buying power around. Fixed-rate borrowers tend to gain, because they repay in cheaper dollars. Savers in low-interest accounts tend to lose. A retiree depends on the pension: Social Security adjusts for inflation each year, and many private pensions don't.
6. People notice prices they pay often, like gas and groceries, and remember the jumps. The index weights everything a household buys, including things that barely moved. Critics have argued both ways: some say the index understates what families feel, and a 1996 commission for the Senate Finance Committee found it overstated increases in the cost of living, by about 1.1 points a year ([the Boskin report](https://www.ssa.gov/history/reports/boskinrpt.html)). Let students weigh both.
7. The case usually made: a little inflation gives the Fed room to cut interest rates in a downturn, makes it easier for pay to adjust when employers rarely cut wages, and keeps a distance from falling prices. Some economists argue for a higher or lower target, or a different kind of target. The Fed explains its choice in its [Statement on Longer-Run Goals and Monetary Policy Strategy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/files/FOMC_LongerRunGoals.pdf).

Activity: the tool shows pay up 3.3% and prices up 3.7% for September 2025 to September 2026, and $15.56 to match. That's 6 cents an hour short, about $125 over a full-time year. Rent up 5.0% beat prices: the renter is behind and the landlord ahead.

**Misconceptions to listen for:** "inflation is going down, so prices are going down" (question 2); "high prices are inflation" (inflation is the change; a price can be high and steady); "inflation hurts everyone equally" (question 5).
