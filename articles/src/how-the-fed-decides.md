## The short version

The Federal Reserve's interest-rate decisions are made by the Federal Open Market Committee, which meets eight times a year. Nineteen officials sit at the table: the seven governors in Washington and the presidents of the twelve regional Federal Reserve Banks. Twelve of them vote: the seven governors, the president of the New York Fed, and four other bank presidents who take turns, one year at a time. The chair runs the meeting and proposes the decision, and the committee almost always goes along, so a "no" vote, called a dissent, is unusual enough to be news. Four times a year the committee also publishes a chart of each official's guess about where rates are headed, the dot plot, which is a set of anonymous opinions, not a promise. In 2026 nearly every part of this was in motion: a new chair, Kevin Warsh, who cut the committee's statements to a few sentences and stopped submitting his own dot; the first rate increase since 2023; dissents in both directions; and a Supreme Court ruling, on an emergency appeal in a case brought by a governor the president tried to fire, that the law's for-cause protection is constitutional and requires notice and a chance to respond before removal. This page reflects the committee as of mid-November 2026, before its December 8–9 meeting.

## The analogy

Think of a city council with a strong chair. The staff prepares a thick packet before every meeting. Everyone at the table gets to speak, including some members who can't vote this year. The chair, who has spent the week before talking to each member one on one, comes in with a motion that can pass. Most votes are lopsided because the deal was made before the meeting started, so the interesting part is the members who still vote no, and what they say about why. The minutes come out a few weeks later, and the full transcript much later still.

## Who sits at the table

**Seven governors.** The president nominates them and the Senate confirms them. Each full term is [14 years](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/242), staggered so that one term ends every two years; a governor who has served a full term can't be reappointed, though one who fills the rest of someone else's term can be. A governor can be removed only "for cause." The chair and vice chairs are chosen from among the governors for four-year terms in those roles. In 2026 the governors are [Kevin Warsh (chair), Philip Jefferson, Michelle Bowman, Michael Barr, Lisa Cook, Christopher Waller and Jerome Powell](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm). Warsh became chair on [May 22, 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/bios/board/boardmembership.htm), taking the board seat that Stephen Miran had held. Powell, whose term as chair ended, [stayed on as a governor](https://foxbusiness.com/economy/powell-says-hell-stay-fed-board-after-chairmanship-ends-wont-shadow-fed-chair), the first former chair to stay on the board for more than a few weeks since Marriner Eccles, who stepped down as chair in 1948 and [remained a governor until 1951](https://www.federalreserve.gov/aboutthefed/bios/board/boardmembership.htm). His governor's term runs to January 2028.

**Twelve bank presidents.** Each regional Federal Reserve Bank's president is chosen by that bank's board of directors, with the approval of the Board of Governors in Washington, for a five-year term. Since 2010, the three directors who represent the member banks themselves (Class A) no longer take part; the president is chosen by the six directors who represent the public, three elected by member banks and three appointed by the Board of Governors. All twelve presidents attend every meeting and speak. Only five vote in a given year:

- The president of the New York Fed, John Williams, always votes. He is the committee's vice chair.
- The other eleven take turns in four seats. In 2026 the voters are [the presidents of Cleveland (Beth Hammack), Philadelphia (Anna Paulson), Dallas (Lorie Logan) and Minneapolis (Neel Kashkari)](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm). In 2027 the seats go to Chicago, Richmond, Atlanta and San Francisco, and in 2028 to Cleveland, Boston, St. Louis and Kansas City.

So in any year, 12 of the 19 people at the table vote, and whenever the Board is full, the majority of the votes belong to governors appointed by a president and confirmed by the Senate.

## How a meeting runs

Most regular meetings run over two days. It opens with staff briefings on the economy, financial markets and the forecast, based on a briefing book circulated beforehand. Then comes the "go-round": each of the 19 participants, voter or not, gives a view of the economy and then of what the committee should do. The chair typically sums up and puts forward a proposal, usually already worked out with the members most likely to object. The voters then vote, in public record, by name.

At 2 p.m. Eastern on the last day, the committee releases a short statement and the vote. At 2:30 the chair holds a press conference. The [minutes](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm) come out three weeks later, without names attached to most views, and the full transcript is released after five years. The committee's last meeting of 2026 is December 8–9, and it [holds eight regularly scheduled meetings a year](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomc.htm). For 2027, Warsh has said one of the eight will be given to [long-range strategy, with no rate vote and no statement](https://www.icba.org/w/warsh-says-fed-will-have-fewer-rate-meetings-in-2027-2028), and two in 2028.

## What a dissent means

Because the chair builds the decision before the vote, most decisions are unanimous or close to it, and dissents tend to come from bank presidents, who have their own staffs and less day-to-day contact with the chair. A governor voting no is much rarer. Before July 2025, [two governors had not dissented at the same meeting since December 1993](https://1027wbow.com/?p=966281). The last two years broke that pattern in both directions:

- **July 2025:** governors Christopher Waller and Michelle Bowman voted for a cut when the committee held, [the first double dissent by governors since 1993](https://1027wbow.com/?p=966281).
- **September 2025 to April 2026:** Stephen Miran, appointed to fill a vacancy, [dissented at every meeting he attended](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/kevin-warsh-senate-confirms-fed-governor/), in favor of faster cuts.
- **July 2026:** the committee held at 3.50–3.75% on a [9–3 vote](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260729a.htm); three presidents, Hammack, Kashkari and Logan, wanted an increase.
- **September 16, 2026:** the committee [raised the range to 3.75–4.00% on a 12–0 vote](https://federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20260916a.htm), the first increase since July 2023.
- **October 28, 2026:** it held at 3.75–4.00% on a [10–2 vote](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/monetary20261028a.htm). Governor Cook and Hammack both preferred another quarter-point increase. It was [Cook's first dissent](https://fortune.com/2026/10/28/fed-rates-steady-kevin-warsh-president-governor-laura-ullrich/) since she joined the board in 2022.

A dissent doesn't change the decision. What it changes is what the public knows: that the committee isn't settled, which way the disagreement runs, and who might move the next vote. That's why markets read the dissent line of a statement before they read anything else.

## What the dot plot is, and isn't

Four times a year, in March, June, September and December, the committee publishes a [Summary of Economic Projections](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomccalendars.htm). Each participant writes down where they expect growth, unemployment and inflation to be at the end of each of the next few years, and the interest rate they think will be appropriate. The rate guesses are drawn as dots on a chart, one per person, with no names. The middle dot, the median, is the number headlines report.

The dots are opinions, written in pencil. Each one assumes that person's own view of what should happen, which may not be what the committee does. The median is not a vote and not a forecast by the committee as a whole. In September 2026, for example, the [median dot for the end of 2026 was 4.1%](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/fomcprojtabl20260916.htm), implying one more quarter-point increase after the September move; the committee then held in October. The participants' median estimate of the rate that neither speeds up nor slows down the economy over the long run, which this site draws on its [Fed funds chart](../chart.html?id=fed_funds&ov=fomc_longrun_ffr) as a reference line, was 3.2%.

**What changed in 2026.** At his first meeting as chair, in June, Warsh [did not submit a dot](https://wolfstreet.com/2026/06/17/regime-change-at-the-fed-fed-chair-warsh-makes-the-first-moves/), so the chart had 18 instead of 19. He said that, for him, the dots are [not helpful in the conduct of policy](https://www.federalreserve.gov/mediacenter/files/FOMCpresconf20260617.pdf), that skipping his own was consistent with his long-held views on the projections, and that he had encouraged the others to keep submitting theirs. The committee also [cut its statement from 341 words to about 130](https://wtop.com/business-finance/2026/06/warshs-gamble-a-quieter-federal-reserve-could-mean-volatile-markets-higher-rates/) and dropped the sentences that hinted at the next move, what the Fed calls forward guidance. Warsh set up five internal task forces, one of them on communications, which is reviewing the projections, the press conferences and other practices. As of mid-November 2026 the dot plot was still being published, and the committee had not announced any change to it.

## What independence protects

The Fed is called independent, but it isn't independent of the law. Congress created it, gave it its goals (stable prices and maximum employment), can change either by passing a law, and hears the chair testify twice a year. What the law does protect is narrower and specific:

- **Terms.** Governors' 14-year terms outlast any president's, and a president can remove a governor only for cause.
- **Money.** The Fed pays its own expenses from the interest on the securities it holds, so it doesn't depend on Congress's yearly budget. Its monetary-policy decisions are also exempt from audit by the Government Accountability Office.
- **The vote.** The president has no vote and no veto. The committee's decision takes effect the next day.

What the law doesn't protect: a president can criticize the Fed in public, as presidents of both parties have; can appoint governors and the chair as seats open; and can try to remove a governor for cause.

In August 2025, President Trump said he was firing Governor Cook over allegations about mortgage applications she signed before joining the Fed; she denied wrongdoing and sued. [No president had tried to remove a Fed governor before](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/trump-establishes-committee-to-investigate-federal-reserves-lisa-cook-in-latest-effort-to-fire-her). On June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court, [ruling 5–4 on the administration's emergency request](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/LSB11449.html), held that the law's for-cause protection is constitutional and that a governor is owed notice and an opportunity to respond before removal. It left Cook in office while her case continues. The same day, in a separate case, the Court [let the president fire a member of the Federal Trade Commission](https://www.democracydocket.com/news-alerts/shattering-precedent-supreme-court-expands-trumps-dismissal-powers/), overruling a 1935 precedent, but treated the Fed as a different case because of the country's long tradition of independent central banking. In October, the White House [set up a "committee of inquiry"](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/trump-establishes-committee-to-investigate-federal-reserves-lisa-cook-in-latest-effort-to-fire-her) to decide whether there was cause to remove her. Her lawyers [called it biased](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/amp/politics/lawyers-for-federal-reserves-lisa-cook-say-white-house-hearing-set-up-by-trump-is-biased) and said she couldn't appear before the December meeting. A White House hearing first set for November 5 was [moved to December 14](https://justthenews.com/government/white-house/white-house-schedules-hearing-allegations-against-feds-lisa-cook-december). As of mid-November the matter was unresolved.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

The committee's decision sets a target range for one rate, the overnight rate banks charge each other. From there it reaches savings accounts within days, credit cards and home equity lines within a statement or two, and jobs and prices over a year or more. Mortgage rates follow the 10-year Treasury yield, which often moves on what bond buyers expect the committee to do before it does it. That's why how the committee talks matters almost as much as what it decides: a hint about the next move moves mortgage rates today. [What the Fed funds rate is](what-is-the-fed-funds-rate.html) follows each of those channels in turn.

## Where the analogy breaks

Nobody on this council was elected, and voters can't remove any of them. Most city councils deliberate in public; the Fed's committee deliberates in private and releases the record later. A council member's vote can be traded for a road project; the committee decides one number for the whole country, and a "deal" in the Fed sense is a change of wording, not a favor. And a council's decision is about the city in front of it, while the committee is deciding what the economy needs a year from now, using data that will be revised in the meantime.

## What people argue about

**How much independence is right.** One side says central banks that answer to politicians keep rates too low before elections and get the inflation that follows, points to research such as [Alesina and Summers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:mcb:jmoncb:v:25:y:1993:i:2:p:151-62) (1993), which found lower and more stable inflation in rich countries with more independent central banks, with no measurable effect on growth, and says the Court's June ruling confirmed a protection Congress meant to give. The other side says an unelected committee shouldn't have this much say over jobs and borrowing costs, that independence has grown into a shield against accountability for misjudgments such as the late response to inflation in 2021–22, and that a president who is blamed for the economy should have more say over who runs the Fed. Those who backed the attempt to remove Cook say it was about her conduct, not her votes; her lawyers and other critics say the timing and the committee's makeup show otherwise.

**Whether the Fed should say what it plans to do.** The case for forward guidance and the dots, made by the chairs who built them, Ben Bernanke and Janet Yellen among them, is that markets do part of the Fed's work when they understand where it's headed, so clear signals make policy work faster and with fewer surprises. Warsh's case, which he made for years before becoming chair, is that the signals box the committee in, that markets treat a pencilled guess as a promise, and that a Fed that talks less can change course when the data change. Some critics of the change say less information shifts power toward the chair, whose words become the main clue, and away from the committee's recorded range of views.

**What dissents say about the committee.** Some former officials and economists, among them [Jason Furman](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1950628024337985683.html), say open dissents show a healthy committee arguing in public rather than one rubber-stamping the chair. Others, among them [Peter Conti-Brown](https://www.klcc.org/2025-07-31/two-federal-reserve-governors-dissent-on-holding-interest-rates), a historian of the Fed, have raised the possibility that dissents by members who share a president's view can reflect pressure from outside the room, and that the committee's credibility depends on decisions that read as the committee's own.

The site shows the votes, the chairs and the dates; it doesn't referee.
