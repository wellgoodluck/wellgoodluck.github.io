## Short version

Zelle, Venmo, Cash App and similar apps are built for paying people you know. They move money almost instantly, and once it's sent, it's usually gone. Federal law protects you when someone takes money from your account without your permission, but not, in most cases, when a scammer talks you into sending it yourself. Scammers know this, so they work hard to get you to hit send: a fake buyer, a fake seller, a fake landlord, or a fake call from your own bank.

## The trap in one sentence

An app payment works like handing over cash: if you sent it, even because you were tricked, the law usually treats it as your decision.

## How it works

**Authorized vs. unauthorized.** A federal law from 1978, the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, and the rule that carries it out, Regulation E, cover electronic transfers, including payment apps. If someone gets into your account and sends money without you, that's an unauthorized transfer. Tell your bank or app within two business days of finding out your login, phone or card was stolen, and the most you can lose is $50. Wait longer and it can be up to $500. Wait more than 60 days after the statement that shows the theft, and you could lose everything taken after that. But if you sent the money yourself, even to a scammer, it's generally treated as authorized, and the law doesn't require anyone to give it back. There's one important exception: if a scammer tricks you into handing over your password or a one-time code and then sends the money himself, that still counts as unauthorized.

**Cards are different.** If you pay with a credit card and the item never arrives, federal law lets you dispute the charge, in writing within 60 days of the statement it appears on. Payment apps between people have no such rule. Some apps offer their own purchase protection, but usually only when you pay a business or mark a payment as being for goods and services, and the seller pays a fee.

**The common scripts:**

- **The fake seller.** Concert tickets, a used phone or a puppy, at a good price, from someone who wants payment by app "as friends and family." Nothing arrives.
- **The fake buyer.** Someone buying your item "sends" a payment and a fake email saying it's on hold until you pay a fee or "upgrade to a business account."
- **"I sent it by mistake."** A stranger sends you money, then asks you to send it back. The original payment came from a stolen account and gets reversed later; your payment back doesn't.
- **The fake bank.** A text or call that appears to come from your bank's fraud department says your account is under attack and tells you to move your money "to keep it safe" by sending it to yourself through Zelle. The account belongs to the scammer.
- **The fake landlord.** A rental listing for a place that isn't really for rent, with a deposit due by app to hold it. Apps' purchase protection doesn't cover rent or deposits. People 18 to 29 were three times as likely as other adults to report losing money to rental scams in the FTC's figures for mid-2024 to mid-2025.

## How to avoid it

**Use the app only with people you know.** For anything you buy from a stranger, pay by credit card or through the app's goods-and-services option, and accept the small fee.

**Your bank will never ask you to move money to protect it.** Hang up and call the number on the back of your card. Banks don't send you to Zelle, gift cards or crypto to "secure" your account.

**Don't send money back to a stranger.** If someone says they paid you by mistake, contact the app and let it reverse the payment.

**See it before you pay for it.** For rentals, tour the place in person, or have someone you trust do it, before paying anything. For tickets, buy through the venue or a resale site that guarantees them.

**Act fast if it happens.** Contact your bank and the app right away, file a report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and if someone else sent the money, even using a code you were tricked into sharing, say so: that's what triggers your rights under Regulation E. You can also file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau.

For purchases from strangers, a credit card's dispute rights are stronger than a debit card's or an app's; [The Charge You Never Noticed](reading-your-statements.html) explains why.

## The bigger picture

People reported losing $15.9 billion to fraud in 2025, according to the FTC, a record and up from $12.5 billion in 2024; bank transfers and payments accounted for the most money lost of any payment method. People 29 and under reported losing nearly $500 million, according to FTC figures cited by the Federal Reserve. Real losses are higher, because most fraud is never reported.

**Who should cover scams people are tricked into?** In December 2024 the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sued the company that runs Zelle and three of the banks that own it, saying they failed to protect customers; the bureau dropped the case in March 2025. New York's attorney general filed her own suit against Zelle's operator in August 2025, and in July 2026 a state judge let nearly all of it go forward. In 2024, congressional Democrats introduced the Protecting Consumers from Payment Scams Act. It would have treated many scams people are tricked into like unauthorized transfers, with the sender's and receiver's banks splitting the cost, much as the United Kingdom has required since October 2024. It didn't pass. In July 2026, Republicans on the House Financial Services Committee released a report, "Fighting Back," calling for better fraud information-sharing, a single federal task force and fewer rules that slow banks down, but not for making banks repay scams customers authorize.

**Supporters of making banks pay** argue that banks and app companies design the systems, profit from instant payments and are best placed to spot and stop fraud, that customers can't reasonably detect sophisticated scams, and that the UK's rules pushed banks to block more fraud up front.

**Opponents** argue that making banks liable for payments customers choose to make would invite false claims, raise costs for every customer, make banks slower to send payments and quicker to close accounts, and reduce people's own caution. Bank groups say better information sharing and the power to delay suspicious payments would prevent more fraud than reimbursement would. They also say scams usually start on social media, phone calls and texts, so tech and phone companies and law enforcement should share the job, not just banks.
