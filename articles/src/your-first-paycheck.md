## Short version

The first paycheck from a job is almost always smaller than people expect. The number you were promised, your pay rate times your hours, is your gross pay. Before it reaches you, your employer takes out Social Security and Medicare taxes, an estimate of your federal income tax, usually state income tax, and anything you signed up for, such as health insurance or a retirement plan. What's left is your take-home pay, or net pay. Income tax withholding is only an advance payment: in the spring you file a tax return, and if too much came out, you get it back.

## The idea in one sentence

A paycheck is your gross pay minus a set of payments made on your behalf, some fixed by law and some set by the forms you fill out when you're hired.

## How it works

**Social Security and Medicare.** Almost every job takes out 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare, together often labeled FICA on a pay stub. Your employer pays the same amounts again on top. Social Security tax stops once your pay for the year passes a cap: [$184,500 in 2026, rising to $192,300 in 2027](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/news/2026/oct/social-security-wage-base-and-cola-announced-for-2027/). Medicare has no cap. These taxes come out from the first dollar, at any age (with a few exceptions, such as students working for their own college), and you don't get them back at tax time. They pay for today's retirees and earn you credits toward your own benefits later.

**Federal income tax withholding.** This is your employer's estimate of the income tax you'll owe for the year, spread across your paychecks. The estimate comes from two things: IRS tables, which already build in the [standard deduction ($16,100 for a single filer in 2026)](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill), and the Form W-4 you fill out when you start.

**The W-4.** The current form has five steps. Only the first (your name and filing status) and the last (your signature) are required. The middle steps are for people with a second job or a working spouse, children or other dependents, other income, deductions, or extra withholding they want taken out. The [2026 version](https://blogs.payroll.org/pay-news-now/irs-releases-2026-forms-w-4-w-4p-with-changes-due-to-obbba) grew to five pages, with new worksheet lines for the federal deductions on tips and overtime. If you don't turn in a W-4, your employer withholds as if you were single with no adjustments.

**Claiming exempt.** If you owed no federal income tax last year and expect to owe none this year, you can claim exemption from withholding on the W-4, and no federal income tax comes out (the 2026 form has a checkbox for it). The exemption [lasts one year](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p505): to keep it, you turn in a new W-4 by February 15. It never stops Social Security and Medicare.

**Tips and overtime.** For [2025 through 2028](https://irs.gov/newsroom/one-big-beautiful-bill-provisions-individuals-and-workers), workers can deduct up to $25,000 of qualified tips and up to $12,500 of overtime premium pay ($25,000 on a joint return) from income subject to federal income tax, with both deductions shrinking once income passes $150,000 ($300,000 on a joint return). They don't reduce Social Security or Medicare taxes, and each state decides whether to follow them for its own income tax. Unless you adjust your W-4, your paychecks don't change; the benefit shows up when you file.

**State and local taxes.** [Nine states don't tax wages](https://blog.taxact.com/states-with-no-income-tax/amp/): Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington and Wyoming. (Washington has passed [a tax on income over $1 million](https://www.rsmus.com/insights/tax-alerts/2026/washington-state-enacts-millionaires-tax) that starts in 2028.) The rest do, and some cities and counties add their own. A few states also take out small amounts for disability or paid family leave programs.

**What you choose.** Health, dental and vision premiums, a traditional 401(k), and health savings or flexible spending accounts usually come out before income tax is figured, which lowers your income tax withholding, and most of them lower Social Security and Medicare too. A traditional 401(k) is the exception: it still counts for Social Security and Medicare. A Roth 401(k) comes out after tax.

**The pay stub.** It lists gross pay, each deduction, net pay, and year-to-date totals. [Federal law doesn't require employers to give you one](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/flsa/faq), but many states do, on paper or online.

## A worked example

You earn $15 an hour, work 30 hours a week, and are paid every two weeks. You're single, turned in a W-4 with no adjustments, and live in a state with no income tax on wages.

<table><thead><tr><th>Line on the stub</th><th>This paycheck</th><th>If you also put 3% in a 401(k)</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Gross pay (60 hours × $15)</td><td>$900.00</td><td>$900.00</td></tr><tr><td>401(k), before tax</td><td>none</td><td>−$27.00</td></tr><tr><td>Social Security (6.2%)</td><td>−$55.80</td><td>−$55.80</td></tr><tr><td>Medicare (1.45%)</td><td>−$13.05</td><td>−$13.05</td></tr><tr><td>Federal income tax withheld</td><td>−$28.08</td><td>−$25.38</td></tr><tr><td><strong>Take-home pay</strong></td><td><strong>$803.07</strong></td><td><strong>$778.77</strong></td></tr></tbody></table>

Putting $27 into the 401(k) costs only $24.30 of take-home pay, because less income tax comes out.

**The summer job.** Now say the same job lasts only 10 weeks, five paychecks. About $140 of federal income tax is withheld, because the tables assume you'll earn at this rate all year. But $4,500 for the year is less than the standard deduction, so you owe no federal income tax at all. Filing a return gets the $140 back. The $344 of Social Security and Medicare stays paid.

## What to do with it

**Read the first stub line by line.** Check the hours, the pay rate, and that your filing status is right. Mistakes are easiest to fix on the first one.

**Revisit the W-4 when life changes:** a second job, marriage, a child, or a big raise. The IRS's free Tax Withholding Estimator walks through it.

**Decide what you want in April.** A big refund means you lent the government money all year at no interest; a bill means too little came out, and a large one (generally $1,000 or more) can bring an underpayment penalty. Neither is wrong, but the bill shouldn't be a surprise.

**File even if you don't have to,** when income tax was withheld and you owed none. Nobody sends it back automatically.

**Keep your last stub of the year** and compare it with the W-2 your employer sends by the end of January.

**Use the split.** Many employers can send part of each paycheck straight to a savings account, which makes [a small cushion](emergency-cushion.html) automatic. If some of your income comes from delivery, rideshare or freelance apps, [nothing is withheld from it](gig-work-taxes.html).
