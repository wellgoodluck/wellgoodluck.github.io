## In one sentence

A homeowner's premium is your share of what your insurer expects to pay out to rebuild houses like yours in places like yours, plus what it costs the insurer to run, to buy its own insurance, and to earn a profit.

## The analogy

A neighborhood kitty. Every house puts money in each year, and the houses that burn or flood are rebuilt from it. The yearly contribution has to rise if more houses are damaged, if rebuilding costs more, or if the kitty's own backstop gets dearer. And a house on the riverbank is asked for more than a house on the hill.

Figures are as of November 2026.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

The average borrower with a single-family mortgage paid $209 a month for property insurance in the second quarter of 2026, a record, and nearly 80% more than at the start of 2020. That was 9.6% of the average monthly payment of principal, interest, taxes and insurance ([ICE](https://ir.theice.com/press/news-details/2026/ICE-Mortgage-Monitor-Property-Insurance-Costs-Rise-8-7-Annually-but-Rate-of-Growth-Is-Sharply-Slowing/default.aspx)). Measured against principal, interest and insurance alone, economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas put the share at 14% in 2025, up from 10% in 2013 ([Dallas Fed](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0324)).

Many borrowers pay it through the lender, in the monthly payment. So a "fixed" payment rises when the premium does.

It depends heavily on where the house is. Insurance was 4.3% of the average payment in San Jose and 24.3% in New Orleans (same ICE report). In the Treasury's study of 2018 to 2022, homeowners in the fifth of ZIP codes with the highest expected losses from storms, fires and floods paid an average of $2,321 a year, 82% more than those in the lowest-risk fifth ([Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/311/Analyses_of_US_Homeowners_Insurance_Markets_2018-2022_Climate-Related_Risks_and_Other_Factors_0.pdf)).

The Dallas Fed economists found that it changes what households do. A $1,000 rise in the yearly premium raised the chance that a borrower moved by about half a percentage point, and they estimate that premium increases pushed roughly 31,000 mortgages into delinquency in 2022 ([Dallas Fed](https://www.dallasfed.org/research/economics/2026/0324)).

## What moves it

**Claims.** More damage means more paid out. The Treasury study covers five years in which major disaster declarations for weather events ran at nearly double their average from 1960 to 2010. In 2024 the country had 27 weather disasters that each did more than $1 billion in damage, $182.7 billion in all ([KMPH](https://kmph.com/news/nation-world/a-really-big-deal-new-report-offers-most-detailed-look-yet-at-home-insurance-struggles-growing-problems-of-cost-and-accessibility-for-homeowners-insurance-natural-disasters-wildfires-hurricane-risks-department-of-the-treasurys-federal-insurance-office)).

**What rebuilding costs.** A premium insures the cost of rebuilding, not the sale price. The Treasury study puts the rise in replacement costs at 45% from 2020 to 2023, and that reaches houses nowhere near a coast: the Consumer Federation of America found premiums up in 95% of ZIP codes from 2021 to 2024 ([CFA](https://consumerfed.org/media/legacy/post_30876/OverburdenedReport.pdf)).

**The insurers' own insurance.** Insurers buy reinsurance to cover a catastrophic year, and its price has risen too ([United Policyholders](https://uphelp.org/42-of-homeowners-say-insurance-costs-have-gone-up-a-lot-survey-finds-heres-why/?print=pdf)).

**How much is insured.** As rebuilding costs rise, policies are written for larger amounts. ICE found that about two-thirds of the past year's rise in borrowers' insurance payments came from higher coverage amounts; the price per $1,000 of coverage rose about 3% (same ICE report).

**The state.** Each state regulates rates, some far more tightly than others ([Insurance.com](https://www.insurance.com/state-of-home-insurance/)).

## How to read the numbers

**"Up 8.7%" and "up 24%" can both be right.** ICE found the average borrower's insurance payment 8.7% higher in the year to June 2026, down from a peak of 15.1% at the end of 2024. The Consumer Federation of America measured 2021 to 2024 and found the typical premium up 24%, or $648 a year ([CFA](https://consumerfed.org/media/legacy/post_30876/OverburdenedReport.pdf)). The Treasury measured 2018 to 2022 and found premiums rising 8.7% faster than inflation. Different years, different samples.

**The increases are slowing, not reversing.** One insurance marketplace reported that renewals rose an average of 10.6% in the first half of 2026, against 19.4% in 2025 and 28% in 2024, and that 11.7% of homeowners saw a decrease ([Matic](https://matic.com/blog/2026-home-insurance-report/)). A slower rise still leaves the bill well above where it was.

**Price is only half of it.** In the Treasury data, insurers declined to renew policies about 80% more often in the highest-risk ZIP codes than in the lowest. When private insurers pull back, owners fall to state-created plans of last resort. California's FAIR Plan listed $773 billion of exposure in September 2026, against $458 billion two years earlier ([California FAIR Plan](https://www.cfpnet.com/key-statistics-data/)).

## Where the analogy breaks

A kitty has no owner. An insurer does, and it invests the money it holds and keeps what it doesn't pay out, which is one of the arguments below. And neighbors can't leave the kitty. Insurers can stop selling in a state, and owners without a mortgage can go without.

## What people argue about

**Risk or profit.** One side: consumer groups say the increases have outrun the losses. Public Citizen points to state regulators' figures showing property and casualty insurers' underwriting income at $68.7 billion in 2025, the most in over two decades, and the Consumer Federation of America wants regulators to look harder at rate requests ([Public Citizen](https://www.citizen.org/wp-content/uploads/Insurer-Scores-Record-Profits.pdf); [NAIC](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/2025-annual-property-and-casualty-and-title-insurance-industries-analysis-report.pdf); [CFA](https://consumerfed.org/homeowners-deserve-transparency-on-rising-insurance-costs/)). The other side: that total covers auto and commercial insurance as well as homes. On homes alone, insurers made an underwriting gain of $16.5 billion in 2025, their first since 2019, after losses of more than $35 billion over the five years before ([Independent Agent magazine, on AM Best's figures](https://www.iamagazine.com/news/homeowners-insurance-rebounds-with-16-5-billion-underwriting-profit/)). The National Association of Mutual Insurance Companies, a trade group, puts the average return on net worth in homeowner's insurance at 2.9% from 1990 through 2024, against 7.7% for property and casualty insurance as a whole, and the Insurance Information Institute says a year's profit has to be weighed against several years of higher rebuilding costs and severe weather, and against the capital insurers must hold for catastrophes still to come ([Insurance Journal](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/10/06/888051.htm)). In the Treasury's data, insurers paid out about 65 cents of each premium dollar in the highest-risk fifth of ZIP codes, against 55 cents in the lowest-risk fifth ([Treasury](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/311/Analyses_of_US_Homeowners_Insurance_Markets_2018-2022_Climate-Related_Risks_and_Other_Factors_0.pdf)).

**Tighter rate rules or looser.** One side: the Consumer Federation calls for stronger oversight by state regulators when companies ask to raise premiums ([CFA](https://consumerfed.org/homeowners-deserve-transparency-on-rising-insurance-costs/)). The other: Marc Ragin, who studies insurance at the University of Georgia, says prices need to catch up to the risk, or the risk has to come down, before the market settles. Seven of the 12 largest home insurers limited their coverage in California, Bloomberg reported, partly citing wildfire risk ([KMPH](https://kmph.com/news/nation-world/a-really-big-deal-new-report-offers-most-detailed-look-yet-at-home-insurance-struggles-growing-problems-of-cost-and-accessibility-for-homeowners-insurance-natural-disasters-wildfires-hurricane-risks-department-of-the-treasurys-federal-insurance-office)).

**Who should pay to lower the risk.** Stronger roofs, cleared brush and raised foundations cut losses. Ragin argues that mitigation is where the effort should go and that insurers need to get better at pricing it into policies (same KMPH report). The Consumer Federation asks for grants to help owners fortify their roofs ([CFA](https://consumerfed.org/homeowners-deserve-transparency-on-rising-insurance-costs/)).
