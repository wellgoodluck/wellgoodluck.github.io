## The short version

In 1920 more than nine in ten American daily newspapers were independently owned; by 2000, fewer than one in four. Broadcasting began as a licensed use of public airwaves, under rules that capped how many stations one company could own and, from 1949 to 1987, required broadcasters to cover controversial issues and air opposing views. Those rules loosened step by step, in 1984, 1987 and 1996, and in 1985 and 1986 two of the three broadcast networks were sold and the third came under new control. Then the money changed. Advertising, which by the 1980s paid for about four-fifths of a newspaper, fell from $48.7 billion in 2000 to under $10 billion by 2022, and newspaper newsrooms lost more than half their staff. A study of the 1980s and 1990s found the news itself softer, with more human-interest and sensational stories and fewer about public policy. Whether new owners caused that, or changes in technology and audiences pushed every owner the same way, is the argument this page leaves open.

## The analogy

A shopping mall with a bookstore in it. The bookstore draws people and gives the mall its character, but it doesn't pay much rent. The department store and the rows of small shops do, and their rent keeps the lights on in the corridor where the bookstore sits. For decades nobody had to ask whether the bookstore paid for itself. Then the department store and most of the small shops moved online, and every owner of the mall, old family or new investor, had to decide what to do with a bookstore the rent no longer covered.

In a newspaper, the department store was display advertising, the small shops were classified ads, and the bookstore was the newsroom.

## 1919–1940s: keeping the airwaves American, and licensing them

American broadcasting started with a national-security worry. After World War I, General Electric was about to sell exclusive rights to its long-distance radio transmitter to British Marconi. Navy officials objected, GE bought out the British company's American subsidiary instead, and the Radio Corporation of America was chartered in October 1919, by its own account "as a result of suggestions by officials of the United States Navy." Its charter barred foreigners from its board and officer ranks and from holding more than a fifth of its stock ([RCA, 1944](https://www.worldradiohistory.com/ARCHIVE-RCA/RCA-What-it-is/RCA-What-It-Is-What-It-Does-1944.pdf); [FCC, Report on Chain Broadcasting, 1941](https://earlyradiohistory.us/1941cb02.htm)).

That fifth became law. The Radio Act of 1927 created the Federal Radio Commission and told it to license stations as "public interest, convenience, or necessity" required, and it refused licenses to companies with foreign officers or directors or with more than a fifth of their stock voted by foreigners ([Radio Act of 1927](https://www.earlyradiohistory.us/1927act.htm)). The Communications Act of 1934 replaced the commission with the FCC, kept the one-fifth limit, and added a second, looser one: the FCC could refuse a license to a station whose parent company was more than a quarter foreign-owned, if it found refusing served the public interest. Both limits survive, renumbered, as Section 310(b) ([Noe v. FCC, 1958, quoting the 1934 text](https://m.openjurist.org/260/f2d/739); [47 U.S.C. 310](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/310)).

The bargain was clear from the start. The airwaves belonged to the public; a station got exclusive use of a frequency in return for serving the public interest, and Washington decided what that meant. Newspapers, which used no public resource, got no such rules. In 1920, 92.5% of American dailies were independently owned rather than part of a chain ([FCC, Information Needs of Communities, 2011, citing Noam](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121017022339/http://www.fcc.gov/osp/inc-report/INoC-1-Newspapers.pdf)). Chains were growing: Frank Gannett bought his first paper in 1906 and formed Gannett Co. with six of them in 1923 ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/books/politics-and-business-magazines/gannett-co-inc)).

## 1949–1970s: the public-interest bargain, and news that paid

In 1949 the FCC told broadcasters that serving the public interest meant giving reasonable time to controversial public issues and to opposing views on them, the Fairness Doctrine ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R40009.html)). The Supreme Court upheld it in 1969, reasoning that because frequencies are scarce, the public's right to hear varied views outweighs a broadcaster's right to air only its own ([Red Lion Broadcasting v. FCC](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/395/367)). Ownership was capped too: by 1954 one company could own at most seven TV stations nationwide ([FCC](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-420003A1.pdf)).

Newspapers were consolidating meanwhile. The share of dailies independently owned fell from 83% in 1940 to about 68% in 1960 ([FCC, citing Noam](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121017022339/http://www.fcc.gov/osp/inc-report/INoC-1-Newspapers.pdf)).

The idea that news could make real money arrived in this era too. CBS's *60 Minutes* premiered in September 1968, entered the Nielsen top 10 in 1977 and reached No. 1 in 1979 ([EBSCO](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/60-minutes-becomes-first-televised-newsmagazine)). Its creator, Don Hewitt, liked to say the show had "ruined television" by proving you could make money in news, as his successor recalled at his death ([NPR, 2009](https://www.npr.org/2009/08/20/112051169/tv-news-pioneer-don-hewitt-dies-at-86)). Local TV news was changing as well. The first TV news consultants, Frank N. Magid Associates (1957) and McHugh & Hoffman (1962), advised stations on what audiences watched, and the formats they spread, "Eyewitness News" and "Action News," with friendly banter between anchors, became the template for local newscasts ([University of Michigan, McHugh & Hoffman records](https://findingaids.lib.umich.edu/catalog/umich-bhl-07165); [Next TV](https://www.nexttv.com/news/media-consultant-frank-magid-dies-42158); [NewscastStudio](https://www.newscaststudio.com/2022/10/03/al-primo-death-eyewitness-news/)).

## The 1980s: the networks change hands, and the rules loosen

Three things happened within a few years.

**The cap went up.** In 1984 the FCC raised the national limit to 12 TV stations, and early in 1985 it added a cap on the share of the country's households one owner's stations could reach, 25% ([FCC, Commissioner Gomez's March 2026 statement, which recounts the history](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-420003A1.pdf)).

**The networks changed owners.** Capital Cities agreed to buy ABC for more than $3.5 billion in March 1985, a deal Warren Buffett helped finance ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1985/3/19/smaller-company-signs-deal-to-buy/)). General Electric bought RCA, NBC's parent, for $6.28 billion ([Christian Science Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/1985/1213/fge.html)). CBS wasn't sold, but Laurence Tisch's Loews built a 24.9% stake and Tisch became acting chief executive in September 1986 ([Time](https://time.com/archive/6707895/a-cut-above-the-ordinary/)). CBS News had about 215 fewer people by March 1987 ([Christian Science Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/1987/0313/ecbs.html)).

**The Fairness Doctrine ended.** Earlier in 1987 Congress passed a bill to write the doctrine into law, and President Reagan vetoed it. In August the FCC voted 4–0 to stop enforcing it, concluding that the rule chilled coverage of controversial issues rather than encouraging it and that the growth of outlets had weakened the scarcity argument; a federal appeals court upheld the decision in 1989 ([Reagan Library](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/topic-guide/fairness-doctrine); [Museum of Broadcast Communications](https://www.museum.tv/portfolio-2/fairness-doctrine); [Syracuse Peace Council v. FCC, D.C. Cir. 1989](https://openjurist.org/867/f2d/654)). The FCC formally deleted the rule from its books in 2011 ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2011-09-09/html/2011-23010.htm)).

New outlets appeared at the same time. CNN, the first 24-hour news channel, launched on June 1, 1980 ([Georgia Historical Society](https://www.georgiahistory.com/marker-monday-cnn-cable-news-network/)). Rupert Murdoch became a U.S. citizen in September 1985, which let him meet the FCC's ownership rules for the Metromedia stations that became the core of Fox, whose network launched in October 1986 ([CNN](https://edition.cnn.com/2013/06/10/world/rupert-murdoch-fast-facts); [Christian Science Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/1995/0728/28014.html); [WVXU](https://www.wvxu.org/media/2015-10-09/happy-29th-birthday-to-the-fox-network)). And a phrase for a kind of local newscast was in print by 1982: "if it bleeds, it leads" ([Barry Popik, citing *Broadcasting*](https://barrypopik.com/blog/if_it_bleeds_it_leads)).

## 1996: the Telecommunications Act

The 1996 Act removed the national limit on the number of TV stations one company could own and set the household-reach cap at 35%. For radio it went further, ending national ownership limits altogether ([Telecommunications Act of 1996](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-104publ104/pdf/PLAW-104publ104.pdf)). Radio consolidated fast. By the FCC's own count, Clear Channel went from 62 stations in March 1996 to 972 in March 2001, and the number of radio station owners fell by a quarter while the number of stations grew ([FCC, Review of the Radio Industry, 2001](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20090507195022/http://www.fcc.gov/mb/policy/docs/radio01.pdf)).

Two more cable news channels launched that year: MSNBC, a Microsoft–NBC venture, in July, and Fox News in October ([Museum of Broadcast Communications](https://www.museum.tv/tv-encyclopedia-11/msnbc); [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/print/article/1469592)).

By then the news had changed in a way researchers could measure. Thomas Patterson of Harvard's Shorenstein Center coded 5,331 stories from network newscasts, newsweeklies and national and local papers between 1980 and 1999. Stories with no clear connection to public policy rose from less than 35% to about half; stories rated moderately or highly sensational from about 25% to nearly 40%; human-interest stories from under 11% to 26% ([Patterson, *Doing Well and Doing Good*, 2000](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/37375431/2000_Patterson.pdf?sequence=1)).

## 2000s–2020s: the money leaves

The industry's old rule of thumb was about $4 of advertising for every $1 readers paid, roughly four-fifths of a paper's revenue, and the ratio peaked at 4.6 to 1 in 2005. Before the late 1970s, papers had depended on ads much less, closer to two dollars of ads for every dollar from readers ([Columbia Journalism Review](https://www.cjr.org/the_audit/newspaper_subscription_revenue.php)). Classified ads alone, for jobs, homes and cars, brought in $19.6 billion of the industry's $48.7 billion in ad revenue in 2000 ([FCC, Information Needs of Communities](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121017022339/http://www.fcc.gov/osp/inc-report/INoC-1-Newspapers.pdf)). Those advertisers moved to websites built for the purpose, many of them free to post on. Newspaper ad revenue peaked at $49.4 billion in 2005, before inflation, and was about $9.8 billion in 2022; since 2020, readers have paid newspapers more than advertisers have ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/newspapers/)).

Jobs followed the money. Newspaper newsrooms employed about 71,000 people in 2008 and about 31,000 in 2020, a drop of 57%; over the same years, newsrooms at digital-only outlets grew from about 7,400 to about 18,000, and broadcast TV newsrooms held steady at about 28,000 ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2021/07/13/u-s-newsroom-employment-has-fallen-26-since-2008/)). Counting everyone at newspaper publishers, from reporters to the press room and delivery, the Bureau of Labor Statistics shows the longer slide. In {{v newspaper_jobs period else="2025"}}, newspaper publishers employed about {{v newspaper_jobs fmt=k_round1k else="82,000"}} people ([BLS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IPUJN511110W200000000)). In 1990 they employed about 491,000. Magazine publishers lost about 60% of their jobs over the same years, falling from about 164,000 to 65,000, while radio and TV broadcasting lost about a fifth, from about 240,000 to 190,000 ([BLS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IPUJN51112W200000000); [BLS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/IPUJN5151W200000000)).

Ownership kept shifting as the money left. By 2000 only 23.4% of dailies were independently owned ([FCC, citing Noam](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20121017022339/http://www.fcc.gov/osp/inc-report/INoC-1-Newspapers.pdf)). Investment firms bought chains whose owners wanted out; one of the best known, Alden Global Capital, bought Tribune Publishing in 2021, and its executives describe the goal as making papers operate sustainably over the long term, while the NewsGuild says Alden cut staff at its union papers by an average of 75% over six years ([NPR, 2021](https://www.npr.org/2021/05/21/998730863/vulture-fund-alden-global-known-for-slashing-newsrooms-buys-tribune-papers)). Northwestern's Medill school counted 170 papers closed or merged in the year to its October 2026 report, about three a week, and more than 425 sold; 238 counties now have no local news source, up 26 in a year, the largest one-year rise in at least five years. It also counted 75 news startups in the same year ([Medill, 2026](https://www.medill.northwestern.edu/news/2026/local-news-deserts-skyrocket-as-newspaper-closures-escalate-even-as-startups-emerge-medill-study-shows.html)).

Local TV, which kept its audience longer, consolidated through station-group mergers. Of the country's 678 commercial TV newsrooms, three-quarters belong to the 10 largest chains ([Medill, 2026](https://www.medill.northwestern.edu/news/2026/local-news-deserts-skyrocket-as-newspaper-closures-escalate-even-as-startups-emerge-medill-study-shows.html)). In 2018, a montage of Sinclair Broadcast Group anchors reading the same company-written promo about "fake" news on dozens of stations drew wide attention; Sinclair called it part of a journalistic initiative focused on fair and objective reporting ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/donald-trump/tv-anchors-decrying-fake-news-put-spotlight-sinclair-broadcast-company-n862016)). Part two of this series covers the 2026 mergers.

## What the evidence says ownership does

Most of the studies are about local TV, where stations change owners often enough to compare.

- **Sinclair's 2017 purchase of 14 Bonten stations.** Comparing those stations with others in the same markets, political scientists Gregory Martin and Joshua McCrain found coverage of national politics rose by about a quarter, local politics fell, and coverage moved rightward; viewership dipped slightly, by an amount too small to be sure of ([American Political Science Review, 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/local-news-and-national-politics/C8EEA488A777C37C7987964F8F85AEB5)).
- **Three big station groups, 2010–2020.** Across more than 215 acquisitions by Sinclair, Gray and Nexstar, Martin and colleagues found the effect depended on the buyer: Sinclair stations cut local coverage about 10%, Gray's didn't change, and Nexstar's mentioned local places more. What every buyer did was run more advertising ([Stanford, December 2024, on the authors' working paper](https://news.stanford.edu/stories/2024/12/remote-control-how-consolidation-changing-local-tv-news)).
- **The industry's numbers.** A 2020 study by the economist Mark Fratrik, funded by Gray and covering Gray's 93 markets, found local news output rose almost 28% in markets where stations combined, against less than 18% where they didn't ([Next TV](https://www.nexttv.com/news/study-media-mergers-boost-local-news)). A study DirecTV filed with the FCC in 2026 found that co-owned stations in the same market shared 90.5% of their news websites and 98.2% of their news directors ([TV Technology](https://www.tvtechnology.com/regulatory-legal/directv-study-finds-consolidation-decreases-quality-and-diversity-of-local-news)). Both sides have a stake: station groups want merger approvals, and DirecTV pays the stations' carriage fees.

## Where the analogy breaks

A mall's owner answers only to its tenants and shoppers. Broadcasting came with public-interest rules because Washington decided news on the public airwaves owed something beyond its customers, and newspapers, which never had such rules, are what most of the closures and sales above are about. And the tenants didn't move to another mall. The ads went to websites that charge little or nothing, so the money that had paid for the newsroom wasn't there for any owner to follow.

## What people argue about

**Did new owners change the news, or did the money change the owners?** One reading puts ownership first: owners who run the same segments across many markets choose more national and less local news, as the Sinclair study found, and the NewsGuild blames investment-firm owners for deep newsroom cuts. The other reading puts money and technology first: the ad revenue left every newspaper, family-owned or not, and the measured shift toward softer news began in the 1980s, across outlets with many different owners. Patterson's own explanation was audiences: he argued softer news was a bid for viewers and readers who were drifting away, and that it was driving away more of them than it kept ([Patterson, 2000](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/37375431/2000_Patterson.pdf?sequence=1)). The station-group research adds that owners differ: the same kind of deal meant less local news under one buyer and more under another.

**Do ownership limits protect local news or starve it?** Supporters of limits, among them the FCC's Democratic commissioner and the advocacy group Free Press, say fewer owners means fewer independent newsrooms, and point to co-owned stations sharing staff and websites. The FCC's majority and the big station groups say broadcasters need scale to compete for viewers and ads with cable, streaming and the big online platforms, which face no such limits; Nexstar told the FCC this year that consolidation benefits broadcasters and the public ([Free Press](https://www.freepress.net/news/trump-fccs-ignores-evidence-and-law-approves-nexstars-tegna-takeover); [The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/03/fcc-says-it-granted-ownership-rule-waivers-to-approve-nexstar-tegna-deal/); [The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/10/nexstar-asks-fcc-to-reject-stay-of-national-tv-ownership-cap-repeal/)). Both sides cite the studies above, and [part two](who-owns-the-news-in-2026.html) covers where that argument stands in 2026.
