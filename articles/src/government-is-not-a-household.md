## In one sentence

A household borrows against a finite working life and has to pay the loan off; the federal government borrows against a tax base that doesn't retire, never has to pay the principal off as long as it can refinance, and is judged by its creditors on whether the economy grows faster than its interest rate.

## The analogy

The one everyone uses first: a family that spends more than it earns is heading for trouble, and so is a government. It is wrong in four ways that matter.

A household retires. Its income stops and its debt comes due against savings. The government's income is the tax base, which grows with the economy and has no retirement date, so the debt is never "due" the way a mortgage is; it is rolled over, continuously, as it has been since 1790.

A household can't tax. Its income is what employers pay it. The government's is what it decides to collect, within what voters will stand for, and that is a very different kind of ceiling.

A household's spending doesn't change its income. The government's does: a dollar it spends is someone's income, part of which comes back as tax, and in a slump that circuit is much of what holds incomes up.

A household can't print the currency its debt is in. The government can, which is why it cannot be forced into default on dollar debt. The catch is that printing to pay debt is inflation, which [rate vs. level](rate-vs-level.html) calls the quiet default. The constraint on a government isn't bankruptcy. It is prices.

The better analogy is a permanent business. A company that will exist forever doesn't pay off its debt either; it refinances it, and its lenders ask two questions: is revenue growing faster than the interest rate, and will it be able to borrow next year at a price it can carry? For the government, revenue is the economy. When growth exceeds the interest rate, a debt can be carried indefinitely even while running deficits; when the interest rate exceeds growth, the debt grows on its own and something has to give. That is the fiscal argument in one comparison, and the almanac's [10-year yield](what-is-the-10-year-yield.html) is one side of it.

## Where the analogy breaks

A permanent business can still go bankrupt; a government that borrows in its own currency can't be made to. What it can do is inflate, and lenders know it, so the price they charge rises with that risk, which is part of what the term premium on the 10-year measures. A business can't tax its customers; a government can, which makes its debt safer than any company's. And a business that borrows to build a factory has an asset against the loan. Much of what a government borrows for is consumption and transfers, which is the part of the household analogy that survives: borrowing to pay this year's bills is different from borrowing to build, whoever the borrower is.

## What people argue about

**Whether the debt matters.** One side: interest on the debt now costs more per year than national defense, the 10-year at 5% is the closest it has been to the economy's growth rate since 2007, and the arithmetic that let the 2010s run deficits for free is nearly gone; the permanent business has to cut costs or raise revenue. The other: Japan has carried far larger debts at near-zero rates for decades, the dollar's reserve status is a standing source of cheap borrowing, and the growth-versus-interest gap has flipped before and flipped back. Both read the same chart; they differ on whether 2023–26 is the new condition or a passing one.

**Which fix.** Given that the arithmetic has to be met, one side says cut spending, since the programs are the driver; the other says raise revenue, since taxes as a share of the economy are low by rich-country standards; a third says grow the denominator, since growth is what pays debt. Every budget is some mix, and the site marks the laws on the chart without scoring them.

**Whether the household analogy does harm.** Critics say it has justified cutting in slumps, which is the opposite of what the business that refinances through a bad year does; defenders say it is the only version of the constraint the public ever enforces, and governments freed from it have the inflation records to show. The permanent business borrows through the slump and watches the rate. Whether governments can be trusted to do the second half is the real disagreement.
