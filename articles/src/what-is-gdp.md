## In one sentence

GDP is the dollar value of all the goods and services produced in the United States in a quarter, and the growth rate on the almanac is how much that total rose or fell from the quarter before, after inflation, stated at an annual pace.

## The analogy

A paycheck, not a bank balance. GDP is a flow: what the economy earned this quarter, not what it owns. "GDP fell 1%" means this quarter's paycheck was smaller than last quarter's, not that the country got poorer. A country with a vast stock of houses, roads, factories and savings can have a bad quarter and still be rich, the way a family with a paid-off house can have a bad month. The question people usually want answered, are we better off than we were, is partly the paycheck's trend and partly the balance, and GDP measures only the first.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**Your job: this is where the job market comes from.** Hiring follows output with a lag of a quarter or two. Growth around 2% roughly holds unemployment steady; a stretch below 1% is when layoffs start; a run above 3% pulls people off the sidelines. The job market is GDP arriving at your door six months later.

**Your slice: through "per person."** The headline is the whole pie. What you live on is your share of it. With the population growing about half a percent a year, 2% growth is about 1.5% more per person; in 2023–24, when immigration pushed population growth higher, a larger part of the headline was more people rather than better-off people. Per-person growth is the number to compare across countries and decades.

**Markets, the Fed and the news: the day it comes out.** The advance estimate lands about four weeks after the quarter ends and moves markets for a morning. The Fed reads it alongside jobs and inflation. And a negative quarter is the loudest single number in American politics, which the argument section comes back to.

## What moves it

Four pieces. What households buy, about two-thirds of the total and the steadiest part. What businesses invest in: equipment, buildings, software, and the inventories they stock up or run down. What governments spend on goods and services, which means soldiers, roads and teachers but not Social Security checks; those show up when the recipients spend them. And exports minus imports. The last three swing, and inventories and trade swing hardest. In early 2025 a surge of imports ahead of tariffs pushed the first quarter to 0.1% and the reversal pushed the second to 4.0%, while households and businesses did roughly the same thing in both. The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes a line that strips out inventories, trade and government, "final sales to private domestic purchasers," which is what the Fed looks at when the headline is doing that.

## How to read this quarter's number

**It is annualized.** "{{v gdp}}" is not what happened in the quarter. The quarter's actual change was about {{fig gdp_quarterly}}, stated as if it continued for a year, so that quarters can be compared with annual figures. The cost is that every wobble looks four times bigger than it was.

**It is a first draft.** Each quarter gets an advance estimate, a second and a third, as the slow data (services, trade, inventories) arrive, and then annual revisions for years after that. Since 2022 the gap between the advance estimate and the current figure has averaged about 0.6 of a point in one direction or the other, and three quarters have moved by more than a point. The first estimate for the second quarter of 2022 was minus 0.9%. It now reads plus 0.6%. The two negative quarters of 2022, which were a political fight at the time, no longer exist in the data.

**It is already real.** Inflation is taken out. In 2022 nominal GDP grew about 9% and real GDP about 2%; the difference was prices, not production. [Real vs. nominal](real-vs-nominal.html) has that pair.

## Where the analogy breaks

A paycheck is money someone can spend. GDP counts things nobody buys: government services at cost, the rent homeowners would pay themselves, the rebuilding after a hurricane, the gasoline burned in a longer commute. It leaves out things that plainly have value: a parent's child care, a volunteer's hours, the free things the internet replaced. And a paycheck belongs to one person. GDP is everyone's added together, so the total can rise while most people's slice doesn't, and the number will not tell you which.

## What people argue about

**GDP versus how people feel.** In 2023–24 the United States grew faster than any other large rich country, inflation fell, unemployment stayed between 3.4% and 4.2% ([UNRATE](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/UNRATE)), and consumer sentiment surveys sat at levels usually seen in recessions. One side says the data were right and the mood was partisanship plus the memory of 2022's price jump, the level rather than the rate. The other says the aggregates missed what households felt: prices up 20% in three years, interest on cards and car loans that GDP doesn't subtract, and gains concentrated at the top. The data can show the growth and the price level. It can't say which one people should weigh.

**Whether government spending counts.** Government purchases are in GDP at cost. One camp says that inflates the figure with spending nobody chose and that produces nothing measurable; the other says private output depends on roads, courts and schools, and that markets simply don't price public goods. Transfers like Social Security aren't in GDP at all. Both sides agree on the accounting; they disagree about whether it measures value.

**What a recession is.** Two negative quarters is a rule of thumb, not a definition. The National Bureau of Economic Research's committee dates recessions months after the fact from jobs, incomes, sales and production, and it has called recessions without two negative quarters (2001) and declined to call them with two (2022, as first reported). The 2022 argument was about the word. The data were the same for both sides, and the data have since changed.
