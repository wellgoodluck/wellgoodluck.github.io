## In one sentence

The longer someone has rented the same home, the further their rent has usually fallen behind what a newcomer would pay, so a move costs them not just a truck and a deposit but the whole gap, every month, from then on.

## Why staying is cheap and moving is expensive

Rents for people who stay put are sticky. Landlords raise them less often, and usually by less, than they raise the asking rent for the next tenant, partly because a reliable tenant saves them a vacancy, cleaning, repairs and the risk of a worse replacement. Researchers at the Bureau of Labor Statistics who followed tens of thousands of rental units in the price-index survey found that continuing tenants pay a few percent less than a new tenant would for the same unit on average, and that the gap is wider the longer the tenant has been there.

That discount builds without anyone noticing, and it is invisible until it is gone. A 2025 study of older renters in New York City found that their larger rent savings came from the length of their tenancies, not their age. A 2026 analysis of metros with many long-term renters found that close to four in ten of those renter households could manage their current rent but could not afford the going rate for a similar place in the same city.

This is the same lag that makes [the rent line in the inflation number](you-rent.html) trail the listings by a year: most renters are mid-lease, or long past their first one.

## The fixed-income squeeze

Social Security rises once a year by a cost-of-living adjustment computed from a price index for working households. Rent is in that index, but only as one item among many. When rent outruns prices overall, as it has since 2019, a benefit that keeps up with prices falls behind on rent.

Older renters feel housing costs far more than older homeowners, many of whom have paid off their mortgages. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies, using the Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey, found that 58% of renters 65 and older spent more than 30% of their income on housing, and more than 60% of renters 80 and older did.

## The paperwork wall

Many landlords screen applicants with a rule of thumb: monthly income of at least three times the rent. It is a habit, not a law, and it was built for people with paychecks.

For a retiree the arithmetic is severe. The average retired worker's Social Security benefit will be about $2,170 a month in January 2027. Under a three-times rule, that qualifies for rent of about $723. The median asking rent for studios through two-bedrooms in the 50 largest metros was $1,694 in September 2026.

Many older renters get by on help that doesn't show up as income on paper: savings, a small pension, money from family. A few ways to make that help count with a landlord:

- **Bank statements** showing months of regular deposits from whatever the source.
- **Signed letters** from the family members or friends who contribute, stating the amount and how long they have given it.
- **A co-signer or guarantor** with income that meets the landlord's rule.
- **Proof of savings or retirement accounts**, which some landlords accept in place of a monthly income.
- **Smaller landlords**, who more often judge an applicant as a person than as a ratio, and will weigh eleven years of on-time rent.

Some states and cities limit how landlords may screen on income, or require them to count benefits and vouchers. It is worth checking before applying.

## The up-front cost

The costs of moving come due together, before the first night in the new place: the first month's rent, a security deposit (state limits vary), application and screening fees paid to every landlord applied to, pet deposits or monthly pet rent, movers or a truck, and utility deposits and connection fees. The old deposit comes back, if it comes back in full, weeks after the move.

## What cash for keys is

"Cash for keys" means a landlord pays a tenant to move out voluntarily by an agreed date. For the landlord it is usually faster and cheaper than court, where an eviction can take months and cost legal fees. For the tenant it can mean money to move with and no eviction filing, which matters because filings can show up in tenant screening for years, even when the tenant wins.

It is an offer, not an order. A few principles hold almost everywhere:

- **Find out the rules where you live first.** Some states and cities require a landlord to have a legal reason to end a long tenancy, and some require relocation payments when the reason is no fault of the tenant's. Notice periods vary too. What a landlord would have to do without your agreement is the floor under any offer.
- **Compare the offer with what moving will cost you**, not with zero. The worked example above is the arithmetic; use your own numbers.
- **Everything is negotiable**: the amount, the move-out date, the deposit.
- **Get it in writing**: the amount, the date, the full return of the deposit, and when and how the money is paid. Payment when the keys are handed over protects both sides.
- **Know who has authority.** If the owner has died or can no longer act, the person making the offer needs legal authority to make it: an executor, a trustee, or someone holding a power of attorney.

## Where to get help

Legal aid offices give free civil legal help to low-income renters, and many programs funded under the Older Americans Act serve anyone 60 or older. [LawHelp.org](https://www.lawhelp.org) lists them by state. The [Eldercare Locator](https://eldercare.acl.gov), 1-800-677-1116, connects older adults and their families to the local Area Agency on Aging, which can point to housing counseling and senior legal services. Dialing 2-1-1 reaches local housing and rental-assistance programs in most of the country.

## What people argue about

**How much protection long tenancies should have.** Supporters of stronger rules argue that forced moves fall hardest on older and lower-income renters, that the cost of being displaced is much larger than the rent difference alone, and that stable tenancies keep people in their communities and out of costlier care. Opponents argue that limits on ending a tenancy take control of a property away from its owner, discourage people from renting homes out at all, and over time shrink the supply of rentals, which raises rents for everyone still looking. The evidence on both effects is real; the disagreement is over which matters more, and to whom.

**Whether the income rule is fair.** Landlords say the three-times rule is a simple, consistent screen that predicts who can pay, and that it protects tenants from taking on rent they can't carry. Critics say it shuts out renters whose means are real but irregular, retirees with savings and people supported by family among them, and that a long record of paying rent on time predicts more than a ratio does.

What isn't in dispute is the arithmetic: the longer someone has rented in one place, the more a move costs them.
