## Short version

A car repair, a vet bill, a short paycheck. Everyone gets them, and without some cash set aside, each one gets paid with something that costs extra: an overdraft fee, a payday loan, a cash advance, a credit card balance. An emergency cushion is just money you keep for that, in a separate savings account you don't touch for anything else. It doesn't have to start big. A few hundred dollars handles the most common surprises, and it's the first goal, before investing and before paying extra on low-rate debt.

## The trap in one sentence

Without a cushion, every surprise bill becomes a loan, and the loans in this series are expensive.

## How it works

**Why $400.** Since 2013, the Federal Reserve has asked adults how they would pay an unexpected $400 expense. In [its survey of 2025](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-savings-investments.htm), 63% said they would cover it with cash, savings or a credit card paid off at the next statement. That means 37% would have to borrow, sell something or skip paying, and 12% said they couldn't pay it by any means. The 63% was unchanged from 2024 and down from a high of 68% in 2021. Only 55% said they had enough set aside to cover three months of expenses.

**Where the surprise goes when there's no cushion.** Each of these is a trap with its own page in this series:

- **[Overdraft fees](overdraft-fees.html):** the card goes through anyway, and the bank charges a fee, [often around $35 at large banks](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-09-10_IF11460_87163c672312a8df5aa57e853366bbb8c33f04c9.html), for each overdrawn purchase.
- **[Payday loans and paycheck apps](payday-loans-and-paycheck-apps.html):** a two-week loan with a fee that works out to a very high annual rate, and that many borrowers roll over again and again.
- **[Credit card balances](credit-card-minimum-payments.html)** and **[cash advances](credit-card-cash-advances.html):** interest at 20% or more, and on a cash advance, a fee and interest from day one.
- **[Buy now, pay later](buy-now-pay-later.html):** fine once, risky when several plans' payments land in the same week.
- **Borrowing from a [401(k)](employer-retirement-match.html):** possible in many plans, but it pulls money out of investing and can turn into taxes and a penalty if you leave the job.

**Two goals, not one.** A common approach is a starter cushion, a few hundred dollars to $1,000 or so, and then a fuller one of three to six months of essential expenses. The starter amount comes first because it covers most surprises and stops the expensive borrowing. The fuller one protects against a job loss, and it can take years. There's no official number for either; the right size depends on how steady your pay is and who depends on you.

**Where to keep it.** A savings account at a bank or credit union insured by the FDIC or NCUA, separate from checking so it's not spent by accident, and easy to reach in a day or two. Not invested in stocks, which can be down just when you need the money.

## How to avoid it

**Start with a number you can reach.** $400 or $500 first. Hitting a small goal makes the next one easier.

**Make it automatic.** Set up a transfer to savings on payday, even $10 or $20. Money that moves before you see it is the easiest money to save. Many employers will also split your direct deposit between two accounts.

**Use the windfalls.** A tax refund, a bonus, a birthday check, the first paycheck after a raise ([lifestyle creep](lifestyle-creep.html)): put part of each into the cushion.

**Keep it out of sight.** A savings account at a different bank than your checking account adds a day's delay, which is often enough to stop you from spending it on something that isn't an emergency.

**Decide what counts as an emergency before one happens.** Car repair, medical bill, a missed paycheck, yes. Concert tickets, a sale, no.

**Refill it after you use it.** Using the cushion is the point. Afterward, put the automatic transfer back to work until it's full again.

**If you have high-rate debt too,** build the starter cushion first, then put extra money toward the debt. Without the cushion, the next surprise goes right back on the card.

## The bigger picture

Policymakers have tried to bring emergency saving into the workplace. The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 created two options for employers' retirement plans, both available since 2024:

- **Emergency savings accounts linked to the retirement plan** (the law calls them pension-linked emergency savings accounts). Employees who aren't highly paid can keep up to $2,500 of their own contributions in the account, adjusted for inflation ([$2,600 in 2026](https://www.segalco.com/consulting-insights/numbers-retirement-plan-sponsors-need-to-know-for-2026/)). The money goes in after tax, and workers can take it out at least once a month with no penalty. Employers can enroll workers automatically at up to 3% of pay. Employers can't put money into the emergency account itself, but if the plan matches regular contributions, it has to match these too, with the match going into the retirement account.
- **Emergency withdrawals.** Plans, and IRAs, can let workers take out [up to $1,000 once a calendar year](https://www.napa-net.org/news/2024/6/irs-clarifies-10-additional-tax-certain-emergency-distributions/) for an emergency without the usual 10% early-withdrawal penalty, though income tax still applies. After one, there's no other for three years unless the money is paid back or new contributions at least as large go in.

Few employers have added the linked accounts. An August 2026 article from the Plan Sponsor Council of America described adoption as [close to nonexistent](https://www.psca.org/news/psca-news/2026/8/plan-sponsors-remain-wary-of-in-plan-emergency-savings-accounts/); in one of the council's polls, no sponsor in the sample offered one, though 13% were considering it. Gallagher found in October 2026 that [fewer than one in 20 employers](https://www.insurancebusinessmag.com/us/news/benefits/more-than-half-of-us-workers-have-no-emergency-cushion-and-few-employers-offer-one-593641.aspx) offered any formal emergency savings program. A bill to make the linked accounts easier to offer, the Emergency Savings Enhancement Act, passed the Senate's labor and pensions committee [19 to 3 in July 2026](https://www.napa-net.org/news/2026/7/senate-panel-advances-plesa-enhancements-employee-ownership-funding/). It would open the accounts to all workers in the plan, including highly paid ones, and raise the cap from $2,500 to $5,000.

**Supporters of workplace emergency savings** argue that people who have emergency savings are less likely to raid their retirement accounts, and that [the BlackRock Emergency Savings Initiative's June 2026 report](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/emergency-savings-gaps-are-quietly-draining-american-retirement-accounts/267120), drawing on seven years of employer pilots, found that about one in five workers who hadn't been saving for retirement started after opening an emergency account.

**Skeptics**, including many plan sponsors, argue that the linked accounts are complicated for employers and recordkeepers to run, which is why few have added them, and that simpler savings accounts outside the retirement plan, which some employers already offer, do the same job with less risk of compliance mistakes.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 11, 2026.
