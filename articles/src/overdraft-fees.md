## Short version

If your checking account runs short and your bank covers a purchase anyway, it may charge an overdraft fee. [Many large banks charge about $35](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-09-10_IF11460_87163c672312a8df5aa57e853366bbb8c33f04c9.html), no matter how small the purchase. For debit card swipes and ATM withdrawals, you can avoid this entirely: don't opt in to overdraft coverage, and the bank declines the purchase instead of charging you. There is currently no federal limit on how much a bank can charge.

## The trap in one sentence

Your bank lends you a few dollars for a few days and charges you $35 for it.

## How it works

An overdraft happens when your bank pays for something your balance can't cover. The bank covers the gap, then charges a flat fee. The fee doesn't depend on how much you were short. Being $3 short costs the same as being $300 short.

You then owe the bank the shortfall plus the fee, and it usually comes out of your next deposit. If that deposit is a paycheck, you start the next pay period behind.

## A worked example

You have $20 in checking on a Tuesday, and payday is Friday.

{{illustration name=overdraft-statement caption="The worked example as a statement. Each fee is $35, whether you are $4 short or $40 short."}}

You spent $43 more than you had. You now owe the bank $148: the $43, plus $105 in fees. The fees cost more than twice what you borrowed, for a loan that lasted three days.

Banks differ. Some cap how many overdraft fees they charge per day, some don't charge for very small overdrafts, and some give you until the end of the day to deposit money and avoid the fee. Check your own bank's fee schedule.

## How to avoid it

**Don't opt in for debit and ATM transactions.** [Since 2010, banks have needed your permission](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1005.17) before charging overdraft fees on everyday debit card purchases and ATM withdrawals. If you never opted in, or you opt out now, those transactions are simply declined. Being declined at a register is embarrassing. It's also free. You can usually check or change this in your bank's app, or by calling.

{{illustration name=overdraft-opt-in caption="What the setting can look like in a bank's app. Names and wording vary by bank; look for "overdraft coverage" or "overdraft protection" for debit cards."}}

**Know what opting out doesn't cover.** Checks, automatic bill payments from your account, and recurring card charges like subscriptions can still overdraw your account and trigger a fee, or a fee for a returned payment. If you have autopays set up, keep a cushion.

**Link a savings account as a backup.** Many banks will pull money from your savings to cover a shortfall. Some charge a small transfer fee and many don't. Either way it's usually far cheaper than $35.

**Turn on low-balance alerts.** Most bank apps will text you when your balance drops below an amount you pick.

**Consider a bank that doesn't charge overdraft fees.** [Many credit unions and online banks have dropped them entirely](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/checking/checking-account-fees/).

**If it happens, ask for a refund.** Call and ask, especially if it's your first time. Banks often waive a fee for customers who ask.

Checking your statement each month catches overdraft fees and other surprises early; [The Charge You Never Noticed](reading-your-statements.html) explains the deadlines.

Keeping your own running balance is the surest way to stay out of this; [The Balance Your App Shows](how-a-checking-account-works.html) explains how.

## The bigger picture

Overdraft fees have been a long-running fight in Washington.

In late 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau [finalized a rule for banks and credit unions with more than $10 billion in assets](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-09-10_IN12513_7f82cb854408a4c2ce7e2e777f36a2e089fdc9c0.pdf). Those banks could charge an overdraft fee of $5 or less, charge only enough to cover their actual costs, or treat overdraft coverage as a loan, with the same disclosures as a credit card. In May 2025, before the rule took effect, a resolution from Congress overturning it was signed into law. [Today there is no federal maximum overdraft fee](https://www.americascreditunions.org/node/16756).

**Supporters of a cap** argue that a flat $35 fee for a small, short-term shortfall is wildly out of proportion to the bank's cost, and that the fees fall hardest on people with the least money, who overdraft most often.

**Opponents of a cap** argue it was government overregulation beyond the bureau's legal authority, that banks would respond by offering overdraft coverage, and even checking accounts, to fewer customers, and that people who need short-term cash would turn to worse options such as payday loans.

Meanwhile, the fees have shrunk anyway. The Congressional Research Service, citing CFPB data through 2023, noted that [overdraft fee revenue fell by nearly half](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2025-09-10_IN12513_7f82cb854408a4c2ce7e2e777f36a2e089fdc9c0.pdf) in recent years as many banks cut or dropped the fees, amid both competition and regulatory scrutiny.

These rules have changed more than once and may change again. This page reflects federal law as of November 8, 2026.
