## Short version

Many employers add money to your retirement account when you contribute to it. That's called a match, and it's part of your pay. If you don't contribute enough to get the full match, you're turning down money your employer has already offered. Starting early matters even more than the match itself: ten years of saving in your twenties can be worth nearly as much as thirty-three years of saving starting at 32.

## The trap in one sentence

Every other trap in this series sends you a bill; this one just quietly never pays you.

## How it works

**A 401(k)** (or a 403(b) at schools, hospitals and nonprofits) is a retirement account offered through your job. You choose a share of each paycheck to put in, and it's invested, usually in a mix of stock and bond funds.

**The match** is your employer adding to it. A common formula is "50 cents for every dollar you put in, up to 6% of your pay," but formulas vary widely, so check yours. Vanguard reported that [the average match offered was worth 4.7% of pay](https://workplace.vanguard.com/content/dam/inst/iig-transformation/has/2026/pdf/HowAmericaSaves2026.pdf) in 2025.

**The catch:** you only get the match if you put money in yourself. Contribute less than the formula's limit, and you leave part of it behind.

**Two more things to know:**

- **Taxes.** Money in a traditional 401(k) goes in before income tax, so putting in $100 lowers your take-home pay by less than $100. A Roth 401(k), if your plan offers one, works the other way: you pay tax now and withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.
- **Vesting.** Your own contributions are always yours. The employer's match may require you to stay a certain number of years before it fully belongs to you. Under federal law, [that can take up to three years for full vesting](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/29/1053) all at once, or six years if it vests gradually.

**Many people are enrolled without asking.** [About six in ten plans Vanguard administers](https://workplace.vanguard.com/content/dam/inst/iig-transformation/has/2026/pdf/HowAmericaSaves2026.pdf) automatically enroll new workers, and federal law now requires most newly created plans to do so. But the default rate is often below what it takes to get the full match. Being enrolled isn't the same as getting all the free money.

## A worked example

**The match you miss.** You earn $45,000. Your employer matches 50% of what you put in, up to 6% of pay.

$675 a year doesn't sound like much. Invested from age 25 to 65 at a 6% average annual return, it would grow to about $110,700.

**The cost of waiting.** Two workers each put $4,050 a year into their accounts (6% of $45,000, plus the full match) and earn a 6% average return.

Ten extra years of contributions, $40,500 in all, nearly double the result. In fact, someone who saved only from 22 to 31 and then stopped completely would end up with about $387,000, nearly as much as the person who saved from 32 all the way to 65.

{{illustration name=two-savers caption="The three savers' balances from 22 to 65, at an assumed 6% a year."}}

These examples assume a flat salary and a steady 6% return. Real returns go up and down from year to year, nobody can promise any rate, and in practice salaries and contributions usually rise over a career. The point is the shape, not the exact numbers: early dollars have the most time to grow.

## How to avoid it

**Find your match formula.** It's in your benefits materials or on your plan's website. Look for "matches X% of contributions up to Y% of pay."

**Contribute at least enough to get the full match.** If money is tight, this is the one retirement step worth prioritizing, since it's an instant return before any investment growth.

**If you were auto-enrolled, check your rate.** Raise it to at least the match level. Many plans also let you set automatic increases of 1% a year.

**Don't cash out when you change jobs.** Withdrawing early usually means income tax plus a 10% penalty. Roll the money into your new employer's plan or an IRA instead.

**Pay off high-rate debt too.** If you're carrying a credit card balance at 22% ([how card interest works](credit-card-minimum-payments.html)), get the match and then put extra money toward the card. Few investments reliably beat paying off 22% debt.

**No plan at work?** You can open an IRA on your own at most brokerages. [The 2026 limit for 401(k) contributions is $24,500](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500); the IRA limit is $7,500.

Raising your contribution each time you get a raise is the easiest way to get there; see [Where Did the Raise Go?](lifestyle-creep.html).

## The bigger picture

The biggest gap isn't people skipping the match. It's people without a plan at all. In March 2026, [72% of private-industry workers had access to a retirement plan at work](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2026/retirement-benefits-were-available-to-72-percent-of-private-industry-workers-in-march-2026.htm), and 52% participated, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics.

Policy has leaned toward nudges. The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 [requires most new 401(k) and 403(b) plans, starting in 2025](https://www.grantthornton.com/insights/newsletters/tax/2025/hot-topics/jan-28/proposed-regs-address-enrollment-for-certain-retirement-plans), to enroll workers automatically at 3% or more, rising each year to at least 10%, with workers free to opt out. Very small and very new businesses, churches and governments are exempt. Starting with 2027 contributions, [a federal "Saver's Match" is scheduled](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-begin-implementing-executive-order-14403-by-announcing-intent-to-issue-proposed-regulations-on-savers-match-which-will-benefit-millions-of-low-and-moderate-income-taxpayers) to add up to $1,000 a year to the retirement accounts of lower- and moderate-income savers, with the first deposits in 2028. Many states also run their own programs that require employers without a plan to enroll workers in a state-run IRA.

**Supporters of automatic enrollment** argue it works: people rarely opt out, and participation jumps when saving is the default rather than something you have to sign up for.

**Skeptics** argue that defaults can be set too low to be enough, that money locked in retirement accounts isn't there for emergencies, which pushes some savers into early withdrawals and penalties (since 2024 the law allows one penalty-free emergency withdrawal of up to $1,000 a year), and that mandates add costs for small employers.

These rules may change. This page reflects federal law as of November 9, 2026.
