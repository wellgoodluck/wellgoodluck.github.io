## The short version

Most American dental offices are still small: about 135,000 offices with employees, nearly all with fewer than 20 people. But fewer dentists own their practices than a generation ago, and a growing share work with dental support organizations, companies that run the business side of many offices. The share of dentists affiliated with one rose from 7.2% in 2015 to 16.1% in 2024, and more than a quarter of dentists within ten years of dental school are. Many of those groups are owned by private equity firms; one study put the share of dentists in private equity–affiliated practices at about 13% in 2021, nearly double the 2015 share. A 2025 study of practices that private equity bought found that their list prices rose about 3%, that the prices insurers actually paid didn't change, and that the work shifted away from preventive care toward fillings, crowns, specialty work and surgery. Dental chains serving children on Medicaid have paid tens of millions of dollars to settle claims of unnecessary treatment, and California settled allegations against Aspen Dental this year. California's 2025 law bars private equity owners from interfering in dentists' clinical decisions.

## The analogy

A restaurant whose chef owns the kitchen, but whose building, staff, purchasing, books and advertising are handled by a management company that also handles hundreds of other restaurants and takes a share of the revenue. The chef still decides what goes on the plate. The question is how much the company's targets shape what the chef cooks.

The chef is the dentist who legally owns the practice. The management company is the dental support organization, and behind many of them, a private equity fund.

Figures below are as of early November 2026.

## How big the field is

The Census Bureau counted 135,665 dental offices with employees in 2023, employing about 1 million people; about 95% had fewer than 20 employees ([Census Bureau, County Business Patterns](https://data.census.gov/table/CBP2023.CB2300CBP?n=621210)). The BLS counted 161,400 dentist jobs in 2025, with median pay of $176,110 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/dentists.htm)).

The American Dental Association's research arm tracks how dentists practice:

- About 65% of dentists in private practice worked alone in 1999; 46% did in 2021 ([ADA Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/research/hpi/hpigraphic_solo_practice_continues_to_decrease.pdf)).
- The share who owned their practice fell from 84.7% in 2005 to 73% in 2021 ([ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/ada-news/2022/march/hpi-changes-in-dentist-demographics-practice-modalities-accelerating/)).
- The share affiliated with a dental support organization was 16.1% in 2024, up from 7.2% in 2015 ([Becker's Dental, citing the ADA](https://www.beckersdental.com/benchmarking/16-of-us-dentists-affiliated-with-a-dso-state-by-state-breakdown/); [ADA Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/resources/research/health-policy-institute/dental-practice-research/practice-modalities-among-us-dentists)). In 2024 more than one in four dentists up to ten years out of dental school were affiliated ([ADA News](https://adanews.ada.org/new-dentist/2025/november/hpi-more-new-dentists-affiliated-with-dsos/)).

Not every dental group is owned by private equity: some large ones are owned by their founders or by dentists and employees. Economists at the ADA and DePaul University estimated that the share of dentists affiliated with private equity nearly doubled, from 6.6% in 2015 to 12.8% in 2021, rising particularly among larger practices and specialists ([Nasseh, LoSasso and Vujicic, Health Affairs, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1377/hlthaff.2023.00574)).

## How a non-dentist can run a dental office

Most states bar anyone but a licensed dentist from owning a dental practice. California, for example, treats anyone who manages or owns a place where dental work is done as practicing dentistry ([Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 1625](https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-bpc/division-2/chapter-4/article-2/section-1625/)). The workaround is a contract. A dentist owns the practice on paper; a dental support organization, often owned by investors, supplies the office, staff, equipment, billing, marketing and management, and is paid a fee. The arrangement is legal when the dentist keeps control of clinical decisions; enforcement cases turn on whether that's true in practice.

## The big groups

Location counts are each group's own:

- **Heartland Dental**, majority-owned by KKR, supports more than 2,000 offices and more than 3,200 doctors in 38 states and Washington, D.C. ([Heartland Dental, via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/heartland-dental-reaches-2-000-supported-dental-offices-expanding-its-doctor-led-culture-and-support-model-nationwide-302914622.html)).
- **The Aspen Group**, owner of Aspen Dental and ClearChoice, has more than 1,400 locations in 46 states across its dental, urgent care, veterinary and aesthetics brands ([The Aspen Group](https://aspendental.mediaroom.com/2026-03-10-The-Aspen-Group-TAG-Invests-in-the-Future-of-Retail-Healthcare,-Reporting-Strong-2025-Results)). Aspen Dental uses one national name. In May 2026 Bloomberg reported that the group, owned by Leonard Green, Ares and American Securities, was seeking new investors as its earnings slumped, with about $3 billion of loans due in 2027 ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-05-19/aspen-group-eyes-new-investors-to-address-debt-as-earnings-slump)); in June it paused a planned sale of its urgent-care business, citing improved results, and said it was working on a refinancing ([Octus](https://octus.com/resources/articles/aspen-group-pauses-wellnow-sale-targets-q3-for-comprehensive-refi/)).
- **PDS Health**, formerly Pacific Dental Services, is privately held and led by its founder, Stephen Thorne, and supports more than 1,000 practices, with $3.1 billion in 2025 revenue ([PDS Health](https://www.pdshealth.com/news/press-releases/pds-health-reports-2025-progress-in-integrated-care-and-expanded/)); we found no disclosed private equity owner. Its offices use local names that pair a town with "Modern Dentistry," under the Smile Generation brand ([PDS Health press kit](https://www.smilegeneration.com/content/dam/web-production/md/images/unprocessed/PDS-Health-Press-Kit_011525.pdf)).
- **Sonrava Health**, owner of Western Dental, is owned by New Mountain Capital and has more than 580 offices in 20 states ([New Mountain Capital](https://www.newmountaincapital.com/portfolio/sonrava-health/)). Western Dental describes itself as California's largest Medi-Cal dental provider ([Sonrava Health](https://www.sonrava.com/news/westerndentalrespondstostatebudget)).
- **Smile Brands**, owned by Gryphon Investors since 2016, has more than 600 offices in 29 states under 75-plus names, among them Bright Now! Dental ([Gryphon Investors](https://www.gryphon-inv.com/companies/smile-brands-inc/); [Smile Brands](https://smilebrands.com/about/)).
- **MB2 Dental**, backed by Charlesbank, KKR and Warburg Pincus, said in 2024 that it had partnered with more than 700 practices in 43 states since 2007 ([MB2, via Business Wire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20241113108475/en/MB2-Dental-Announces-Recapitalization-Event-With-New-Investor-Warburg-Pincus-Solidifying-Company-Growth-Milestone)).

**A year of debt trouble.** Groups bought with borrowed money have struggled with it. In June 2026 Dental Care Alliance cut more than $1.1 billion of debt in a restructuring that left its lenders as majority owners ([Dental Care Alliance, via Business Wire](https://www.morningstar.com/news/business-wire/20260602555971/dental-care-alliance-closes-transaction-to-significantly-strengthen-long-term-financial-foundation-and-support-continued-growth); [Dental Economics](https://www.dentaleconomics.com/money/investments/article/55394111/dental-care-alliances-restructuring-signals-a-new-era-for-dsos)). In August 2026 Affordable Care, which runs Affordable Dentures & Implants, passed to its lenders, led by Blackstone and KKR, with its debt reduced by about $1 billion ([PitchBook](https://pitchbook.com/news/articles/affordable-care-turned-over-to-private-credit-lenders-reduces-debt-by-1b)).

## Why dentists sell, and join

**Debt.** Dental school graduates in 2025 who borrowed owed an average of $297,800 ([American Dental Education Association](https://www.adea.org/home/publications/research-and-data/graduating-oral-health-students/dentists-of-tomorrow-2025)).

**Price.** An adviser that sells practices puts practices bought as add-ons to an existing group at about 5 to 8 times adjusted earnings, and practices bought as a group's first "platform" at 9 to 13 times ([Focus Investment Banking](https://focusbankers.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Maximizing-Your-Dental-Practice-Sale.pdf)).

**Insurance.** Reimbursement rates to dentists, averaged across all payers, rose 19% from January 2021 to August 2026 while consumer prices rose 27%, which the ADA calls a fiscal squeeze on practices ([ADA Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/research/hpi/state_us_dental_economy_q32026.pdf)).

## What dental care costs

The BLS price index for dental services rose 153% from September 2000 to September 2026, against 94% for all items. Since September 2019 it has roughly matched overall inflation, 32.2% against 31.2%, but in the year to September 2026 it rose 6.0%, against 3.7% ([BLS, dental services](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SEMC02); [BLS, all items](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SA0)). Americans spent $189.2 billion on dental care in 2024; patients and private insurance paid about 80% of it ([CMS](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/highlights.pdf)).

## What the evidence shows

**What changes after a purchase.** A 2025 study by Nasseh and colleagues compared practices bought by private equity from 2015 to 2021 with similar practices that weren't. After a purchase, the practices' list prices rose about 3.3%, but the prices insurers negotiated didn't change. The mix of work shifted away from preventive care toward restorative, specialty and surgical procedures, and practices became more likely to offer several specialties. Their participation in Medicaid didn't change ([Health Services Research](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6773.70075)). The authors noted that such gains for the practices "may not translate into benefits for providers or patients." The study didn't measure patients' health.

**Medicaid.** Dentists affiliated with dental support organizations are more likely to take Medicaid: 53.3% against 40.3% for others ([ADA and Association of Dental Support Organizations](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/research/hpi/dental_care_access_vulnerable_populations.pdf)). That comparison doesn't show whether the groups caused the difference.

**Enforcement.** Several chains serving children on Medicaid have settled federal allegations of unnecessary or improperly billed treatment; in the Kool Smiles and MB2 cases the government said there was no determination of liability:

- **Small Smiles** (FORBA), 2010: $24 million, to resolve allegations of medically unnecessary or substandard baby root canals, crowns, extractions, fillings and anesthesia ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/national-dental-management-company-pays-24-million-resolve-fraud-allegations)).
- **Kool Smiles** (Benevis), 2018: $23.9 million, over claims of unnecessary root canals, extractions and crowns from 2009 to 2011 ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/usao-ct/pr/dental-management-company-benevis-and-its-affiliated-kool-smiles-dental-clinics-pay-239)).
- **MB2 Dental** and 21 affiliated pediatric practices, 2017: $8.45 million to Texas Medicaid, over allegations of billing for fillings not provided, using the wrong provider numbers, and paying kickbacks to patients' families and marketers ([HHS Office of Inspector General](https://oig.hhs.gov/fraud/enforcement/texas-dental-management-firm-21-affiliated-dental-practices-and-their-owners-and-marketing-chief-agree-to-pay-845-million-to-resolve-allegations-of-false-medicaid-claims-for-pediatric-dental-services/)).

In May 2026 California's attorney general settled with Aspen Dental for $2 million in penalties and $300,000 in restitution, alleging that it practiced dentistry without a license through its management role, paid hygienists incentives for selling clear aligners, and advertised "all insurance or no insurance" when its offices took no public insurance. The settlement, subject to court approval, resolves allegations; Aspen agreed not to base its service fees on practices' revenue, sales or profits, not to pay practice staff sales incentives, and to name practice owners in its ads ([California Attorney General](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-announces-settlement-aspen-dental-over-corporate-practice)). Aspen said the agreement "formalizes many of the best practices already in place across the network" ([FOX40](https://fox40.com/news/aspen-dental-lawsuit-california-false-advertising/)). Massachusetts settled with Aspen for $3.5 million over its advertising in 2023 ([Massachusetts Attorney General](https://www.mass.gov/news/attorney-generals-office-reaches-35-million-settlement-with-aspen-dental-over-claims-of-deceptive-advertising)). In 2024 KFF Health News and CBS News reported that implant experts and patients' lawsuits alleged some chain-clinic patients were advised to remove teeth that could have been saved; the companies denied wrongdoing ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/dental-implants-investigation-failures-unnecessary-healthy-teeth/)).

## California's new rules

A law that took effect January 1, 2026, co-sponsored by the California Dental Association and the California Medical Association, bars private equity groups and hedge funds involved with dental and medical practices from interfering in clinical decisions, such as which tests to order or how many patients to see, and from controlling patient records, billing and coding. It also bars noncompete clauses in those groups' contracts with dentists and doctors, which California law already largely voided, except in the sale of a practice. It doesn't ban the deals, and the attorney general enforces it ([Sidley Austin](https://www.sidley.com/en/insights/newsupdates/2025/10/newly-enacted-california-law-formalizes-corporate-practice-restrictions); [California Dental Association](https://www.cda.org/newsroom/legislation/more-advocacy-wins-new-cda-sponsored-law-protects-against-private-equity-interference/)). A second 2025 law requires private equity groups and management companies to notify the state's Office of Health Care Affordability of certain health care deals; how far it reaches dental groups depends on the office's rules ([Goodwin](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/10/alerts-privateequity-hltc-california-governor-signs-ab-1415)).

## Workers

Hygienists are hard to hire. In the third quarter of 2025, 74% of dentists who were recruiting a hygienist called it extremely challenging ([ADA Health Policy Institute](https://www.ada.org/-/media/project/ada-organization/ada/ada-org/files/resources/research/hpi/q32025_economic_outlook_dentistry_main.pdf)). The BLS puts hygienists' median pay at $98,100 and dental assistants' at $48,070 ([BLS, hygienists](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/dental-hygienists.htm); [BLS, assistants](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/healthcare/dental-assistants.htm)). We found no rigorous public comparison of pay at group and independent practices.

## Where the analogy breaks

Diners can see the menu and the prices before they order and can judge the food for themselves. Dental patients usually can't judge for themselves whether a crown or an implant is needed, and may not know whether their dentist's office is run by a company or who owns it; California's settlement with Aspen requires it to name practice owners in its ads.

## What people argue about

**Access.** The Association of Dental Support Organizations says its members create jobs, lower costs and improve access ([ADSO](https://www.theadso.org/advocacy)), and the ADA's joint report with the group points to their higher Medicaid participation and economies of scale ([ADA](https://www.ada.org/about/press-releases/leading-dental-associations-call-for-oral-health-care-reform-to-improve-medicaid-access)). Aspen and ClearChoice told KFF Health News that their owners "do not have influence or control over treatment recommendations" ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/health-care-costs/dental-implants-investigation-failures-unnecessary-healthy-teeth/)).

**Treatment.** The Private Equity Stakeholder Project, an advocacy group critical of private equity, counted 161 private equity dental deals in 2024, the most of any health care field it tracked, and says the industry "appears to have been created, largely by private equity firms, to avoid regulation" that bars investors from owning dental practices ([Private Equity Stakeholder Project](https://pestakeholder.org/reports/healthcare-deals-2024-in-review/)). The 2025 study cuts both ways: it found more restorative and surgical work after purchases, and no rise in the prices insurers paid or drop in Medicaid participation.

**Regulation.** California chose to limit what investors can control rather than ban their deals, with the dental association's support. In North Carolina, a 2025 Senate proposal to loosen the state's ban on non-dentist ownership stalled in the state House ([KFF Health News](https://kffhealthnews.org/morning-breakout/private-equity-in-dental-care-hits-snag-in-nc-similar-reforms-in-ct-are-a-dud/)).
