## At a glance

- **Who:** grades 10–12 personal finance or economics; adult education and first-time buyer classes.
- **Time:** 45 minutes (hook 5, chart 10, activity 20, discussion 10).
- **Standard:** Council for Economic Education, [National Content Standards in K–12 Economics, 3rd ed. (2025)](https://www.councilforeconed.org/wp-content/uploads/National-Content-Standards-in-K–12-Economics-3rd-Edition.pdf), Standard 14: Banks, Interest Rates, and Financial Markets, and Standard 17: Monetary Policy.
- **Pages used:** the [30-year mortgage rate chart](../chart.html?id=mortgage), [What the Mortgage Rate Is, and Why the Fed Can't Lower It](what-is-the-mortgage-rate.html) and [What a Rate Change Does to a Payment](/tools/rate-change-payment.html).
- **Materials:** one device per pair, and the [student handout](lesson-mortgage-rates-handout.html), printed.
- **Students leave able to:** work out a monthly payment with the tool; say what one point does to it; explain why the mortgage rate follows the 10-year Treasury yield more than the Fed; explain why low-rate owners hesitate to sell.

## Opening hook (5 minutes)

Two buyers, the same $400,000 house, 20% down, a 30-year fixed loan.

- Buyer A locked a rate of 3%.
- Buyer B locked 7%.

Ask: "How much more a month does Buyer B pay?" Take guesses; they usually run low. The answer, from the tool: about $780 a month more, $1,349 against $2,129, and about $280,000 more in interest over the loan.

## Reading the chart together (10 minutes)

Project the [30-year mortgage rate chart](../chart.html?id=mortgage).

1. **What is it?** Freddie Mac's weekly average rate for a 30-year fixed loan, built from mortgage applications lenders send it, for borrowers with good credit and 20% down. The rate was {{v mortgage fmt=pct2}} in {{v mortgage period}}, against {{v mortgage.year_ago fmt=pct2}} a year earlier.
2. **Compared with what?** The 2015–19 average was {{v mortgage.baseline fmt=pct2}}. Click **All**: the record low was {{v mortgage.min fmt=pct2}} ({{v mortgage.min period}}) and the record high {{v mortgage.max fmt=pct2}} ({{v mortgage.max period}}).
3. **What moves it?** Under Companion series, tick **Mortgage rate minus 10-year yield**. A second line shows the gap between the mortgage rate and what the government pays to borrow for ten years. The gap moves, but much less than the rate itself: the mortgage rate rides on the 10-year yield.
4. **Why not the Fed?** Read the late-2024 example from [What the Mortgage Rate Is](what-is-the-mortgage-rate.html): the Fed cut its rate by a full point, the 10-year yield rose by about a point, and mortgage rates went up.

## Activity: What a point is worth (20 minutes)

In pairs, using [What a Rate Change Does to a Payment](/tools/rate-change-payment.html). Every case: a $400,000 home, $80,000 down (20%), 30 years.

1. **The ladder.** Fill in the monthly payment and the total interest at 3%, 5% and 7%.
2. **One point.** At 7%, what does a point lower do to the payment? A point higher?
3. **Same payment, smaller loan.** At 5%, read the tool's "the same payment would carry" line: how big a loan does that payment pay off one point higher, at 6%? How much less can the buyer borrow?
4. **Today.** Enter this week's rate from the chart. How does today's payment compare with the 2015–19 average rate's?

**Exit ticket:** "A friend says rates will drop because the Fed is cutting. What would you tell them?"

## Discussion questions (10 minutes)

1. *Reading it.* Why is Freddie Mac's rate an average for a borrower with good credit and 20% down? Who pays more than it, and why?
2. In one sentence: what does the mortgage rate follow, if not the Fed?
3. *Placing it.* Compare this week's rate with its whole history since 1971, then with 2015–19. Which comparison do buyers feel, and why?
4. Millions of owners have mortgages below 4%. Why might they stay put instead of moving to a house that suits them better? {{check mortgage.value >= 5 "today's rate well above the sub-4% mortgages owners hold"}}
5. *Judging it.* Some argue the government should help buyers with lower rates, so more of them qualify; others argue that would push prices up. Using the activity, make each case.
6. Is a 15-year or a 30-year loan the better deal? What does each cost, and what does each give you?

## Answer key

**Ladder (tool, $320,000 loan, 30 years):**

**Activity:**

- **Step 2.** At 6%, $1,919, about $210 less. At 8%, $2,348, about $219 more. One point is worth roughly $200 a month on this loan.
- **Step 3.** The $1,718 payment carries about $286,500 at 6%, against $320,000 at 5%: about $33,500 less borrowing power from one point. The same payment buys a smaller house.
- **Step 4.** Depends on the week; the tile under the chart gives the same comparison for the median home.

Questions:

1. The average is for borrowers with good credit and 20% down. Lenders charge more for more risk: lower credit scores, smaller down payments, unusual properties. The average is a benchmark, not anyone's quote.
2. The 10-year Treasury yield, plus a gap that widens when markets are jumpy.
3. Buyers compare with what friends and parents paid recently, so 2015–19 is the reference they feel, not 1981.
4. Selling means giving up a low rate for today's, so the same payment buys less house (activity step 3). [You Own With a Fixed Mortgage](you-own-with-a-fixed-mortgage.html) covers this.
5. For: at a lower rate the same payment carries a bigger loan, so more buyers qualify and owners locked into low rates may finally sell. Against: if more buyers can bid more for the same houses, prices can rise and eat the savings. Which wins depends on how many houses come for sale; [What the Mortgage Rate Is](what-is-the-mortgage-rate.html) gives both arguments.
6. Neither for everyone: a 15-year loan charges a lower rate and far less interest, but a higher payment. The 30-year keeps the payment lower and leaves room in the budget.
