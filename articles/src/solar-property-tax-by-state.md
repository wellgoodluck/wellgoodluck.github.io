## The short version

Property tax follows value, and solar panels add value: in a large Berkeley Lab study of home sales, buyers paid about $4 a watt more for homes with panels the owner owned, about $15,000 for a typical system of the time. Most states exempt at least some of that added value or let local governments do so: DSIRE, the state-incentives database, counted 36 such states in 2020. They do it in different ways. Some exempt it for as long as the system is in use. Some exempt it for a fixed number of years, 15 in New York and 20 in Massachusetts. Some exempt it only until the house is sold. In some, towns or school districts can opt out or opt in. A few, Washington among them, offer no break, so the panels can be counted like a new kitchen. Several of the breaks have end dates. California's, in place since 1980, stops covering new systems on January 1, 2027. Because property tax is set where the house sits, someone with homes in two states lives under two sets of rules.

## The analogy

An appraiser with instructions. When an assessor values a house, the job is to estimate what a buyer would pay, and that means counting what's been added: a remodeled kitchen, a pool, a bedroom. A solar exemption is an instruction to look past the panels. Depending on the state, the instruction says to look past them for good, for a set number of years, or until the house changes hands, or it leaves part of the decision to the town. In a state with no instruction, the panels are priced like the kitchen.

Laws below are as of mid-November 2026.

## How property tax treats what you add

A property tax bill is the assessed value times the tax rate. In most states assessors aim to keep assessed values near market value and revalue on a regular cycle, so anything that raises what a house would sell for can raise its assessment at the next revaluation. California is the best-known exception: a home is assessed at what it last sold for, plus no more than 2% a year, and the value of anything newly built is added on top. ([Property tax limits by state](property-tax-limits-by-state.html) covers the different systems.)

Whether solar adds value turns on who owns the panels. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory studied nearly 23,000 home sales in eight states from 2002 to 2013 and found buyers paid premiums averaging about $4 a watt for systems the homeowner owned, roughly $15,000 for the average 3.6-kilowatt system in the sample ([Berkeley Lab, Selling into the Sun](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1172644)). The same study found the premium appeared to shrink quickly as systems aged ([Berkeley Lab](https://newscenter.lbl.gov/?p=33444)). A later Berkeley Lab study of California sales found that homes with leased systems sold for about the same as comparable homes without solar, neither more nor less ([Berkeley Lab](https://emp.lbl.gov/news/berkeley-lab-report-says-solar-homes)). Systems are bigger and cheaper now than in those samples, so the dollar figures shouldn't be carried forward.

For scale, here's the arithmetic for a system that adds $25,000 to an assessment: about $250 a year at a combined tax rate of 1%, and about $500 a year at 2%. Combined rates vary widely by state and by address. {{due 2027-01-15 "California: any 2027 bill to restore the section 73 exclusion; any BOE letter on new homes or additions after Jan 1, 2027"}} {{due 2029-03-01 "Oregon ORS 307.175 sunset (tax years after July 1, 2029); New York RPTL 487 construction deadline (Jan 1, 2030): extended or not"}}

## Four ways states handle it, and none

The details differ in nearly every state, but the approaches fall into a few groups.

**Exempt for as long as the system is in use.** Arizona's law says solar devices designed mainly to produce power for use on site are considered to add no value to the property ([A.R.S. §42-11054](https://www.azleg.gov/ars/42/11054.htm)). Texas exempts the appraised value that comes from installing a solar device used mainly on site, if the owner files an application with the county appraisal district ([Texas Comptroller, Form 50-123](https://comptroller.texas.gov/forms/50-123.pdf)). Maryland makes solar equipment not subject to real property tax ([Md. Tax-Property §7-242](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/Statute_Web/gtp/7-242.pdf)). Virginia has exempted home and farm systems up to 25 kilowatts since 2023 ([Va. Code §58.1-3661](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title58.1/chapter36/section58.1-3661/)), and Vermont exempts systems under 50 kilowatts that are net-metered or off the grid ([32 V.S.A. §3802](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/32/125/03802)). Florida, Nevada, Colorado, Minnesota and New Jersey have their own versions, and Illinois lets an owner ask to have the house valued as if it had ordinary heating and cooling instead of the solar system, whichever is lower (see the note under the table below).

**Exempt until the house sells.** California, for systems finished before 2027, and Michigan. In California, the sale price, panels included, becomes the new owner's starting point; in Michigan, the panels count at the first assessment after a sale.

**Exempt for a set number of years.** Massachusetts exempts qualifying systems, owned or leased, for 20 years ([G.L. c.59 §5, clause 45](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleIX/Chapter59/Section5)). New York exempts the added value for 15 years ([N.Y. Real Property Tax Law §487](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPT/487)).

**Left partly to local governments.** New York's exemption applies unless a county, city, town, village or school district opts out by local law or resolution, and the state tax department keeps a list of those that have ([New York State Department of Taxation and Finance](https://www.tax.ny.gov/research/property/legal/localop/487opt.htm)). A jurisdiction can't opt out for large solar farms only and keep the break for homes; it's all or nothing ([NYSERDA Solar Guidebook](https://www.nyserda.ny.gov/-/media/Project/Nyserda/Files/Programs/NY-Sun/Solar-Guidebook.pdf)). Other states add local options on top of a statewide break: Maryland lets counties and cities offer a property tax credit for up to three years ([Md. Tax-Property §9-203](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/Statute_Web/gtp/9-203.pdf)), Virginia lets localities exempt systems larger than 25 kilowatts ([§58.1-3661](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title58.1/chapter36/section58.1-3661/)), and Vermont lets towns vote to exempt other systems whose power is used on the property ([32 V.S.A. §3845](https://legislature.vermont.gov/statutes/section/32/125/03845)).

**No break.** Washington taxes all property unless the law specifically exempts it ([Washington Department of Revenue](https://www.dor.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-02/LevyManual.pdf)). Its renewable energy break covers only facilities of 10 megawatts or more, and only the state's share of the tax ([RCW 84.36.680](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=84.36.680&pdf=true)); there is none for home systems.

Several breaks have end dates written in. California's stops covering new systems on January 1, 2027. New York's covers solar systems built before January 1, 2030 ([RPTL §487](https://www.nysenate.gov/legislation/laws/RPT/487)). Oregon's can't be claimed for tax years that begin after July 1, 2029 ([ORS chapter 307, 2025 edition](https://www.oregonlegislature.gov/bills_laws/ors/ors307.html)), a deadline lawmakers have pushed back before, from 2017 to 2023 and then to 2029 ([HB 2760, 2017](https://olis.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2017R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/HB2760/Enrolled); [SB 1519, 2022](https://apps.oregonlegislature.gov/liz/2022R1/Downloads/MeasureDocument/SB1519)).

## California's break ends for new systems

Since 1980, apart from a lapse in the late 1990s, California has kept active solar systems out of the assessment of new construction, so adding panels didn't raise a homeowner's property tax ([Board of Equalization, LTA 2024/031](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta24031.pdf)). Lawmakers extended the break several times. A 2022 law set the current end date: the exclusion doesn't cover systems finished on or after January 1, 2027 ([BOE, LTA 2024/031](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta24031.pdf)). A 2025 law kept that date, and the board has confirmed that systems finished earlier stay excluded ([Board of Equalization, LTA 2026/013](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta26013.pdf); [LTA 2026/034](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta26034.pdf); [BOE analysis of SB 710](https://boe.ca.gov/legdiv/pdf/0710sb090525ml.pdf)). A 2026 bill to extend it through 2030 for customer-sited systems of 10 kilowatts or less, such as most home systems, and for systems on public agencies' property was held in May in the Assembly Appropriations Committee, which weighs a bill's cost ([AB 2389](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB2389)). It never left the committee, and under the state constitution an ordinary bill can't pass after August 31 of an even-numbered year, so it can't become law this session {{due 2027-01-04 "California: the exclusion stopped covering new systems Jan 1, 2027; change the dek, short version, Four ways and this section from stops/ends to stopped/ended, and check for any 2027 bill to restore it"}} ([AB 2389 history](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billHistoryClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB2389); [California Constitution, art. IV, §10](https://law.justia.com/constitution/california/article-iv/section-10)).

Systems that qualify before the date stay excluded until the property changes ownership ([Revenue and Taxation Code §73](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/revenue-and-taxation-code/rtc-sect-73/); [Board of Equalization, LTA 2026/034](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta26034.pdf)). The date that counts is when the system is ready for use, and assessors consider the date of the final inspection by the city or county ([Property Tax Rule 463](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/rules/Rule463.pdf)). A system still being installed on January 1, 2027 generally won't qualify, except for any part already in place on January 1, 2026 ([BOE, LTA 2024/031](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta24031.pdf)). After that, the assessor can add a new system's value to the assessment, and like the rest of the assessment it can rise up to 2% a year.

The change reaches beyond retrofits. Since 2020, California's building code has required solar on most new single-family homes and low-rise apartment buildings, and since 2023 on taller apartment buildings too ([California Energy Commission, 2019 standards FAQ](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2020-03/Title_24_2019_Building_Standards_FAQ_ada.pdf); [solar fact sheet](https://www.energy.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2020-11/2020%20-%20CEC%20-%20Solar%20PV%20Systems_ADA.pdf); [2022 code](https://www.energy.ca.gov/programs-and-topics/programs/building-energy-efficiency-standards/energy-code-support-center/2022-3)). The Board of Equalization's staff say solar installed after January 1, 2027 would be assessed as new construction ([BOE analysis of SB 710](https://boe.ca.gov/legdiv/pdf/0710sb090525ml.pdf)), so new homes whose panels are finished in 2027 or later are likely to carry the panels' value in their assessments. Starting January 1, 2027, the first buyer of a new home whose panels were finished before 2027, bought from a builder that didn't claim the exclusion, must file a claim with the assessor within three years of buying to get the exclusion back to the purchase; a later claim gets it only from the year it's filed ([BOE, LTA 2026/034](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/lta26034.pdf)).

## Seventeen states, side by side

This is a sample weighted toward states where many people own a home, or a second one, rather than a full list. Many of these exemptions require an application, and the county assessor or appraisal district is the place to confirm how one applies to a particular property.

| State | How the value home solar adds is treated |
|---|---|
| Arizona | Solar devices for on-site use are considered to add no value. No time limit. |
| California | Excluded until a sale for systems finished before January 1, 2027. Systems finished later can be assessed. |
| Colorado | Exempt if the system is on a home and its power is used there. That covers panels the homeowner owns and, for systems up to 100 kilowatts, panels a solar company owns. |
| Florida | For homes, the value a renewable energy device adds can't be counted, for devices installed since January 1, 2013. |
| Illinois | The owner files Form PTAX-330 with the county assessment office, and the house is valued as if it had ordinary heating and cooling instead of solar, whichever is lower, for as long as the system is in use. |
| Maryland | Solar equipment isn't subject to real property tax. Counties and cities may also offer credits of up to three years. |
| Massachusetts | Owned or leased systems sized to the property (no more than 125% of its electricity use, or 25 kilowatts or less) are exempt for 20 years. |
| Michigan | Since 2019, installing a home system counts as normal maintenance, so its value isn't counted until the house is sold, whoever owns the system. Systems up to 150 kilowatts that make no more power than the home uses in a year. |
| Minnesota | The panels are exempt as personal property, and the home is classified without regard to them. |
| Nevada | A qualifying system that serves a building is exempt. No time limit in the statute. |
| New Jersey | Exempt once certified: the owner applies to the local construction office, which certifies the system, then to the assessor. The exemption is the difference between the assessed value with and without the system. |
| New York | The added value is exempt for 15 years for systems built before 2030, unless the local government or school district has opted out. Apply to the assessor by the local taxable status date, March 1 in most places. |
| Oregon | The value the system adds is exempt, through the tax year that begins July 1, 2029, unless lawmakers extend it again. |
| Texas | The added value is exempt for a device used mainly on site, whether the homeowner or a solar company owns it. Apply to the county appraisal district between January 1 and April 30. |
| Vermont | Systems under 50 kilowatts that are net-metered or off the grid are exempt. Towns may vote to exempt other systems used on site. |
| Virginia | Home and farm systems up to 25 kilowatts are exempt (since 2023). Localities may exempt larger ones. |
| Washington | No exemption for home systems. |

About Illinois: its law dates from the 1970s and is worded around solar heating and cooling. The assessor values the house two ways, once as if it had ordinary heating and cooling equipment and once with the solar system, and uses the lower figure. The state's definition of a solar energy system now also covers panels that make electricity, including leased ones, so for a rooftop system the panels don't raise the assessment once the owner files Form PTAX-330. An owner who stops using the system must tell the assessor in writing, by certified mail, within 30 days ([35 ILCS 200/10-10](https://ilga.gov/Documents/legislation/ilcs/documents/003502000K10-10.htm); [10-5](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/fulltext.asp?DocName=003502000K10-5); [Madison County assessment office](https://www.madisoncountyil.gov/departments/chief_county_assesment_office/available_exemptions.php)).

## If you own homes in two states

Property tax belongs to the place where the house sits, so each home follows its own state's rules, whatever state you call home. Panels on a house in California finished in 2026 and panels on a house in Washington are treated differently even if they were installed the same week by the same owner.

A few practical differences follow from the table:

- **Applications and deadlines.** Several breaks have to be claimed. New York requires an application to the local assessor by the taxable status date, March 1 in most places ([NYSERDA](https://www.nyserda.ny.gov/-/media/Project/Nyserda/Files/Programs/NY-Sun/NYS-Real-Property-Tax-Law-487-and-Solar-PILOT.pdf)). Texas requires an application to the appraisal district in the county where the property sits, between January 1 and April 30 ([Form 50-123](https://comptroller.texas.gov/forms/50-123.pdf)). Illinois requires a filing (Form PTAX-330) with the county assessment office, and New Jersey a certificate from the local construction office first. A deadline in one state can pass while the owner is in the other.
- **Clocks that run out.** Massachusetts' exemption lasts 20 years, so a buyer of a house with an older system should ask the assessor how many years are left.
- **Breaks that end at a sale.** In California, buying a house with solar means the panels are part of the price the next assessment starts from. In Michigan, the panels count at the first assessment after the sale.
- **Rules that differ within a state.** In New York two neighboring towns can treat the same system differently, because one may have opted out.

## Leased panels and other owners

When a solar company owns the panels and leases them to the homeowner or sells the power, the question becomes whether a break written for homeowners covers equipment the homeowner doesn't own. Many states have said yes. Colorado covers company-owned systems up to 100 kilowatts on a home as well as those the homeowner owns ([Colorado Division of Property Taxation](https://dpt.colorado.gov/renewable-energy); [C.R.S. 39-3-102](https://colorado.public.law/statutes/crs_39-3-102)). Texas changed its law in 2021 so the owner of the device can claim the exemption even when someone else owns the house ([Tex. Tax Code §11.27](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/tax-code/title-1/subtitle-c/chapter-11/subchapter-b/section-11-27/); [Form 50-123](https://comptroller.texas.gov/forms/50-123.pdf)). Massachusetts and Illinois write leased systems in by name ([clause 45](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleIX/Chapter59/Section5); [35 ILCS 200/10-5](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/fulltext.asp?DocName=003502000K10-5)), Michigan covers systems "without regard to ownership" ([MCL 211.27](https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-211-27)), and California's exclusion doesn't depend on who owns the panels ([Board of Equalization](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/active-solar-energy-system.htm); [BOE analysis of SB 710](https://boe.ca.gov/legdiv/pdf/0710sb090525ml.pdf)).

Arizona fought it out in court. Its revenue department set out to value leased rooftop panels for the 2015 tax year, and the Arizona Supreme Court ruled in 2018 that the department had no authority to value them. It sent three questions back to the tax court, among them whether the state's no-value rule covers leased panels ([SolarCity Corp. v. Arizona Department of Revenue](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/2018/cv-17-0231-pr.html)).

## Where the analogy breaks

The instruction to look past the panels applies to the tax, not the market. A buyer still pays for them. In California, where the break lasts only until a sale, the price paid, panels included, becomes the starting point for the next owner's taxes, and Michigan counts the panels at the first assessment after a sale.

And an exemption doesn't make the cost disappear. In places where local governments set the total amount to be raised and the rate is worked out from it, exempting one owner's value moves a little of the bill onto everyone else. Where the rate is capped, as in California, the local government collects less instead. [How a property tax rate is set](how-a-property-tax-rate-is-set.html) explains the difference.

## What people argue about

**Whether solar should be taxed like any other improvement.** Illinois' property tax code states the case for a break: solar should be encouraged, and its value judged in light of its public benefits ([35 ILCS 200/10-5](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/fulltext.asp?DocName=003502000K10-5)). Berkeley Lab's findings suggest the added value is hard to pin down: it depends on who owns the panels and seems to fade as they age. The author of California's 2026 extension bill argued that homeowners aren't reassessed for other upgrades like new windows or heat pumps ([Assembly Revenue and Taxation Committee](https://arev.assembly.ca.gov/media/1036)). On the other side, the committee's staff questioned that comparison and noted that new homes must have solar anyway under the building code ([Assembly Revenue and Taxation Committee](https://arev.assembly.ca.gov/media/1036)). Arizona's revenue department argued in the SolarCity case that valuing the panels at zero would violate the state constitution's requirement that property be taxed uniformly ([SolarCity Corp. v. Arizona Department of Revenue](https://law.justia.com/cases/arizona/supreme-court/2018/cv-17-0231-pr.html)). And Minnesota's revenue department, describing exemptions in general, notes that when property comes off the tax rolls, other taxpayers pay a higher share ([Minnesota Department of Revenue](https://www.revenue.state.mn.us/sites/default/files/2026-07/module-5-exempt-property.pdf)). Local governments don't all line up on one side: the Massachusetts Municipal Association backed keeping that state's exemption for systems sized to the property, while warning in 2022 that unexpected solar tax abatements could shift tax onto homeowners and businesses ([Massachusetts Municipal Association](https://www.mma.org/advocacy/mma-asks-revenue-committee-to-support-bills-related-to-property-taxation-for-solar-and-nonprofits/)). New York built the argument into its law by letting each jurisdiction choose. NYSERDA, the state energy agency, explains how the opt-out works and makes the case for keeping the break ([NYSERDA Solar Guidebook](https://www.nyserda.ny.gov/-/media/Project/Nyserda/Files/Programs/NY-Sun/Solar-Guidebook.pdf)), and the state tax department lists the places that have opted out ([opt-out list](https://www.tax.ny.gov/research/property/legal/localop/487opt.htm)).

**Who pays for the break.** Local governments collect property tax, but the exemptions are mostly set by state law. California's 2026 extension bill said the state wouldn't reimburse local governments for the property tax they'd forgo ([AB 2389](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB2389)). The same divide shows up in the fight over net metering: critics argue the benefit goes to households with panels and part of the cost to those without, and supporters dispute how large that shift is ([Who pays when your neighbor goes solar?](solar-net-metering.html)).

**Whether the breaks should have end dates.** Lawmakers in California, New York and Oregon wrote expiration dates into their exemptions and have extended them before. California law requires new tax breaks to state their goals and how success will be measured, and the Assembly's tax committee won't advance one without an end date, because repealing a tax break usually takes a two-thirds vote ([Assembly Revenue and Taxation Committee](https://arev.assembly.ca.gov/media/1036)).
