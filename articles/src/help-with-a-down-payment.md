## In one sentence

Family money can reach a home purchase as a gift, as a loan or as a signature on the mortgage, and each one is documented, taxed and risked in its own way.

## The analogy

Three ways to help someone over a wall. Give them a boost, and you are done once they are over. Lend them a ladder, and you expect it back. Or tie a rope between you and climb together, and if they slip, you hold the weight.

The lender on the other side of the wall wants to know which one it was. Figures and rules below are as of November 2026. This page describes how the rules work. It isn't tax or legal advice.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

First-time buyers put down a median of 9% in the year to mid-2026. Repeat buyers, many of them bringing equity from the house they sold, put down 19% ([NAR](https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/first-time-home-buyer-share-inches-up-to-22-median-age-remains-at-record-high-of-40)). On a $400,000 house, 9% is $36,000.

Asked where that money came from, 25% of first-time buyers named a gift or loan from relatives or friends. They could name more than one source: 62% used savings, 30% used stocks, retirement accounts or other financial assets, and 10% used an inheritance, the highest share on record (same NAR release).

A Federal Reserve economist, using the Fed's own household survey, found that about 30% of first-time buyers from 2009 to 2016 had a gift or loan from family or friends toward the down payment ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2025094pap.pdf)).

## A gift

**What the lender wants.** Fannie Mae, which buys mortgages from lenders and sets the rules for the ones it buys, accepts a gift from a relative, a fiancé, a domestic partner or someone with a long family-like tie to the buyer. It does not accept one from the builder, the developer, the real estate agent or anyone else with a stake in the sale; a seller who is also a relative is the exception. The giver signs a letter stating the amount, the relationship and that no repayment is expected, and the lender verifies that the giver has the money and documents its transfer. On a one-unit home the buyer will live in, the whole down payment can be a gift. Gifts are not allowed on an investment property ([Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-04/personal-gifts)).

**What the IRS wants.** Usually a form, not money. In 2026 a person can give any one recipient $19,000 with nothing to report. Two spouses can each give that much, $38,000 in all. Any tax is generally the giver's responsibility, not the receiver's ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/frequently-asked-questions-on-gift-taxes)). A giver who goes over the line files a gift tax return by April 15 of the next year, and the excess is subtracted from a lifetime allowance that stands at $15 million per person in 2026 ([Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/10/irs-announces-increased-gift-and-estate-tax-exemption-amounts-for-2026)).

So a parent who gives a child $40,000 toward a house files a gift tax return, and $21,000 of the gift, by our arithmetic, comes off the lifetime allowance. No gift tax is owed unless a lifetime of such gifts has passed $15 million.

## A loan

**What the lender wants.** To know it is a loan. Fannie Mae does not accept an unsecured personal loan as a source of the down payment ([Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-4.3-17/personal-unsecured-loans)), and a gift letter has to say no repayment is expected. Money that will be paid back cannot be described as a gift. Some programs have a place for it. On a loan insured by the Federal Housing Administration, the law sets the buyer's minimum cash investment at 3.5% and, within limits, counts money borrowed from a family member toward it ([12 U.S.C. 1709](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/12/1709)). The FHA lists family members among the acceptable sources of secondary financing, a second loan recorded alongside the first ([HUD](https://entp.hud.gov/wink/files/sf_basics.html)).

**What the IRS wants.** Interest, or something like it. Each month the IRS publishes minimum rates for private loans. For November 2026 they were 4.67% for loans of three years or less, 5.08% for loans of more than three and up to nine years, and 5.52% for longer ones ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rr-26-21.pdf)). When a family loan charges less, the law treats the missing interest as if the lender had given it to the borrower and the borrower had paid it back, which can mean taxable interest income for a lender who never received any. The rule does not apply while loans between the two people total $10,000 or less, unless the money buys income-producing assets. Up to $100,000, the interest the lender is treated as receiving is generally capped at the borrower's investment income for the year, and counts as zero if that income is $1,000 or less ([26 U.S.C. 7872](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7872); the loan lengths are set in [section 1274](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1274)).

At 5.08%, a nine-year family loan of $40,000 paid monthly would cost about $462 a month and about $9,900 in interest over its life, by our arithmetic.

## A signature

A relative can also lend their income and credit. Under Fannie Mae's rules a co-signer or guarantor signs the mortgage note and is liable for it along with the buyer, without owning any of the house. A borrower who won't live there may or may not be on the title. Neither can be the seller, the builder or the agent. When the signer won't live in the house and the loan runs through Fannie Mae's automated system, the loan can cover no more than 95% of the home's value, which means at least 5% down. Loans underwritten by hand are capped at 90% ([Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-2-04/guarantors-co-signers-or-non-occupant-borrowers-subject-transaction)).

The debt follows the signer. When the co-signer later applies for a loan, the lender can leave the co-signed mortgage out of the count only with 12 months of statements showing the buyer made every payment on time, and the house still counts toward the number of financed properties the co-signer holds ([Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-6-05/monthly-debt-obligations)). [Signing for someone else's loan](co-signing.html) covers what co-signing means on any debt.

## How to read the numbers

**"A gift or loan" is one box on a survey.** The 25% lumps together money that is never coming back and money that is, from parents, other relatives and friends. The Federal Reserve figure asks much the same question of buyers in earlier years. Neither says how large the help was.

**"Gift tax" rarely means a tax.** The $19,000 line is where reporting starts, not where giving stops. Tax is due only after the $15 million lifetime allowance is used up.

**A median of 9% means half put down less.** The FHA's minimum, set in law, is 3.5%.

## Where the analogy breaks

A boost is over when feet touch the ground. Money leaves a trail: the lender documents a gift, the IRS may ask the giver for a form, and a co-signature lasts until the loan is paid off, refinanced or the house is sold. And a gift can't quietly become a loan afterward. A gift letter that says no repayment is expected is a statement to the lender.

## What people argue about

**Whether policy should lower the down payment hurdle.** One side: the authors of an Urban Institute brief argue that for many would-be buyers with good credit the down payment is the main thing in the way. In 2024 they proposed an FHA mortgage with no down payment, with a higher insurance premium, a cap on how many such loans are made and required counseling, and said it would push prices up less than cash grants would ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/urban-institute-zero-down-fha-mortgages-first-time-homeowners/)). The other side: Edward Pinto of the American Enterprise Institute argued in 2021 that government down payment assistance brings more buyers to a short supply of houses and pushes prices up ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/podcast/ed-pinto-on-alternatives-to-down-payment-assistance/)), and Howard Husock of AEI wrote in 2024 that low-down-payment FHA loans have historically had higher default rates ([AEI](https://www.aei.org/op-eds/the-harris-campaigns-foolish-down-payment-assistance-scheme/)). A Federal Reserve economist's model cuts across both: it estimates that parents' transfers account for 13 percentage points, or 27%, of the homeownership rate among households aged 25 to 44, and that lowering the barriers to buying generally makes parents' wealth matter more, not less ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2025094pap.pdf)).

**Whether a co-signature is help or a hazard.** One side: a parent's income on the application can be what gets a loan approved, and the rules above allow it. The other: the signer owes the whole debt if the buyer stops paying, and the mortgage counts against the signer when the signer applies for a loan of their own. The site's [co-signing page](co-signing.html) sets out both cases, with sources.
