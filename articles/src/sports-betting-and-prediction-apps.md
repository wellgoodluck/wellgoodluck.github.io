## Short version

Sports betting apps and prediction markets let you bet on games from your phone in a few taps. Every bet is priced so that, on average, the app keeps a slice: about 5 cents of every dollar on a simple bet, and much more on parlays that string several picks together. That slice is small on one bet and large over a season. Researchers have linked legal online betting to more bankruptcies and less saving. Prediction apps, which say they are financial markets rather than sportsbooks, mostly accept customers at 18 in states where the sportsbook age is 21, and the courts are split over whether states can stop them.

## The trap in one sentence

You can win on a given night, but the price of every bet is set so that the more you bet, the more surely you lose.

## How it works

**The price is built in.** On a typical bet on one game, the odds are listed as −110: you risk $110 to win $100. If you pick winners exactly half the time, you lose about $4.55 for every $100 you risk. That gap is the sportsbook's edge, and it's there on every bet before the game starts.

**Parlays multiply the edge.** A parlay combines several picks into one bet that pays only if every pick wins. The payout looks huge, but the edge compounds with each pick added. On a four-pick parlay at standard odds, you lose about 17 cents of every dollar on average. Parlays are sportsbooks' most profitable bets per dollar: in Illinois in January 2026, the two biggest apps kept about 20% of parlay money, compared with about 9% of all bets.

**Bonus bets come with strings.** Sign-up offers like "Bet $5, get $200 in bonus bets" usually pay out only the winnings, not the bonus itself, and often must be used within days.

**The app is always there.** Betting used to mean a trip to a casino or a call to a bookie. Now it's on the same phone as your bank, at any hour, with live bets that settle every few minutes during a game.

**Prediction apps charge you differently, with the same result.** Kalshi, Polymarket and similar apps sell "event contracts" that pay $1 if something happens, including the outcome of a game. They are regulated by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC), a federal agency, rather than by state gaming boards, and most, including Kalshi, accept customers at 18. You trade against other users, and instead of building a price that favors the house, the app charges a fee on each trade, about 3.5 cents per dollar on a 50/50 contract on Kalshi, plus the gap between buy and sell prices. A CNN analysis estimated that Kalshi users under 21 traded about $5.4 billion in contracts in 2026 through late August, about $3.9 billion of it on sports. That is the total value of their trades, not what they lost.

## A worked example

You bet $20 a day for a year, $7,300 in all, and you're an average bettor: you pick winners half the time.

## How to avoid it

**Treat it as entertainment with a price.** Decide what a month of betting is worth to you, the way you would a concert or a streaming service, and stop there. Money you need for rent or bills isn't betting money.

**Skip the parlays.** If you bet, single bets at standard odds cost the least. Parlays and "same-game parlays" cost several times as much on average.

**Use the limits in the app.** Licensed sportsbooks let you set deposit, time and spending limits, and you can exclude yourself for a set period. Many states run self-exclusion lists that cover every licensed sportsbook in the state.

**Read the bonus terms.** Look for how many times you must bet the bonus, whether the bonus itself is paid out, and when it expires.

**Never bet with borrowed money.** Most big sportsbooks and at least eight states no longer allow credit-card deposits, but some apps, including some prediction markets, still take them. A losing bet on a card can turn into debt at 22% interest (see [how card interest works](credit-card-minimum-payments.html)).

**Know the signs.** Chasing losses, betting more to feel the same excitement, hiding it, or borrowing to bet are warning signs. Free, confidential help is available 24 hours a day by call or text at 1-800-MY-RESET (1-800-697-3738), the national helpline run by the National Council on Problem Gambling.

## The bigger picture

In 2018 the Supreme Court struck down a federal law that had kept most states from allowing sports betting. As of November 2026, 39 states and Washington, D.C., allowed it, and about 30 of them plus D.C. allowed betting on phone apps. Most set the betting age at 21.

**The research.** A study by researchers at UCLA, USC and Harvard, using credit records for about 4.4 million people, found that in states that allowed online betting, bankruptcies became as much as 25% to 30% more likely, with smaller increases in debt collections and late payments on cards and car loans. A study of about 184,000 households published in the *Journal of Financial Economics* found that after online betting became legal, households put less money into investment accounts, and that households with little savings were hit hardest, adding credit-card debt. A 2026 study for the Massachusetts Gaming Commission, based on interviews with 62 young adults, found that many saw betting as low-risk fun and thought of themselves and their friends as largely immune to its risks.

**The prediction-market fight.** Prediction apps say their sports contracts are federally regulated financial products, so state gambling laws, including age limits and taxes, don't apply. States say they are sports bets by another name. Since April 2026 the CFTC has sued 10 states, starting with Arizona, Connecticut and Illinois, to stop them from enforcing their gambling laws against the apps. In July, 44 state attorneys general told the CFTC that its proposed rules for these markets went beyond its legal authority. Federal appeals courts have split. The Third Circuit sided with Kalshi in April 2026, and the Ninth and Sixth Circuits sided with Nevada, Ohio and Tennessee in August and September. In September, New Jersey asked the Supreme Court to settle the question, with 39 other states and D.C. supporting it. As of November 9 the Court had not decided whether to hear the case; Kalshi's response is due December 7.

**Supporters of legal, regulated betting** argue that people were already betting with offshore sites and bookies, and that legal apps bring consumer protections, limits, identity checks and tax revenue: states collected about $3.7 billion in 2025. They say most bettors wager small amounts for fun, and adults should be free to spend their money as they choose. Prediction-market companies add that their exchanges are open to anyone 18 or older, like a stock exchange; that the exchange doesn't win when you lose, because it earns fees whichever side wins; that one federal rulebook is better than 50 state ones; and that Kalshi added a $2,500 monthly deposit limit for users under 21 in October 2026.

**Critics** argue that phone-based betting is far more habit-forming than a trip to a casino, that the industry's marketing reaches young people constantly, and that the research on bankruptcies and savings shows real harm. Many want limits on advertising and on credit-card deposits, a ban on prop bets on college athletes, and an age of 21 for prediction apps. A federal bill, the SAFE Bet Act, would set national limits on betting ads, require checks on whether bettors can afford large losses, cap deposits at five a day and ban credit-card deposits. It was introduced in 2025 and has not had a vote. The sportsbook industry's trade group says it would override state regulators and push bettors toward illegal offshore sites.

These rules are changing quickly. This page reflects them as of November 9, 2026.
