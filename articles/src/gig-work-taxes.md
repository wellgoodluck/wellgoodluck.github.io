## Short version

When you work a regular job, your employer takes taxes out of every paycheck and pays half of your Social Security and Medicare tax. When you drive, deliver, tutor or freelance through an app, nobody does either. You owe self-employment tax of about 14% of your profit, plus any income tax, and it's all due at once unless you pay as you go. People who spend every payout get a surprise bill in April, sometimes with a penalty on top.

## The trap in one sentence

The app pays you the whole amount, so it feels like yours to spend, but part of it already belongs to the IRS.

## How it works

**You're running a small business.** Most gig apps treat workers as independent contractors, not employees. That means no tax is withheld, and you're responsible for figuring out and paying what you owe.

**Self-employment tax.** Employees and their employers each pay 7.65% of wages for Social Security and Medicare. When you work for yourself, you pay both halves: 15.3% on about 92% of your profit, which works out to about 14% of profit. It applies once your net self-employment earnings reach $400 (about $433 of profit), even if you owe no income tax at all.

**Profit, not payouts.** You owe tax on what you earn minus your business costs. For drivers, the biggest cost is usually the car: you can deduct the IRS mileage rate (72.5 cents a mile for miles driven January through June 2026 and 76 cents for miles driven from July 1) for miles driven for work, if you keep a log. To use these rates, you generally have to choose them in the first year you use the car for work. Phone costs, delivery bags and fees the app charges you may count too.

**Paying as you go.** If you expect to owe $1,000 or more for the year, the IRS expects you to pay in four installments, due around April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15. Skip them and you can owe a penalty that works like interest; the rate is 7% a year for October through December 2026. If you owed no federal tax last year, you won't owe the penalty this year, but you'll still owe the tax in April.

**The forms may not tell you.** Apps and payment platforms send a 1099 form when you earn above certain amounts: $2,000 or more from one company in 2026 for a 1099-NEC, and, for payment apps and online marketplaces, more than $20,000 and more than 200 payments for a 1099-K. (If customers pay you directly by card, the card processor sends a 1099-K no matter how little you made.) Below those amounts you may get no form at all, but the income is still taxable.

## A worked example

You deliver food in 2026. The app pays you $20,000, and you drive about 6,800 miles for work, which at the IRS rates comes to about $5,000. Your profit is $15,000.

## How to avoid it

**Set aside a share of every payout.** Move 20% to 25% of each payout into a separate savings account the day it arrives, more if you also have a regular job. When the tax comes due, the money is there.

**Track your miles from day one.** A mileage log, or an app that records trips, is the biggest deduction most drivers have. Without records, you can't claim it.

**Pay quarterly.** Once you expect to owe $1,000 or more, make estimated payments through IRS Direct Pay or your IRS online account. If you also have a regular job, you can instead ask your employer to withhold more by updating your W-4.

**Use the free help.** The IRS Volunteer Income Tax Assistance program (VITA) prepares returns for free for people who generally earn about $70,000 or less, and the IRS Gig Economy Tax Center explains the rules in plain language.

**Keep your own records.** Save your monthly earnings statements from each app, whether or not you get a 1099.

**Check the tips deduction.** Rideshare and app-based delivery drivers can deduct tips, up to $25,000 a year from 2025 through 2028, if the app reports them. That lowers income tax only; you still owe self-employment tax on your tips.

For a regular job, [Where Did the Rest of It Go?](your-first-paycheck.html) explains what does get withheld.

## The bigger picture

About one in four adults ages 18 to 29 (26%) earned money from gig activities in the month before a late-2024 Federal Reserve survey, counting everything from app-based driving to selling things online and renting out property.

**Who counts as an employee** is the fight underneath the tax bill. Employees get withholding, an employer-paid half of Social Security and Medicare tax, minimum wage, overtime, unemployment insurance and workers' compensation; independent contractors don't. The Labor Department, whose test decides who gets federal minimum wage and overtime, tightened that test in 2024, stopped using it in its own enforcement in 2025, and in February 2026 proposed returning to a 2021 test that makes it easier to treat workers as contractors. A final version cleared White House review in late October 2026. (The IRS uses its own test for taxes.) Some states draw the line on their own; California voters exempted app-based drivers from the state's employee test in 2020, in exchange for a minimum pay rate for time spent driving and a health-insurance stipend for drivers who work more than 15 hours a week.

**Supporters of contractor status** argue that it gives workers the flexibility to log on when they want and work for several apps at once, that most gig workers do it part-time, and that requiring employee status would force apps to set schedules, cut the number of workers, or raise prices.

**Supporters of employee status** argue that apps control pay, assignments and ratings the way an employer does, so they should share the payroll tax and provide basic protections, and that leaving workers to handle their own taxes shifts costs and risk onto the people least able to carry them.

**Reporting thresholds** are a smaller fight. In 2021 Congress lowered the reporting threshold for payment apps to $600, with no minimum number of payments. Backers said it would catch unreported income, and Congress's scorekeepers estimated it would raise about $8.4 billion over 10 years. After years of delays, the 2025 tax law put it back at $20,000 and 200 payments, and raised the threshold for 1099-NEC forms from $600 to $2,000 starting in 2026. Backers of the 2025 change said the $600 rule would have buried casual sellers and people splitting bills in paperwork; opponents said it makes it easier for income to go unreported. Either way, the income is taxable.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 9, 2026.
