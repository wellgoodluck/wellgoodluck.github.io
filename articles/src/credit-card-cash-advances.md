## Short version

Your credit card can usually get you cash from an ATM. That's a cash advance, and it's priced very differently from a purchase: an upfront fee of 3% to 5%, a higher interest rate, and no grace period, so interest starts the day you take the money. Some things that don't look like cash withdrawals are treated as cash advances too.

## The trap in one sentence

The card you use for free every day starts charging you the moment it hands you cash.

## How it works

When you buy something with a credit card and pay the statement in full, you pay no interest (see [how card interest works](credit-card-minimum-payments.html)). Cash advances skip all of that:

- **A fee up front.** [Typically 3% to 5% of the amount](https://www.chase.com/personal/credit-cards/education/basics/how-do-credit-card-cash-advances-work), with a minimum around $10. The fee is added to your balance immediately.
- **A higher rate.** Cash advance APRs commonly run near 30% at the big card companies. Curinos data for September 2026 [put the average cash advance rate offered on bank-issued cards around 28%](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/research/current-credit-card-interest-rate/), versus about 19% for purchases on the same cards.
- **No grace period.** [Interest starts accruing the day you take the cash](https://www.experian.com/blogs/ask-experian/what-is-a-cash-advance/), even if you pay your statement in full.
- **ATM fees on top.** The machine's owner may charge its own fee, usually a few dollars.

**Things that can count as cash advances:** using a credit card to send money to a friend through a payment app, buying money orders or casino chips, and using the "convenience checks" card companies mail you. Your card agreement lists what counts.

## A worked example

You take a $500 cash advance from a card with a 5% fee and a 29.99% cash advance APR, and you pay it back in 60 days.

## How to avoid it

**Keep your card's PIN out of your routine.** If you never use the card at an ATM, you won't take an advance by habit.

**Don't fund payment apps with a credit card.** Link them to your bank account or debit card instead.

**If you need cash, compare first.** A small loan from a credit union, a payment plan with whoever you owe, or even a purchase you can put on the card directly will almost always cost less.

**If you've already taken one, pay it off first.** Payments above the minimum [generally go to the highest-rate balance first](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1026.53), so paying extra this month attacks the advance.

**Toss the convenience checks.** They're cash advances with a nicer envelope.

## The bigger picture

Cash advances aren't a big policy fight; they're legal, disclosed in every card agreement, and priced this way on purpose. Card companies say they charge more because people who need cash from a credit card are, on average, more likely to be in financial trouble and less likely to repay, so the lender treats it as riskier lending.

The useful lesson is about reading the price tag. A credit card has several prices hiding in one piece of plastic: one for purchases, one for balance transfers, one for cash. They're all listed in the box on your card agreement, usually called the Schumer box, after Charles Schumer, who as a congressman [sponsored the 1988 law requiring it](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1026.60).
