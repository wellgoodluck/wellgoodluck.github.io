## The short version

In much of the country, the property tax rate is the last number decided, not the first. A local government decides how much it needs, the assessor decides what all the taxable property is worth, and the rate is roughly the one divided by the other. When values rise, the same rate brings in more money, so many states require local governments to calculate a lower rate, called the rollback rate, rolled-back rate or no-new-revenue rate, that would bring in about the same money from the same properties as last year. Adopting a rate above it counts as a tax increase in those states, even when the rate itself didn't change, and triggers notices, hearings or, in Texas, sometimes an election. The rollback rate holds the total steady, not any one bill: a home that gained value faster than the town's average still pays more, and so does any kind of property revalued in a year when the rest wasn't. [Part two](property-tax-limits-by-state.html) covers how the rules differ from state to state.

## The analogy

A property tax works like splitting a restaurant bill by how much each person ordered, except the table decides the size of the bill first. If everyone's order is suddenly marked up 8% and the bill stays the same, each person's share of it doesn't change and the "rate" per dollar ordered has to fall. The argument at budget time is about the size of the bill. The rate follows from it.

## The words

**Mill.** One-tenth of a cent. A rate of 10 mills is $10 of tax for every $1,000 of taxable value, the same as 1%. Many states state rates in mills; others use dollars per $100 of value (Texas) or percent.

**Assessed and taxable value.** The assessor estimates what each property would sell for. Some states then tax only a fraction of that: Georgia taxes 40% of market value ([Georgia Department of Revenue](https://dor.georgia.gov/property-tax-valuation)), so a $400,000 house has an assessed value of $160,000. Exemptions come off next, most commonly a homestead exemption for an owner's main home. What's left is the taxable value.

**The digest.** Georgia's word, and a few other states', for the total taxable value of every property in a jurisdiction, also called the tax roll or the grand list. The gross digest is the total before exemptions; the net digest is what the rate is actually applied to. The digest is what the rate divides into.

**The levy.** The total property tax a government plans to collect. Rate times net digest equals the levy.

**Reassessment and new growth.** The digest grows two ways. Existing property is revalued, usually upward when prices rise, though not always all of it in the same year. And new buildings, additions and annexed land are added to the rolls. The distinction matters because nearly every rollback rule treats the two differently.

## One town, one year

Take a made-up town whose net digest last year was $500 million and whose rate was 10 mills. It collected $5 million.

This year the assessor finds that existing property is worth 8% more on average, which adds $40 million, and $10 million of new construction comes onto the rolls. The new digest is $550 million.

At last year's 10 mills, the town would collect $5.5 million, 10% more than last year, without anyone voting to raise the rate. That increase is what the rollback rule is about.

The rollback rate asks what rate would bring in last year's $5 million from the properties that were already there. Those properties are now worth $540 million, so the answer is $5 million divided by $540 million: about 9.26 mills. Applied to the whole $550 million digest, including the new buildings, 9.26 mills brings in about $5.09 million. The extra $93,000 comes from the new construction, which is new taxable property, not a higher tax on old property.

States compute the rollback rate a little differently. Texas's no-new-revenue rate is close to the version above: last year's taxes divided by this year's value of the same properties ([Texas Comptroller](https://www.comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/truth-in-taxation/index.php)). Georgia's subtracts the value added by revaluing existing property, expressed in mills, from last year's rate ([Georgia rule 560-11-2-.58](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/georgia/Ga-Comp-R-Regs-R-560-11-2-.58)); for this town that comes to about 9.27 mills, and in effect the new buildings pay at last year's rate. The idea is the same in both: existing property, taken together, pays what it paid last year.

## What it means for one house

Three homes, each taxed on $200,000 last year. The rollback rate keeps the town's total from rising on existing property. It does nothing to keep any one bill steady, because homes don't all gain value at the town's average pace.

<table><thead><tr><th>Home's value</th><th>Now taxed on</th><th>Last year, 10 mills</th><th>This year, 9.26 mills</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Gained 2%</td><td>$204,000</td><td>$2,000</td><td>$1,889 (−5.6%)</td></tr><tr><td>Gained 8% (average)</td><td>$216,000</td><td>$2,000</td><td>$2,000 (no change)</td></tr><tr><td>Gained 15%</td><td>$230,000</td><td>$2,000</td><td>$2,130 (+6.5%)</td></tr></tbody></table>

At the rollback rate the town collects the same from existing property, but the burden moves toward the homes and businesses that gained the most. That's why a homeowner can see a higher bill in a year when the council "rolled back" its rate, and why a neighbor's bill can fall in the same year. It is also why assessment caps, covered in part two, exist: they limit how fast one home's taxable value can rise, which a rollback rate doesn't do.

## When only part of the town is revalued

The town above revalued everything at once. Many places don't. Ohio counties reappraise every parcel every six years and update values statistically at the three-year mark, on staggered schedules ([Ohio Legislative Service Commission](https://www.lsc.ohio.gov/assets/organizations/legislative-service-commission/files/property-tax-the-triennial-update.pdf)). Cook County, Illinois, reassesses one-third of the county each year ([Cook County Assessor](https://www.cookcountyassessoril.gov/quick-information/how-often-my-property-reassessed)). And even where values are set every year, an assessor may update homes from this year's sales while commercial or industrial property, or one part of the jurisdiction, waits for a later review.

The rollback rate is calculated on the total, so it can't tell the difference. Suppose the town's $40 million of reassessment came entirely from homes: homes went from $250 million to $290 million, and business property stayed at $250 million because it wasn't revalued this year. The rollback rate is the same 9.26 mills, and existing property still pays $5 million in all. But a home that went from $200,000 to $232,000 now pays $2,148, up 7.4%, and a business property still valued at $200,000 pays $1,852, down 7.4%. Homes' share of the existing tax base rose from half to about 54%, with no change in the rate that anyone could point to. When business property is revalued in a later year, much of that can swing back.

Appeals move the shares too. Values cut on appeal come out of the digest after the fact, and whatever the cuts don't cover falls on everyone else. In Cook County's north and northwest suburbs, the Assessor's Office reported that appeals cut commercial values 14% in the 2025 cycle and that homes' share of assessed value rose from 66% before reassessment to 68% after ([Cook County Assessor](https://www.cookcountyassessoril.gov/news/north-suburban-homeowners-see-slower-burden-shift-2025-reassessment)).

## The bill has several authors

A property tax bill is usually several taxes on one sheet. The county, the school district, the city if the property is inside one, and special districts for things like fire protection, water, libraries or hospitals each set their own rate on the same assessed value, though not always on the same taxable value: exemptions can differ from one government to the next, so a single home can be taxed on a different amount on each line, or on nothing at all for some, as with a senior exemption from school taxes. Each runs its own rollback calculation and makes its own decision. The school district's rate is often the largest. A city that adopts its rollback rate can sit on the same bill as a school board that doesn't, so "my taxes went up" may be true while one of the governments on the bill truthfully says it didn't raise them.

Voter-approved debt is usually separate too. A bond for a new school is repaid with its own rate, which most rollback rules leave out, because voters already approved it.

## What else moves a bill

Four things can change what a household owes, and only the first is the rate:

1. **A rate above the rollback rate**, adopted by one of the governments on the bill.
2. **A value that rose faster than the average**, as in the table above.
3. **A change in exemptions**: a new homestead exemption lowers bills; losing one, after a sale or a move, raises them. Exemptions also shift the burden: when homesteads are exempted more, the same levy falls more heavily on everything else, such as rental housing and businesses.
4. **A new levy**, such as a voter-approved bond or a new special district.

## Where the money question is decided

The rate fight is really a budget fight. A government that adopts its rollback rate every year is holding its revenue from existing property flat while its costs, such as wages, fuel, insurance and construction, rise with inflation, and new homes and businesses bring in new taxpayers who also need roads, police and schools. A government that keeps last year's rate in a year of rising values collects more from existing property without ever voting on a higher rate. Rollback rules don't decide which is right. They make the choice visible, by requiring a government that wants more money from existing property to say so and to say how much.

## What the requirement looks like

Where these rules exist, adopting a rate above the rollback rate usually sets off some combination of a published notice that calls it a tax increase, public hearings and a recorded vote. A few examples, as of November 2026:

- In **Georgia**, a government must advertise a "notice of property tax increase," issue a press release and hold three public hearings, one of them starting between 6 and 7 pm ([Georgia Department of Revenue](https://dor.georgia.gov/property-taxpayers-bill-rights)). For 2025, assessment notices showed each government's estimated rollback rate, and a bill had to say in bold if the adopted rate was higher ([HB 92, 2025](https://gov.georgia.gov/document/2025-signed-legislation/hb-92/download)). A 2026 law repealed the yearly certification of that estimated rate; 2026 notices instead list a home's exemptions and estimate what they save ([SB 566, 2026](https://gov.georgia.gov/document/2026-signed-legislation/sb-566/download); [Coastal Courier](https://coastalcourier.com/opinion/ask-your-assessor-a-new-era-of-clarity-what-to-expect-from-updated-notices/)).
- In **Florida**, since June 2026, a city or county can adopt a rate no higher than the rolled-back rate by a simple majority; up to 10% above it takes a two-thirds vote, and more than that takes a unanimous vote (three-fourths on a board of nine or more) or a referendum ([Florida Senate, SB 4-F](https://www.flsenate.gov/Committees/BillSummaries/2026F/html/4); [CBS Miami](https://www.cbsnews.com/miami/news/ron-desantis-bills-florida-city-county-property-taxes-local-june-24-2026/)).
- In **Texas**, most cities and counties that adopt a rate above the voter-approval rate, which allows about 3.5% more operating revenue than last year plus debt payments, must put it to an election ([Texas Comptroller](https://www.comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/truth-in-taxation/index.php)).
- In **Utah**, a government that wants more than its certified tax rate allows must give notice, by mail to each owner or by advertisement depending on its budget year, and hold a public hearing ([Kem Gardner Policy Institute, via Utah Business](https://www.utahbusiness.com/press-releases/2026/08/07/utah-truth-taxation-system-property-tax-system-kem-gardner-policy-institute/)); a 2026 law added an earlier public announcement for governments on a July budget year and requires them to set the extra revenue aside until the final budget is adopted ([HB 236, 2026](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2026/bills/enrolled/HB0236.pdf)).

## Where the analogy breaks

The restaurant table decides its bill once. A local government decides each year, under rules that differ by state, and some states don't let it decide the bill at all: California fixes the rate and limits how fast values can rise, and Massachusetts limits how fast the total can grow. Part two takes those up.

## What people argue about

**Is a rate above the rollback rate a tax increase?** One side: yes. If a government collects more from the same properties than it did last year, taxpayers are paying more, and calling a flat rate "no increase" hides a decision behind the assessor's work. That is the reasoning behind laws that make governments advertise it as an increase. The other side: holding to the rollback rate year after year is a real cut once inflation is counted, since wages, insurance and construction costs keep rising, and an advertisement calling it a tax increase treats ordinary cost growth as a new tax.

**Should governments have to vote, or should voters?** One side: notices and hearings are enough, because elected officials answer for the result at the next election, and requiring referendums for routine increases slows budgets and pushes governments toward fees and debt. The other: a hearing that few people attend is weak protection, and only a vote, as in Texas above a set threshold, gives taxpayers the last word.
