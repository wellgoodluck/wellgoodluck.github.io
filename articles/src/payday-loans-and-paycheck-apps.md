## Short version

A payday loan is a small loan due on your next payday, priced as a flat fee, commonly $15 for every $100 borrowed. That sounds modest, but it works out to an annual rate near 400%. The real trap is that most borrowers can't repay in two weeks, so they pay the fee again and again. Paycheck-advance apps work similarly and can cost less per advance, but frequent use adds up fast.

## The trap in one sentence

You pay $45 to borrow $300 for two weeks, then pay it again when payday comes and the $300 still isn't there.

## How it works

**Storefront and online payday loans.** You borrow a small amount, usually a few hundred dollars, and give the lender access to your bank account or a post-dated check. On your next payday, the lender takes back the loan plus the fee.

If you can't cover it, many lenders let you "roll over" the loan: you pay just the fee, and the full loan is due two weeks later. Federal research has found that [most payday loans are followed by another one within weeks](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/newsroom/cfpb-finds-four-out-of-five-payday-loans-are-rolled-over-or-renewed/), because paying back $345 out of one paycheck often leaves you short again.

**Paycheck-advance apps.** These apps advance part of your pay before payday and take it back when your paycheck lands. Many are marketed as "earned wage access" and say they aren't loans. Costs come as monthly subscriptions, fees for getting money instantly instead of in a few days, and "tips" the app suggests you add. A study released in October by the Center for Responsible Lending, a consumer advocacy group, found that [96% of advances from direct-to-consumer apps carried some fee](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/2026/10/02/232-apr-the-high-cost-of-payday-loan-apps-by-state/190991/), including tips, and that the average works out to an annual rate of 232%. Each advance is usually small, so the danger is using them every pay period.

## A worked example

You borrow $300 from a payday lender at $15 per $100. The fee is $45, due with the loan in two weeks.

{{illustration name=payday-treadmill caption="Rolling over pays the fee but not the loan, so the $300 stays owed until the last payday."}}

After ten weeks, you've paid $225 in fees to borrow $300. The annual percentage rate on a single two-week loan at that price is about 391%.

For comparison, the average credit card APR is about 22%, and a credit card cash advance ([how cash advances work](credit-card-cash-advances.html)) usually runs under 30%, plus a fee.

## How to avoid it

**Call the people you owe first.** Many landlords, utilities and medical offices will set up a payment plan or push back a due date if you ask before it's late. That costs nothing.

**Check with your bank or credit union.** Many offer small-dollar loans or emergency advances at a fraction of payday prices. Credit unions in particular often have them.

**If you use a paycheck app, use the free option.** Most apps have a no-fee, slower transfer. Set any tip to zero; the app is required to work either way in a growing number of states.

**Treat any of these as a one-time fix, not a habit.** If you need an advance every pay period, the app or loan isn't solving the problem; it's charging you for it. That's the moment to look hard at the budget, or at help from a nonprofit credit counselor.

**If you're in the military,** federal law [caps the rate on most consumer loans to servicemembers and their families at 36%](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/10/987).

## The bigger picture

Payday lending is regulated mostly by states, and the rules vary enormously. Some states effectively ban payday loans by capping rates; others allow fees well above $15 per $100.

At the federal level, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 2017 payday rule would have required lenders to check whether borrowers could afford to repay. That part was rescinded in 2020. A narrower piece limiting repeated withdrawal attempts from borrowers' bank accounts took effect in 2025, but the bureau said it [would not prioritize enforcing it](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/cfpb-will-not-enforce-payday-lending-rule), and [its 2026 agenda calls for reconsidering the remaining rule](https://natlawreview.com/article/cfpbs-2026-regulatory-agenda-tees-long-awaited-nprm-reconsider-payday-lending-rule). In December 2025, the bureau also said that wage advances repaid through payroll deduction, usually offered through employers, [are not credit under federal lending law](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-12-23/pdf/2025-23735.pdf), so their fees and tips don't have to be disclosed as an interest rate; it left the status of most apps that pull repayment from your bank account unresolved. States are moving in different directions, and [a federal bill on these apps cleared a House committee this summer](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2026/07/07/house-financial-services-committee-advances-federal-earned-wage-access-bill-a-major-step-toward-national-regulation/).

**Supporters of tighter limits** argue these products are built on repeat borrowing by people in financial trouble, that the fees are wildly out of line with the amounts borrowed, and that borrowers deserve a clear interest rate to compare.

**Opponents** argue that people with an emergency and no savings need fast small-dollar credit, that rate caps push lenders out and leave borrowers with worse choices like overdrafts or unlicensed lenders, and that paycheck apps are often cheaper than the alternatives.

These rules may change again. This page reflects federal law as of November 8, 2026.
