## The short version

Three things changed in 2026. Nexstar bought Tegna, giving one company TV stations that reach about 80% of American households; the FCC waived its 39% national cap to allow it, then in August voted to repeal the cap altogether, a decision now being challenged, and a federal judge has ordered Nexstar to run Tegna's stations separately while a lawsuit by state attorneys general and DirecTV goes to trial. Paramount bought Warner Bros. Discovery, putting CNN and CBS News under one company, now called Skydance, with an editorial-independence board required by a settlement with the states. And the FCC approved Saudi, Emirati and Qatari sovereign funds holding about 39% of Skydance through non-voting shares, under the one law that limits foreign ownership of American news. That law has applied to broadcast licenses since 1934. It doesn't reach newspapers, cable channels, streaming services or apps like TikTok, which a separate law forced into majority non-Chinese ownership this January. The FCC's majority and the big station groups say local broadcasters need scale to survive competitors that face no limits; critics, from state attorneys general to cable companies and press advocates, say fewer owners mean fewer independent newsrooms and higher cable bills.

## The analogy

Zoning rules for a town that apply only to the buildings on Main Street. For decades Main Street was where everything happened, so the rules covered most of the town: how many buildings one landlord could own, how much of a building an out-of-town investor could hold. Then most new building happened on the side streets and out by the highway, where the rules don't reach. The town is now deciding whether to loosen Main Street's rules so its landlords can compete, or keep them because Main Street is still where many people shop.

Main Street is the broadcast airwaves, which the FCC licenses. The side streets are cable, newspapers, streaming and social media.

## The cap that went away

The FCC has limited how many TV stations one company can own nationwide since 1941: three stations then, seven by 1954, twelve in 1984, and from early 1985 a cap on the share of the country's households an owner's stations could reach, 25%. The 1996 Telecommunications Act dropped the station count and set the reach cap at 35%. The FCC raised it to 45% in 2003; Congress, in a 2004 spending law, set it at 39% instead ([FCC, Commissioner Gomez's March 2026 statement, which recounts the history](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-420003A1.pdf); [FCC, on the 2004 law](https://www.fcc.gov/document/implementation-section-629-consolidated-appropriations-act-2004)).

**Nexstar and Tegna.** Nexstar, already the largest owner of local TV stations, agreed in August 2025 to buy Tegna for $6.2 billion, which would put its stations in front of about 80% of U.S. households ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/17/nx-s1-5789059/judge-halts-nexstar-tegna-merger-lawsuits); [Free Press](https://www.freepress.net/news/trump-fccs-ignores-evidence-and-law-approves-nexstars-tegna-takeover)). On March 19, 2026, the FCC's Media Bureau approved the deal, waiving the 39% cap and, in 23 markets, its rule against owning more than two stations in one market, with six stations to be sold; federal regulators approved it, and Nexstar closed within hours ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/03/fcc-says-it-granted-ownership-rule-waivers-to-approve-nexstar-tegna-deal/); [Nevada Broadcasters Association](https://nevadabroadcasters.org/fcc-media-bureau-approves-nexstars-acquisition-of-tegna-what-does-it-mean-for-consideration-of-the-broadcast-ownership-rules/); [NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/17/nx-s1-5789059/judge-halts-nexstar-tegna-merger-lawsuits)). The FCC's reasoning was that the cap, last set by Congress more than two decades ago, predates streaming, and that local broadcasters face pressure from much larger media and tech companies ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/03/fcc-says-it-granted-ownership-rule-waivers-to-approve-nexstar-tegna-deal/)).

**The lawsuit.** Eight state attorneys general sued in federal court in California in March, and five more joined in April, three of them Republicans; DirecTV sued separately, and the cases were combined. They argue the deal would raise what cable and satellite companies pay for local stations and cut local news ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/04/five-states-added-to-antitrust-lawsuit-against-nexstar-over-tegna-deal/); [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/tech-and-telecom-law/gop-state-attorneys-general-join-suit-to-stop-nexstar-tegna-deal)). On April 17 the court issued a preliminary injunction: Nexstar must run the Tegna stations separately until the antitrust case is decided, and could be forced to undo the deal if it loses ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/17/nx-s1-5789059/judge-halts-nexstar-tegna-merger-lawsuits)). In July the plaintiffs told the court Nexstar wasn't keeping Tegna separate, because Tegna's board was made up of Nexstar executives ([NewscastStudio](https://www.newscaststudio.com/2026/07/22/directv-ags-nexstar-tegna-separation/)). The Ninth Circuit hears Nexstar's appeal of the injunction on November 17, and the trial is tentatively set for July 2027 ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/10/appeals-court-sets-date-for-arguments-over-nexstar-tegna-injunction/); [The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/10/judge-approves-delays-nexstar-tegna-case/)). {{due 2026-11-30 "Ninth Circuit argument on the Nexstar–Tegna injunction was Nov 17: update the lawsuit paragraph if it has ruled"}} A separate challenge to the FCC's approval, by the advocacy group Free Press, was dismissed in July because the full commission hadn't yet reviewed the bureau's decision ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/07/appeals-court-tosses-challenge-to-fccs-approval-of-nexstar-tegna-deal/)). {{due 2027-01-15 "Has the full FCC ruled on Free Press's application for review of the Nexstar–Tegna approval?"}}

**The repeal.** On August 6, the FCC voted 2–1 to eliminate the national cap and review station deals case by case instead. Commissioner Anna Gomez dissented, arguing that Congress set the cap and the FCC lacks the authority to remove it ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/06/fcc-broadcast-ownership-cap-vote)). The order takes effect 60 days after it is published in the Federal Register. In October, ten state and regional cable associations and, separately, public-interest groups and unions including Free Press and the NewsGuild asked the FCC to put it on hold, and the cable groups said they would take it to court; Nexstar, opposing the requests, told the FCC that "consolidation will benefit television broadcasters and the public interest, not harm them" ([Light Reading](https://www.lightreading.com/video-broadcast/cable-orgs-say-they-ll-sue-to-block-fcc-s-repeal-of-broadcast-ownership-rule); [The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/10/nexstar-asks-fcc-to-reject-stay-of-national-tv-ownership-cap-repeal/)). {{due 2026-12-15 "Has the cap repeal taken effect, been stayed, or been taken to court? Update the repeal paragraph and the short version"}}

**The money behind it.** Much of a local station's income now comes not from ads but from retransmission fees, what cable and satellite companies pay to carry it. Those fees came to $14.08 billion in 2021 ([Pew Research Center, from S&P Global](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/chart/sotnm-local-tv-u-s-local-tv-station-retransmission-fee-revenue/)). S&P forecast about $15.4 billion for 2025, counting what streaming TV services pay too, and about $7.3 billion after what stations pay the networks they're affiliated with ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2025/10/s-and-p-forecast-cable-broadcast-retrans-fees/)). A bigger station group has more leverage in those negotiations, which is why carriage fees are at the center of the lawsuit.

## CNN and CBS News under one roof

Paramount Skydance, controlled by David Ellison's family, won Warner Bros. Discovery in a bidding war with Netflix and closed the roughly $110 billion deal on October 6; the combined company is called Skydance ([The National](https://www.thenationalnews.com/business/2026/02/27/paramount-skydance-wins-warner-bros-bid-as-netflix-drops-out); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/paramount-warner-bros-discovery-deal-skydance/)). It owns two of the country's best-known news brands. CBS News's editor in chief since October 2025 has been Bari Weiss, named when Paramount bought her outlet, The Free Press ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/paramount-buys-the-free-press-names-weiss-cbs-news-chief-1)). Mark Thompson, who ran CNN before the deal, stays as its chairman and editor in chief, and the company says he and Weiss will run CNN and CBS News separately; no combination of the two newsrooms has been announced ([Barrett Media](https://barrettmedia.com/2026/10/05/mark-thompson-to-remain-at-cnn/); [TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/business/skydance-leadership-team-paramount-warner-bros-bari-weiss-mark-thompson-james-gunn-casey-bloys/)). Warner Bros. Discovery projected in January that CNN would bring in about $1.8 billion in revenue and $600 million in adjusted profit in 2026 ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/industry-news/business/cnn-revenue-profit-projections-2026/)).

Ellison has said repeatedly that the company wants to reach what he calls the 70% of Americans who identify as center-left to center-right; surveys by YouGov, Pew and Gallup put the center closer to 40–45%, The Guardian reported, and Paramount stands by the figure ([The Guardian, via AOL](https://www.aol.com/entertainment/david-ellison-says-70-americans-110017639.html)). Critics pointed to the Ellison family's ties to the White House and to President Trump's public support for selling CNN ([CNN, via ABC 17](https://abc17news.com/?p=1599269)). Ellison told CNBC in March that CNN's editorial independence would be maintained ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/?p=4482526)). State attorneys general sued over the deal; the settlement that cleared it requires an editorial board to help CNN and CBS News maintain their independence ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/paramount-warner-bros-discovery-deal-skydance/)).

## Foreign money: the oldest rule, and what it covers

**The rule.** The Communications Act bars foreign governments, foreign citizens and foreign companies from holding a broadcast license at all, and bars a license to any company more than a fifth foreign-owned, a limit that goes back to 1927. Since 1934 it has also let the FCC refuse a license whose parent company is more than a quarter foreign-owned ([47 U.S.C. 310](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/310); [part one](who-owned-the-news.html)). The second limit has always been the FCC's to waive, and it has: in 1995, finding Fox about 99% owned by Rupert Murdoch's Australian-based News Corp, the FCC moved to let it keep its stations as serving the public interest ([Christian Science Monitor](https://www.csmonitor.com/1995/0728/28014.html)). In January 2026 the FCC wrote its longstanding practice for these reviews into its rules ([FCC 26-3](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-26-3A1.pdf)) and, in a companion order, required licensees to declare whether they are controlled from a "foreign adversary" on the Commerce Department's list (China, including Hong Kong and Macau, Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia and Venezuela's Maduro regime), citing risks such as false emergency alerts and influence over programming ([FCC 26-2](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-26-2A1.pdf); [15 CFR 791.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/15/791.4)).

**Skydance.** About $24 billion of Paramount's financing came from three Gulf sovereign wealth funds ([Variety](https://au.variety.com/2026/biz/news/middle-east-paramount-warner-bros-discovery-33803/)). Because the company holds CBS's station licenses, it needed the FCC's approval to go past 25%. In September the FCC's Media Bureau allowed foreign investors to hold up to 100% of its equity. Paramount expected foreign investors to hold about 49.5%: Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund 15.1%, entities tied to Abu Dhabi's L'imad Holding 12.8%, and the Qatar Investment Authority 10.6%, each allowed to rise to 20%. The shares carry no votes; the Ellison family and RedBird Capital keep the voting stock. The approval is conditioned on an agreement with federal national security officials barring the funds from governance, from influence over content or management, and from non-public data about Americans. Commissioner Gomez said a decision of that size should have had a full commission vote ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/09/fcc-media-bureau-approves-paramount-foreign-investment/)). Paramount had said the deal fell outside review by the Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States because the funds gave up governance rights; a House member and three senators, all Democrats, asked the committee to review it anyway ([Rep. Liccardo](https://liccardo.house.gov/media/press-releases/after-foreign-governments-dump-billions-paramount-warner-bros-bid-liccardo)).

**What the rule doesn't reach.** Section 310 applies to broadcast licenses because they use public airwaves. Newspapers, cable channels, streaming services, websites and social apps need no license, so no foreign-ownership limit applies to them. The check that does apply is that committee, the Treasury-led review of foreign investments in American companies for national-security risks, which looks at a deal case by case rather than by a fixed share. As news moved off the licensed airwaves, the oldest rule in American media came to cover a smaller part of where people get it.

**TikTok.** Congress handled one app directly. Under a 2024 law forcing its sale, TikTok's U.S. business moved in January 2026 into a joint venture in which its Chinese parent, ByteDance, keeps 19.9%; Oracle, the private equity firm Silver Lake and the Emirati investment firm MGX hold 15% each, and other investors the rest ([The Week](https://theweek.com/tech/tiktok-finalizes-deal-us-version)). Lawmakers' concern was that China could surveil Americans or spread propaganda. One in five American adults regularly gets news on TikTok, and 39% of adults under 30 ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/10/19/1-in-5-americans-regularly-get-news-on-tiktok/)).

## Where the audience is

- **Social media.** 59% of American adults get news there at least sometimes, up from 53% in 2020. The shares who regularly get news on each platform: Facebook 40%, YouTube 34%, Instagram 23%, TikTok 20%, X 10% ([Pew Research Center, August 2026 survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/social-media-and-news-fact-sheet/)).
- **Local TV.** 65% got local news from local TV at least sometimes in 2025, down from 70% in 2018 ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/fact-sheet/local-news-fact-sheet/)).
- **Trust.** 33% of Americans trust the news media a great deal or a fair amount, up from a record-low 28% in 2025; it was 72% in 1976 ([Gallup, 2026](https://news.gallup.com/poll/714938/trust-news-media-edges-slightly-higher.aspx)).

The platforms where audiences have grown are the ones the ownership rules don't cover.

## Where the analogy breaks

Main Street's zoning protects shoppers who walk past; broadcast rules also protect something less visible, the emergency alerts and local coverage a licensed station is expected to carry. And the side streets aren't empty of rules: national-security review, antitrust law and, for one app, an act of Congress all reach them. The difference is that those rules work case by case, after a deal is proposed, rather than by a fixed limit everyone knows in advance.

## What people argue about

**The national cap.** The FCC's majority argues the cap is an artificial limit that keeps local broadcasters small while they compete for viewers and ads with streaming services and tech platforms that face none; Chairman Brendan Carr called it "an arcane, artificial limit" in 2025 ([Washington Examiner](https://www.washingtonexaminer.com/?p=3906164)), and Nexstar argues that consolidation benefits broadcasters and the public ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/10/nexstar-asks-fcc-to-reject-stay-of-national-tv-ownership-cap-repeal/)). Supporters of the cap, including Commissioner Gomez and groups such as Free Press, say Congress set it and only Congress can remove it, and that consolidation leads to newsroom layoffs and more shared, less local news ([Free Press](https://www.freepress.net/news/trump-fccs-ignores-evidence-and-law-approves-nexstars-tegna-takeover)). Opposition doesn't follow party lines: Newsmax, a conservative network, told the FCC the Nexstar deal would "further concentrate distribution and set the stage for a domino of additional similar transactions" ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/01/newsmax-urges-fcc-to-deny-nexstar-tegna-merger/)), and three of the attorneys general suing are Republicans. The research summarized in [part one](who-owned-the-news.html) finds the effect of a merger depends on the buyer.

**Carriage fees.** The states, DirecTV and the cable associations argue that a bigger station group can demand higher fees, which reach subscribers' bills, and risk more blackouts when talks fail ([NPR](https://www.npr.org/2026/04/17/nx-s1-5789059/judge-halts-nexstar-tegna-merger-lawsuits); [Light Reading](https://www.lightreading.com/video-broadcast/cable-orgs-say-they-ll-sue-to-block-fcc-s-repeal-of-broadcast-ownership-rule)). The FCC, approving the Nexstar deal, dismissed the concern that it would raise consumers' costs, pointing instead to the pressure broadcasters face from larger media and streaming companies ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/03/fcc-says-it-granted-ownership-rule-waivers-to-approve-nexstar-tegna-deal/)).

**Gulf money in Skydance.** The FCC and Paramount say non-voting shares, no board seats and a binding national-security agreement keep the funds from any say over the news ([The Desk](https://thedesk.net/2026/09/fcc-media-bureau-approves-paramount-foreign-investment/)). Analysts quoted by Variety, and Netflix's co-chief executive Ted Sarandos, argued that an investment that size can't be fully passive, since big investors negotiate access and future leverage even without votes ([Variety](https://au.variety.com/2026/biz/news/middle-east-paramount-warner-bros-discovery-33803/)). The lawmakers who asked for a national-security review argued that calling the funds passive doesn't exempt the deal from one ([Rep. Liccardo](https://liccardo.house.gov/media/press-releases/after-foreign-governments-dump-billions-paramount-warner-bros-bid-liccardo)). None of the three countries is on the foreign-adversary list.

**Who should the rules cover?** The FCC's majority holds that rules written for scarce airwaves no longer fit when most news reaches people another way, and has loosened them for broadcasters. Congress, in the TikTok law, acted on the opposite worry, that who owns a platform can matter as much as who owns a station, but for one company and one set of countries; no general rule for online news has passed.
