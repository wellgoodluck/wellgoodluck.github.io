## The short version

The almanac's six grocery prices are the Bureau of Labor Statistics' monthly averages of what stores across the country charge for one narrowly defined item each, a dozen Grade A large eggs, a gallon of whole milk, a pound of white bread, of ground coffee, of ground beef and of whole chicken, collected along with the prices that make up the consumer price index and not seasonally adjusted; your store can sit well above or below the average, but it moves with the line.

Of each dollar spent on food at home in 2024, 18.5 cents reached a farm; the rest paid for processing, packing, trucking, the store and the people in between. How much of a price comes from the farm, and how fast the farm can recover, decide how it behaves: eggs went from $3.37 a dozen in October 2024 to a record $6.23 in March 2025 as bird flu destroyed the laying flock, and back to $2.14 by June 2026 as the flock was rebuilt. Ground beef, held up by the smallest cattle herd since 1951, went from $3.81 a pound in 2019 to $6.92 in August 2026, and economists expect it to stay high until ranchers rebuild the herd, which takes years. Coffee, at $9.72 a pound in April 2026, cost more than twice what it did in late 2019 after two drought years in Brazil, and bread, in which the wheat is about 3% of the price, rose with everyone's wages and fuel and stayed up when wheat fell. In September 2026 grocery prices as a whole were 1.8% higher than a year earlier, less than half the rise in all prices. What is argued about is how much of the 2025 egg price was bird flu and how much was a price benchmark the Justice Department alleges producers manipulated; whether grocery chains widened their margins; whether the 2025 tariffs reached the coffee shelf before they were lifted; and whether four meatpackers set the price of beef.

## The analogy

A grocery price is a stack of receipts, and the farmer's is at the bottom and usually the smallest. The Department of Agriculture adds them up every year. Of each dollar Americans spent on food eaten at home in 2024, 18.5 cents went to farms. The rest paid for what happens between the farm and the shelf: processing and packaging, trucks and warehouses, the store, its energy, its insurance, its advertising, and the wages of everyone along the way. The stack is different for every item. In 2025 the farm's receipt for a gallon of whole milk was $1.93 of the $4.07 retail price, almost half, because milk reaches the store much as it left the cow. For a pound of white bread it was 6 cents of $1.87, about 3%, because wheat has to be milled, mixed, baked, sliced, bagged and delivered before it is bread.

The size of the farmer's receipt decides how much a shock on the farm moves the shelf. From 2020 to 2022, as wheat climbed to a record, the wheat in a pound of white bread went from about 6 cents to 11, while the pound itself went from $1.45 to $1.70: most of bread's rise was everything else. Eggs are washed, graded and packed but otherwise sold as they were laid, so when bird flu cut the supply the shortage went straight to the shelf, and when the flock was rebuilt the price came back down. When the shock is a cost spread through the whole stack, as fuel, wages and packaging were in 2021 and 2022, every receipt grows a little, none of them shrinks on its own, and the price settles at a new level.

## What moves it

Each item has its own weather.

**Eggs: disease.** The laying flock is the supply, and it can be destroyed in weeks and takes months to replace, because new hens have to be raised before they lay. In the bird flu wave that began in October 2024, more than 49 million layers and pullets were lost by late February 2025, and the table-egg flock fell to 292 million on March 1, 2025, its low for the year. A dozen eggs went from $3.37 in October 2024 to $6.23 in March 2025, the highest on record, as charged or adjusted for inflation, in a series that begins in 1980. Then the flock was rebuilt: by October 1, 2026 it stood at 319 million, about 4% larger than a year earlier, and the Department of Agriculture expects 2026 production to be 5% higher than 2025's. The price fell with the recovery, to $2.14 in June 2026, and the department's October outlook expects retail egg prices to average about 29% less in 2026 than in 2025. It happened before: the 2014–15 outbreak destroyed about 33 million laying hens, 11% of the flock, and the consumer price index for eggs was 36% higher in September 2015 than a year earlier before falling back the next year. The egg chart marks each outbreak.

Each spike came back down as the flock was rebuilt. The month of October 2025 is missing; the federal shutdown that fall meant no prices were collected."}}

**Beef: the herd.** A cow carries a calf for about nine months, and the calf is raised for well over a year before slaughter, so beef supply is set by decisions ranchers made years ago, and the cattle herd rises and falls in cycles the Department of Agriculture puts at eight to twelve years. The herd peaked at 94.7 million head in 2019. From 2020 on, drought, widespread and severe in 2022, dried up pasture and forced ranchers to sell cows rather than keep them, and on January 1, 2026 the national herd was 86.2 million, the smallest since 1951, with the fewest beef cows since 1961. Fewer cows means fewer calves for years to come: the 2025 calf crop was 2% smaller than 2024's, and the department expects beef production to be about 5% lower in 2026 than in 2025. Ground beef went from $3.81 a pound on average in 2019 to a record $6.92 in August 2026. Imports filled part of the gap: a record 5.4 billion pounds in 2025, and a forecast 6.3 billion, nearly 18% more, in 2026, even as cattle from Mexico, normally about 1.2 million head a year, were shut out over a screwworm outbreak there from May 2025 until a phased reopening of border crossings began in late August 2026. Ranchers began holding back more heifers in 2026, which the department's chief economist called the first sign of a rebuild, and an Oklahoma State economist wrote in October 2025 that supplies would stay tight for the next two to four years: a heifer kept back in 2026 has her first calf in 2027, and that calf is beef in 2029.

{{chart id=ground_beef range=all ov=ground_beef_real,ev_beef caption="Ground beef as charged and in today's dollars, with the drought that shrank the herd from 2010 and the meatpacking closures of April 2020. The 2020s rise has no single onset to mark: the herd began shrinking in 2020, and the department ties the record prices of 2025 and 2026 to the herd it left behind."}}

**Coffee: Brazil's weather.** The United States grows almost none of its coffee. Brazil grows about 35% of the world's and Vietnam about 18%, so a bad year in either moves every shelf in America. Two years of drought and heat cut Brazil's arabica crop from 44.5 million bags in 2024–25 to 38.0 million in 2025–26, and the world price of mild arabica beans doubled, from about $2.04 a pound in January 2024 to $4.10 in February 2025. Ground coffee in U.S. stores followed, from $6.12 a pound in January 2024 to $9.72 in April 2026, the highest ever as charged. The crop is now expected to recover: the Department of Agriculture's July 2026 forecast had Brazil and Vietnam both harvesting records in 2026–27 and noted that world prices had dropped 25% in seven months. By September 2026 the shelf price had come down about 4% from its peak. The coffee chart marks the Brazilian frosts of 1994 and 2021; the drought of 2023–25, which the department blames for the record but does not date, is left unmarked.

**Milk: feed, and a formula.** A dairy farmer's milk check is set by a federal formula. The Agriculture Department's milk marketing orders price milk by what it becomes, fluid milk, yogurt, cheese or butter and powder, with minimum prices worked out each month from the wholesale prices of cheese, butter, dry milk and whey. Feed is the farmer's largest cost, about 72% of operating costs in 2024 by the department's estimates, so corn and soybean prices reach the milk check with a lag. In September 2026 farmers were paid $19.50 per hundred pounds, 90 cents (4%) less than a year earlier, and the department's index of farm-level milk prices was down 11.8% from a year before, but the retail price of dairy was down only 0.7%: the gallon is almost half farm, and the other half, which is bottling, cold trucks and the dairy case, did not get cheaper.

**Bread: everything but the wheat.** The wheat in a pound of white bread cost 6 cents in 2025. When wheat climbed to a record $8.83 a bushel in the 2022–23 crop year after Russia invaded Ukraine, the wheat in a pound of white bread nearly doubled in cost, to 11 cents in 2022, 6.5% of the price, and bread went from $1.56 a pound in January 2022 to $2.03 in January 2024. Wheat then fell by more than 40%, to $5.06 in 2025–26, and bread came down about 10%, to $1.83. The other 97 cents of every bread dollar, milling, baking, packaging, delivery, shelf space and the wages behind each, rose with everything else in 2021 and 2022 and stayed there. In 2025 the grain was cheap and the rest was not.

**Chicken: the fastest farm.** A broiler reaches market weight in about 47 days, so chicken supply can expand within a season, and feed, mostly corn and soybean meal, is the largest cost. That is why chicken moved so much less than beef: a pound of whole chicken averaged $1.50 in 2019 and $2.06 in September 2026, while ground beef went from $3.81 to $6.79, and the department expects poultry prices to rise about 1% in 2026, against about 9% for beef. The chicken chart carries boneless breast as a second line, and the ground beef chart carries sirloin.

## How to read this month's number

**Against its own history, in today's dollars.** The almanac's range for each item is adjusted for inflation, because a raw grocery price only ever trends up. By that measure eggs in {{v eggs period}} are {{fig eggs_rank}}, and ground beef is {{fig ground_beef_rank}}. A price can be at a record as charged and ordinary in today's dollars. Milk is the clearest case: its record as charged was $4.32 a gallon in June 2026, but restated in today's dollars a gallon cost more than $6 in June 2004.

**Against the shock calendar.** Each chart page marks the supply events the Department of Agriculture names as a main cause of a move in that item's price, dated from the department's own reports; bird flu on eggs, frosts on coffee, drought and plant closures on beef. If the price jumped and no marker explains it, the cause is in the rest of the stack, or in demand.

**Rate against level.** In September 2026, "eggs cost 35% less than a year ago" and "eggs cost 63% more than in 2019" were both true at once. The first is a rate, the second a level, and most grocery headlines report the rate while most shoppers remember the level. Those are the average prices on the tiles; the consumer price index for eggs fell 20.6% over the same year, because it covers every kind of egg and is built to measure change, which the bureau says its average prices are not. [Rate vs. level](rate-vs-level.html) is the general case. Groceries are the clearest example of when a level does come back down: it takes a shock whose cause is on the farm, as with eggs, and a farm that can rebuild in months, as laying flocks can and cattle herds cannot.

**One month is missing.** No average prices exist for October 2025. The Bureau of Labor Statistics collected no prices during that fall's federal shutdown, so every one of these series skips from September to November 2025.

## Where the analogy breaks

The receipts in the stack are not fixed, and they do not all move together. The farm's share of a gallon of milk was 55% in 2022 and 47.5% in 2025; of a pound of flour, 33% and 15%. When farm prices fall, the other receipts tend to hold, and the Department of Agriculture's own note on the subject says retail prices can respond with a delay. That is why an 11.8% fall in farm-level milk prices, and a 4% fall in what dairy farmers were paid, showed up as less than a 1% fall at the dairy case, and why coffee on the shelf was off 4% from its peak in September 2026 while world bean prices were off about a fifth from theirs. The pattern, prices that rise like rockets and fall like feathers, is the same one documented at [the gas pump](what-sets-the-price-of-gas.html).

The national average is also only an average. The bureau prices one narrowly defined item in each case, packaged white bread, whole milk, a whole fresh chicken with bone and skin, and a store's own brand, a warehouse club or a city with high rents can sit far from the line. And these prices are not seasonally adjusted, so a one-month move may be the calendar.

## What people argue about

**Whether the 2025 egg prices were bird flu or collusion.** One side: the flock numbers explain it. The Department of Agriculture's economists put the rise down to the laying hens lost to bird flu; United Egg Producers, the industry's trade group, told the Senate in February 2025 that bird flu was the reason for the shortage and the high prices; and the head of the American Egg Board, the industry's federally overseen promotion board, said the prices had "nothing to do with anything other than bird flu." Cal-Maine Foods, the largest producer, counted about 40 million layers and pullets destroyed across the industry in 2024 and 39 million more by May 2025, and called its higher prices a direct result of the smaller supply; in its fiscal year to May 2026, with the flock rebuilt, its price for conventional eggs fell by half and its profit by three-quarters. The other: the price rose further than the shortage did. Farm Action, a group that campaigns against concentration in farming, wrote to the Federal Trade Commission and the Justice Department in February 2025 that the flock was only a few percent smaller than in 2021 while Cal-Maine's gross profit had more than tripled, and senators of both parties noted that egg producers had already been found liable for fixing prices, in a 2023 jury verdict covering 2004 to 2008. A Justice Department investigation became public in March 2025. In June 2026 the department and 17 states sued Cal-Maine and two other producers and settled at the same time. The complaint alleged that for years they had coordinated bids on an egg exchange to push up the daily price quotation the industry uses as a benchmark, and that the quotations dropped significantly from their peak once the companies learned of the investigation. The settlement, which still awaits the court's approval, bans the practice and requires compliance officers, with no admission, no federal fine, $3.3 million to the states and 53 million donated eggs. The Iowa attorney general said the companies had overcharged consumers; Cal-Maine denied all wrongdoing and said the communications, made primarily by a single former employee, did not affect egg prices in any market; Farm Action said the settlement might be just another cost of doing business, next to Cal-Maine's $1.2 billion profit in the year of the spike. The conduct the complaint describes went on in the same years bird flu was shrinking the flock, so both explanations can be true at once.

**Whether grocery chains used inflation to widen their margins.** One side: the Federal Trade Commission's March 2024 study of the pandemic supply chain found that food retailers' revenue exceeded their costs by more than 6% in 2021 and 7% in the first three quarters of 2023, against a 5.6% peak in 2015, and that some firms used rising costs as an occasion to raise prices further; in the trial over the Kroger–Albertsons merger that August, a Kroger pricing executive's internal email said retail inflation on milk and eggs had been significantly higher than the company's cost inflation; and Groundwork Collaborative, a progressive think tank, calculated that corporate profits accounted for more than half of inflation in mid-2023. The other: supermarket net margins are thin, 1.6% in 2023 by the industry's count, and economists who looked at the whole economy mostly found that markups were not what drove prices up. The San Francisco Fed found that markups stayed essentially flat through the 2021–23 surge; a Kansas City Fed paper found that rising markups could account for more than half of 2021's inflation but attributed them to firms pricing ahead of costs they expected, not to market power; and when a panel of economists was asked in September 2024 whether there was little evidence that gouging was causing high grocery prices, 77% agreed and none disagreed. Kroger called the email cherry-picked and told the court that fuel and card fees, not gouging, had raised its costs. A federal court blocked the merger in December 2024, and no federal price-gouging law followed the ban proposed in the 2024 campaign. In September 2026 the Justice Department widened its investigation of beef prices to the eight largest grocery chains, asking for their beef margins, costs and pricing strategies; as of November 2026 it has announced no findings.

**Whether tariffs raised the price of coffee and beef.** One side: from August to November 2025 Brazilian coffee, a third of the world's supply, carried a 50% tariff, and Senator Elizabeth Warren pointed out in March 2026 that the consumer price index for coffee had risen 18.4% in the year to February. The Budget Lab at Yale estimated that the tariffs in place in November 2025 would raise food prices about 1.2% in the short run, and the Tax Foundation counted about half of U.S. food imports still under tariff after the November exemptions and wrote that the exemptions tacitly recognized that the tariffs had affected food prices. The other: the president, citing his Council of Economic Advisers, said in July 2025 that tariffs had had "zero impact" on inflation, and in its February 2026 proclamation on Argentine beef the administration tied the price of beef to drought and wildfires that had cut the herd to record lows and to screwworm restrictions that had cut the supply of cattle to feedlots. When it exempted coffee, beef, bananas, tomatoes and other foods from the reciprocal tariffs in November 2025, it described them as products not grown in sufficient quantities in the United States, and it lifted Brazil's extra 40% on coffee and beef, citing initial progress in negotiations with Brazil. The prices themselves fit either reading: the world price of coffee beans had doubled before the reciprocal tariffs began in April 2025, and the shelf price of ground coffee kept rising for five months after they were lifted. After the Supreme Court struck down the emergency tariffs in February 2026, the 10% surcharge that replaced them and the Section 301 tariffs that followed in July both exempted coffee and beef, and the separate 25% tariff on some Brazilian goods in July left them out too. As of November 2026, coffee and beef pay no U.S. tariff beyond the ordinary duties that predate 2025, and beef import quotas have been enlarged twice in 2026: by 80,000 metric tons for Argentina in February and by 300,000 metric tons of lean trimmings from other countries in August. Ranchers dispute that imports help: when the Argentine quota was announced, the National Cattlemen's Beef Association said it fundamentally disagreed that imports lower beef prices, and R-CALF, a ranchers' group, said dominant players could use the extra supply to widen their margins. The Congressional Research Service noted that Argentina had supplied under 2% of U.S. beef imports since 2018 and that its added quota equaled under 5% of 2025's imports, and cited an American Enterprise Institute estimate that it would take about three cents a pound off ground beef.

**Whether the meatpackers set the price of beef.** Four companies slaughter about four in five of the cattle fattened for beef: over 85% of the beef processing market by the Agriculture Secretary's count, against about a quarter in 1977, and about 81% by the meatpackers' trade group, which says the share has barely changed since 1994. One side: in November 2025 the president accused the packers of collusion and price fixing and ordered an investigation, which R-CALF welcomed. In April 2026 news reports said the Justice Department had opened a criminal inquiry; in May its officials said they had reviewed more than 3 million documents and would use every law enforcement tool available; and in September it widened the investigation to the eight largest grocery chains. In private class actions in Minnesota alleging that the packers coordinated to cut slaughter from 2015 on, pushing cattle prices down and beef prices up, JBS, Tyson and Cargill have agreed to pay about $300 million in settlements without admitting liability, and in July 2026 the court certified a class of cattle producers, a ruling National Beef, Tyson and Cargill have appealed. The other: the Meat Institute, the packers' trade group, says beef packers have been operating at a loss for more than a year, about $126 a head in early October 2025 by its count, because cattle are scarce and demand is strong. Tyson's beef business lost $426 million in its 2025 fiscal year before legal accruals and a write-down, and $1.1 billion after them. Derrell Peel, an agricultural economist at Oklahoma State, said the industry has been investigated for fifty years and that breaking up the packers would raise consumer prices while lowering what ranchers are paid, and Glynn Tonsor of Kansas State pointed to strong demand. A 2021 Texas A&M study for Congress found the packers hold some market power but that its effect on prices is small and largely offset by their scale. The National Cattlemen's Beef Association said it wanted the investigation to reach conclusions this time. As of November 2026 it has produced no charges, no complaint and no settlement with the packers.
