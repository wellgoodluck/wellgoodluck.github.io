## In one sentence

A level is where something stands, a rate is how fast it is changing, and most headlines report the rate while most people live in the level.

## The analogy

The speedometer and the odometer. Inflation is the speedometer: 9% in mid-2022 meant prices rising fast; 3.7% now means rising more slowly. The price level is the odometer: about 131 on a scale where 2019 is 100, meaning the same basket costs 31% more. "Inflation is down" means the car slowed. The odometer never runs backward. It only slows.

The same two gauges sit on every indicator, and headlines usually read the speedometer:

- **"The deficit fell."** The deficit is a rate, borrowing per year. The debt is the odometer. A smaller deficit means the debt is growing more slowly, not shrinking.
- **"The Fed cut rates."** A cut is a change. The level can still be high: after three cuts in late 2024, the Fed's rate was still above anything seen from 2008 to early 2022.
- **"Job growth slowed."** Payrolls added 29,000 in September 2026, a weak month. Total employment was at a record. Fewer people joined the party; the party is still full.
- **"Wages are up 3%."** A rate. Whether you are better off is the level of your pay against the level of prices, which the real-wage line on [the inflation chart](../chart.html?id=cpi&range=5y&ov=wages,real_wages) tracks.
- **"Growth slowed to 2%."** Still growing. Shrinking is below zero. The economy is bigger than a year ago, just by less.

Once you see the pair, the habit is to ask which gauge a claim is reading, and whether the other gauge would say the same thing.

## Where the analogy breaks

Some numbers are levels that happen to be called rates. The unemployment rate is a share of the labor force, a level; its monthly change is the rate of change. Interest rates are prices, so "rates rose" is a change in a level. And for some things the rate is the point: the Fed targets 2% inflation, a speed, because it has decided the odometer is not going back and the best it can do is set the cruise control. Whether that was the right decision is argued below.

## What people argue about

**Whether the Fed should target the level.** In 2020 the Fed adopted a form of level targeting: after years of inflation below 2%, it would aim above 2% for a while to make up the shortfall. Inflation then overshot by far more than anyone meant to make up, and in its 2025 framework review the Fed dropped the makeup language and went back to targeting the rate. Supporters of level targeting say it keeps the long-run price path predictable and gives the Fed room when rates are at zero; critics say the makeup promise is part of why the Fed waited through 2021. Nobody proposes making up the 2021–23 overshoot the other way, since that would take years of falling prices.

**Deficit or debt.** One side says a falling deficit is the progress that matters, because debt can be carried indefinitely if it grows more slowly than the economy. The other says the level is what gets serviced: interest on the federal debt now costs more per year than national defense, and that bill is set by the odometer, not the speedometer. Both read the dashboard correctly. They disagree about which gauge the car can run on.
