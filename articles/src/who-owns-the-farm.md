## The short version

America had 1.9 million farms in 2022, 6.9% fewer than five years earlier, and they were larger. Nearly all are still family businesses, and family farms produce most of the food; just over 60% of farmland is worked by the people who own it. Output has concentrated anyway: the 6% of farms that sold $1 million or more a year made 78% of all sales. The concentration that draws the most argument is on either side of the farm. Two companies, Bayer and Corteva, sell more than half the seed for corn, soybeans and cotton; four companies sell most of the nitrogen fertilizer; three shellers buy most of the country's peanuts; the four largest beef packers buy about 85% of the cattle fattened for slaughter, up from 36% in 1980, and fewer than one in five of those cattle is now sold in a negotiated cash trade, down from nearly half in 2005; and nearly every chicken is raised under contract for a company that owns the birds. Of each dollar spent on food, about 12 cents reaches a farm. One chicken company has pleaded guilty to price fixing, a data company that served meat processors has settled with regulators, and the beef packers are under investigation, with no charges reported. The companies say scale lowers costs and that scarce cattle, strong demand and world supply explain prices, and the government's own review of the research found some of each. Confirmed foreign ownership is less than 1% of farmland, mostly forest, and two of the four largest beef packers have Brazilian owners.

## The analogy

An hourglass. Sand runs from a wide top to a wide bottom through a narrow neck. In the meat business the top is the hundreds of thousands of ranches and farms that raise animals, the bottom is every household that buys meat, and the neck is a few packing companies that nearly every animal passes through. On the farm's other side is a second neck: a few companies that sell farmers their seed, fertilizer and machinery.

The farm sits between the two necks. What it pays and what it's paid are set, in part, by the few companies on either side.

Figures below are as of early November 2026.

## Fewer, bigger farms, still mostly family-owned

The Census of Agriculture counted 1,900,487 farms in 2022, 6.9% fewer than in 2017, on 880 million acres. The average farm grew 5%, to 463 acres. The largest 2% of farms, those with 5,000 acres or more, held 42% of the farmland; the 42% of farms smaller than 50 acres held 2% ([USDA NASS](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Highlights/2024/Census22_HL_FarmsFarmland.pdf)). Sales concentrated faster than land: farms selling $1 million or more a year were 6% of farms in 2022, up from 4% in 2017, and made 78% of sales, up from 69% ([Combs et al., Choices](https://www.choicesmagazine.org/choices-magazine/submitted-articles/key-insights-from-the-2022-census-of-agriculture)).

Bigger doesn't mean corporate. The Agriculture Department counts a farm as a family farm when the farmer and relatives own most of the business, and in 2024 about 97% of farms were family farms. Nonfamily farms, about 3% of the total, produced about 14% of the value of farm output. Large family farms, with $1 million or more in gross cash farm income, were 5% of farms and produced half the output. Small family farms, under $350,000, were 86% of farms, worked 40% of the farmland and produced 17% ([USDA ERS, America's Farms and Ranches at a Glance, 2025 edition](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/20834/eib-299.pdf)).

Most farm households live mainly on other income. The department forecasts the median farm household's income at $108,460 in 2026. The median household's income from farming itself is forecast at a loss of about $467, and the median income from off the farm at about $94,000 ([USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/topics/farm-economy/farm-household-well-being/farm-household-income-forecast)). In 2024, 71% of farms had operating profit margins below 10%, which the department calls its high-risk zone ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/20834/eib-299.pdf)).

## Who owns the land

Just over 60% of the land in farms is worked by the people who own it, according to the 2022 Census. The rest is rented. The department's last full survey of farm landlords, in 2014, found that 39% of farmland was rented and that landlords who don't farm owned 30% of all farmland ([USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/topics/farm-economy/land-use-land-value-tenure/farmland-ownership-and-tenure)). The department publishes no estimate of how much farmland investment funds or other institutions own, and we found no reliable national figure.

Land has gotten more expensive. Cropland averaged $6,020 an acre in 2026, up 3.3% from 2025 and 21.8% from 2022, while cash rent for cropland averaged $160 an acre ([Farm Policy News, on USDA's Land Values report](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/2026/08/us-cropland-tops-6000-per-acre-for-first-time-in-2026/)).

**Foreign owners.** Foreign investors reported interests in 46.3 million acres of U.S. agricultural land in 2024, a figure often read as foreign ownership. About 16.3 million of those acres are long-term leases. University of Illinois economists who went through the records count about 6 million acres of confirmed foreign ownership, about 0.67% of the land in farms, which they call an upper bound because much of it is commercial timberland that the Census doesn't count as farmland. Of foreign-owned acres, 88.5% are forest. Canadians hold the most, 38%. Holdings tied to China, counting affiliated companies, total about 248,000 acres, about 0.5% of reported foreign interests ([Sherrick and Tsay, farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/09/foreign-ownership-of-us-agricultural-land.html)).

## What farmers buy: seed, fertilizer, machinery

**Seed.** Bayer and Corteva together sold more than half the retail seed for corn, soybeans and cotton in the U.S. from 2018 to 2020, the latest years the department has estimated. Worldwide, four companies, Bayer, Corteva, Syngenta Group and BASF, sell most crop seed and farm chemicals; six led those markets in 2015, and mergers contributed to the change ([USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/107516)).

**Fertilizer.** Fertilizer is a large part of what crops cost to grow: since 2020 it has been 33% to 44% of the operating costs of growing corn and 34% to 45% for wheat. Nitrogen fertilizer prices reached records in 2022, anhydrous ammonia above $1,600 a ton and urea above $1,000, then fell back but stayed above their levels before 2021 ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/111221)). University of Illinois economists attribute the 2022 shortage mainly to Russia's war in Ukraine, and note that, adjusted for inflation, the price spike of the mid-1970s was higher ([farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2023/09/how-historic-were-fertilizer-prices-in-2022.html)). Four companies, CF Industries, Koch, Yara and Nutrien, sell most of the nitrogen fertilizer bought in the U.S. ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/antitrust/doj-probes-us-fertilizer-market-for-possible-price-fixing-1)). In March 2026 Bloomberg reported, citing people familiar with the matter, that the Justice Department was investigating whether leading producers, those four and Mosaic, had colluded to raise prices (same source). The reports we found carried no comment from the companies; a CHS board member who farms in Minnesota said consolidation reflects how much capital fertilizer plants take, and an agricultural economist said the problem was too little supply worldwide ([Fertilizer Daily](https://www.fertilizerdaily.com/20260310-u-s-justice-department-opens-antitrust-probe-into-fertilizer-producers-amid-price-concerns/)).

**Machinery.** In July 2026 Deere settled a Federal Trade Commission lawsuit, joined by five states, alleging that it kept the software tools needed for many electronic repairs to its own dealers. For 10 years it must offer farmers and independent repair shops the same repair tools and information its dealers get; the settlement included no payment ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/07/ftc-states-secure-settlement-deere-company-advancing-farmers-right-repair)). In a separate class action by farmers who paid Deere's dealers for repairs, Deere agreed in April to pay $99 million into a settlement fund, without admitting wrongdoing; a judge has to approve it ([The Cattle Site](https://www.thecattlesite.com/news/deere-to-pay-99m-in-right-to-repair-settlement)). {{due 2027-02-01 "Has the court approved Deere's $99 million right-to-repair class settlement (N.D. In the fall of 2025 the Agriculture and Justice departments signed an agreement to look into farm input costs, naming feed, fertilizer, fuel, seed and equipment ([USDA](https://www.usda.gov/node/66573)).

## What farmers sell: crops

Much of what row-crop farms grow leaves the country. About 85% of the U.S. cotton crop was exported in the 2025–26 marketing year, and 38% of the winter wheat ([Gigafact, from USDA ERS data](https://gigafact.org/fact-briefs/is-about-80-of-the-cotton-and-40-of-the-winter-wheat-raised-in-the-united-states-exported/)), and the U.S. has supplied about 35% of the world's cotton exports in recent years ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/cotton-and-wool/cotton-sector-at-a-glance)). What those farms earn depends heavily on buyers abroad.

**Grain.** Bunge completed its merger with the grain trader Viterra in July 2025; analysts said the deal strengthened Bunge in U.S. grain exporting and oilseed processing, where it had been smaller than Archer-Daniels-Midland and Cargill ([The Poultry Site, via Reuters](https://www.thepoultrysite.com/news/2025/07/bunge-seals-34b-viterra-deal-to-rival-adm-cargill)). We found no government measure of how concentrated the buying of corn, soybeans or wheat is, nationally or near any given farm.

**Peanuts.** Peanuts have no futures market and no open cash market. Growers sell them to shellers, which clean, shell and sort them for food makers, and three large shellers dominate: Birdsong and Golden Peanut together hold roughly 80% of the market and Olam at least 10%, alongside about a dozen smaller shellers, some of them grower cooperatives. A federal loan program sets a floor of $355 a ton, unchanged since 2002, and shellers buy through contracts that add a premium the shellers set ([Bolotova, Choices](https://www.choicesmagazine.org/choices-magazine/submitted-articles/is-there-price-fixing-in-the-us-peanut-industry)). Georgia grew about 47% of the nation's peanut acres in 2025 ([University of Georgia](https://fieldreport.caes.uga.edu/publications/AP130-4-05/2026-peanut-outlook)). In 2019 runner peanut farmers sued the three large shellers, alleging that they conspired to pay depressed prices from 2014 through 2019. The shellers denied it and settled in 2021 for $102.75 million in all ([plaintiffs' memorandum, In re Peanut Farmers Antitrust Litigation](https://aglaw.psu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/In-Re-Peanut-Farmers-Ps-memorandum-of-motion-for-final-approval-of-settlement-6.7.21.pdf)). The farmers also alleged that the shellers had overstated their inventories in a USDA report, which the department later revised ([Bolotova, Choices](https://www.choicesmagazine.org/choices-magazine/submitted-articles/is-there-price-fixing-in-the-us-peanut-industry)).

## What farmers sell: the packers

The four largest beef packers bought 36% of the steers and heifers sold for slaughter in 1980, 81% by 1995 and about 85% in recent years. The four largest hog packers' share rose from 34% in 1980 to 67% in 2019 ([USDA ERS, Amber Waves](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/january/concentration-in-u-s-meatpacking-industry-and-how-it-affects-competition-and-cattle-prices)).

The four beef packers are JBS, Tyson Foods, Cargill and National Beef ([KATV](https://katv.com/news/nation-world/trump-admin-targets-big-4-meat-packers-antitrust-crackdown-beef-prices-pork-turkey-chicken-todd-blanche-brooke-rollins-peter-navarro-rancher-cattle-supply-chain-jbs-cargill-tyson-foods-national-beef-brazil)). Tyson and Cargill are American; JBS and National Beef have Brazilian owners ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/doj-reportedly-pursuing-criminal-antitrust-probe-major-meatpacking-companies)). The names on the package are often brands of the same few companies:

- **Tyson Foods** (Arkansas) says it produces about 20% of the beef, pork and chicken in the United States. Its brands include Tyson, Jimmy Dean, Hillshire Farm, Ball Park, Wright, Aidells and State Fair ([Tyson](https://ir.tyson.com/about-tyson/facts/)).
- **JBS**, founded in Brazil and the world's largest meatpacker, began trading on the New York Stock Exchange in June 2025 ([The Poultry Site](https://www.thepoultrysite.com/news/2025/06/jbs-begins-trading-on-nyse-after-batista-brothers-return)). It owns 82% of Pilgrim's Pride, whose chicken brands include Pilgrim's, Just Bare, Gold'n Plump, Gold Kist and Country Pride ([Pilgrim's Pride, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/802481/000080248126000011/ppc-20251228.htm)).
- **National Beef**: Brazil's Marfrig raised its stake to about 82% in 2019 ([Drovers](https://www.drovers.com/markets/marfrig-raises-stake-national-beef)), and merged with BRF in 2025 to form MBRF Global Foods ([Leaders League](https://www.leadersleague.com/en/news/marfrig-brf-beefing-up)). U.S. Premium Beef holds about 15% ([U.S. Premium Beef, quarterly report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001289237/000168316826008383/uspb_i10q-092626.htm)).
- **Smithfield Foods**, the pork and packaged-meat company, is 87% owned by Hong Kong's WH Group ([WH Group, SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000091388/000121390025109501/primary_doc.xml)).

## Cattle: from the farm to the packer

Most beef cattle start on cow-calf farms, which keep cows to raise calves, and most of those farms are small. The average beef cow herd was about 47 head in the 2022 Census; operations with 100 or more cows were 10.5% of the total and held 60.5% of the cows, and the department notes that operations with 50 cows or fewer likely rely on other income or off-farm jobs ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/animal-products/cattle-beef/sector-at-a-glance)). There are fewer of them: 622,162 farms had beef cows in 2022, 14.7% fewer than in 2017, and the number of farms with any cattle fell 17.1%, to 732,123 ([USDA NASS](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Highlights/2024/Census22_HL_Cattle%20and%20Cattle%20on%20Feed_final.pdf)).

Many aren't ranches in the Western sense. A family farm in Georgia might grow cotton, peanuts, corn or soybeans on part of its land and keep a cow-calf herd on pasture on the rest, selling its corn for feed; corn is more than 95% of the grain grown for feed in the U.S., and about 40% of the corn used here typically goes to feed animals ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/crops/corn-and-other-feed-grains/feed-grains-sector-at-a-glance)). A farm like that meets both necks of the hourglass. It buys seed and fertilizer from the few companies on one side, and the price its calves bring depends on the few packers on the other.

After weaning, calves are often grown on grass or forage and then fattened on grain in feedlots, most of them in the Great Plains. Feedlots are concentrated too: those selling 1,000 or more cattle a year are 7% of feedlots and sell about 88% of fed cattle ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/animal-products/cattle-beef/sector-at-a-glance)).

**How fattened cattle are sold.** In 2005 nearly half of fed cattle were sold in negotiated cash trades, in which a feedlot and a packer bargain over the price of cattle ready for slaughter; in 2025 fewer than 18% were. Most of the rest are sold under formulas and contracts, each priced from a base price, and a cattle market analyst says smaller independent feeders can't fully see what's happening in the market. In October 2026 the department began a regional cash cattle report for Colorado, Kansas, New Mexico, Oklahoma and Texas, where large feedlots sell mostly by contract ([DTN](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/livestock/article/2026/10/26/cash-trade-shrinks-usda-expands)). The Wall Street Journal reported in April, citing unnamed sources, that antitrust investigators had examined contracts that refer to a pricing benchmark some ranchers say is manipulated ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/doj-reportedly-pursuing-criminal-antitrust-probe-major-meatpacking-companies)).

**Who gets the beef dollar.** Cattle producers' share of the retail value of Choice beef was 36.8% in 2021 and 50.2% in 2024; in September 2026 it was 49.3%, $4.91 of a retail value of $9.94 a pound ([USDA ERS, Meat Price Spreads](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/meat-price-spreads)). Packers' margins moved the other way. Sterling Marketing's tracker put them at about $464 a head in 2020 and, by its December 2021 estimate, about $632 in 2021 ([Sterling Marketing, via Farm Journal](https://cdn.farmjournal.com/inline-files/Beef%20Tracker%20123121.pdf)); in late June 2026 it put them at a loss of $314 a head, with a loss of about $220 a head projected for the year ([Agri-Pulse](https://www.agri-pulse.com/articles/24949-beef-packers-under-strain-as-us-cattle-herd-contracts)). Cattle are scarce: the January 2026 count, 86.2 million head, was the smallest since 1951 ([USDA NASS](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/printable/2026/01-30-2026.pdf); [AgWeb](https://www.agweb.com/news/livestock/beef/u-s-beef-herd-continues-downward-86-2-million-head)). Fed steers averaged $224 per hundred pounds in 2025, and in October the department forecast $238 for 2026; it also said weaker cattle prices and strong wholesale beef prices had begun to support packers' margins ([USDA ERS, Livestock, Dairy, and Poultry Outlook](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/29753/ldp-m-388.pdf)).

{{figure kind=bars title="Cattle producers' share of the retail value of Choice beef" rows="2021=36.8;2024=50.2;September 2026=49.3" fmt="{v}%" caption="Net farm value as a share of the retail value of a pound of Choice beef." source="[USDA ERS, Meat Price Spreads](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/meat-price-spreads)"}}

**Fewer plants.** In November 2025 Tyson said it would close its beef plant in Lexington, Nebraska, which processed 5,000 head a day, about 4.8% of daily U.S. slaughter, and cut its Amarillo, Texas, plant to one shift ([DTN](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/livestock/article/2025/11/24/tyson-close-5-000-head-day-nebraska)). JBS said in June 2026 that it would close a beef plant in Souderton, Pennsylvania, and analysts at Allendale estimated that about 10,000 head a day of capacity had gone offline ([Agri-Pulse](https://www.agri-pulse.com/articles/24949-beef-packers-under-strain-as-us-cattle-herd-contracts)).

**What the department found in 2020.** A fire in August 2019 closed Tyson's plant in Holcomb, Kansas, about 5% to 6% of U.S. beef capacity, for about four months. The gap between the price of fed cattle and the wholesale value of beef peaked at $67 per hundred pounds two weeks later, against $28 in the same week of 2016 to 2018. In the spring of 2020, with nearly 40% of processing capacity idle at the worst point, it reached about $279, the widest since mandatory price reporting began in 2001. The department's report made no finding that packers had broken the law and said its investigation was continuing. It recommended considering minimum levels of negotiated cash purchases, which would need new authority from Congress, and a library of packers' contracts ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/CattleandBeefPriceMarginReport.pdf)).

## Chicken: the company owns the birds

Chicken works differently. In 2020, 99.5% of the value of broilers, the chickens raised for meat, was produced under contracts in which a company owns the chicks and supplies the feed, and a farmer supplies the barns, equipment, utilities and labor. Growers are paid by the pound and ranked against other growers delivering to the same plant the same week: those whose flocks cost less than average get a premium over the base pay, and the rest a deduction. The median grower was paid 6.79 cents a pound in 2020; the bottom tenth got 4.29 cents and the top tenth 9.64 ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/104642)).

In January 2025 the department issued a rule on how those payments are figured and what growers must be told before they make major investments in their farms. In June 2026 it postponed the rule's start from July 1, 2026, to December 31, 2027, citing its costs, legal and policy concerns raised in comments, and direction from Congress ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/01/2026-10880/poultry-grower-payment-systems-and-capital-improvement-systems-delay-of-effective-date)). In July it proposed repealing two other rules under the Packers and Stockyards Act, the law that polices how meatpackers and poultry companies deal with farmers ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/07/07/usda-delays-poultry-rule-proposes-rescinding-packers-stockyards-reforms/)). {{due 2027-12-31 "Did the poultry grower payment rule take effect on Dec 31, 2027, or was it delayed again or withdrawn? ## How much of the food dollar reaches the farm

Of each dollar Americans spent on food in 2024, counting restaurant meals, 11.8 cents went to farms: 18.5 cents of each dollar spent on food at home and 7.1 cents of each dollar spent eating out. The farm share pays for the farm's own fuel, fertilizer and other supplies; what farms kept as value added was 6.7 cents. The department revised the series in 2026, counting soft drinks and bottled water as food, which lowered the farm share, so older figures aren't comparable ([USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/data-products/food-dollar/summary-findings)).

## What courts and regulators have done

**A guilty plea.** Pilgrim's Pride pleaded guilty in 2021 to conspiring to fix prices and rig bids for broiler chicken from 2012 to at least 2017, and was sentenced to a criminal fine of about $108 million ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/one-nation-s-largest-chicken-producers-pleads-guilty-price-fixing-and-sentenced-107-million)).

**A settlement on shared data.** In May 2026 Agri Stats, a company that compiled sales and price data for chicken, pork and turkey processors, settled a lawsuit by the Justice Department and six states alleging that sharing that data among competitors let them coordinate and raise prices. It agreed to stop sharing recent, detailed sales and price figures only among processors, to make its reports available to any buyer and to pay the states ([Minnesota Attorney General](https://ag.state.mn.us/Office/Communications/2026/05/07_AgriStats.asp)).

**Beef: investigated, not charged.** The Justice Department closed an earlier investigation of beef packers in September 2025 and opened a new civil antitrust investigation that November ([U.S. Premium Beef, quarterly report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001289237/000168316826008383/uspb_i10q-092626.htm)), after the president asked for one ([Farm Policy News](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/2025/11/doj-usda-investigating-meatpackers-over-beef-prices/)); in April 2026 the Wall Street Journal reported, citing unnamed sources, that the inquiry also had a criminal side ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/doj-reportedly-pursuing-criminal-antitrust-probe-major-meatpacking-companies)). No charges have been reported. In private class actions alleging that the packers cut slaughter to push cattle prices down and beef prices up, JBS, Tyson and Cargill have agreed to settlements, and Tyson and Cargill denied wrongdoing ([Cattle Range](https://www.cattlerange.com/articles/2025/10/tyson-foods-cargill-agree-to-pay-87-5-million-in-beef-price-fixing-settlement)); in July 2026 the court in Minnesota certified classes of cattle sellers and beef buyers, and an appeals court agreed to hear the packers' challenge (same quarterly report). ## What the research says

The department summarized decades of research on meatpacking in 2024. Studies from the 1990s and 2000s found that bigger packing plants lowered processing costs, which raised demand and the prices packers paid for cattle and hogs. Most also found that packers used some market power to pay less for cattle, but that its effect was more than offset by the lower costs. After 2015, the gap between what packers paid for cattle and what they got for beef grew more volatile and rose to two to three times its earlier average. The department attributes that mainly to packing plants running out of spare capacity, along with closures from a plant fire and the pandemic: "Something appears to have changed after 2015," it wrote ([USDA ERS, Amber Waves](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2024/january/concentration-in-u-s-meatpacking-industry-and-how-it-affects-competition-and-cattle-prices)).

A 2021 study edited by Texas A&M economists, which the department commissioned at the request of the House Agriculture Committee's leaders, reached a similar conclusion: packers have market power, its effect on prices has been small, and the cost savings of large plants largely offset it. It found the four largest packers' share had averaged 84.6% over the previous decade ([Texas A&M, The U.S. Beef Supply Chain](https://afpc.tamu.edu/research/publications/710/cattle.pdf)).

We found no national count of how many farms, or how much farmland, private equity or other investment funds own, so this page doesn't say whether the buying described in the rest of this series has reached farms themselves.

## Where the analogy breaks

Sand doesn't bargain; farmers and packers do, and the companies in the neck sell to grocery chains and restaurant companies that are large buyers themselves. A narrower neck isn't simply worse, either: the department's review found that the big plants that narrowed it also lowered costs. And the neck isn't equally narrow for everything farms grow: peanuts pass through a few shellers, while most cotton and much of the wheat is sold onto world markets. This page doesn't cover fruit and vegetables.

## What people argue about

**Power or scale.** One side: the administration, which asked for the investigation, says concentration in meatpacking has hurt cattle producers, and the Agriculture Secretary called the foreign ownership of half the largest packers a threat to them ([KATV](https://katv.com/news/nation-world/trump-admin-targets-big-4-meat-packers-antitrust-crackdown-beef-prices-pork-turkey-chicken-todd-blanche-brooke-rollins-peter-navarro-rancher-cattle-supply-chain-jbs-cargill-tyson-foods-national-beef-brazil)), and Farm Action, an advocacy group, says the meatpacking system is broken and needs a lasting fix ([Farm Policy News](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/2025/11/doj-usda-investigating-meatpackers-over-beef-prices/)). The other: the Meat Institute, the packers' trade group, says packers have been losing money because cattle are scarce, and that the industry is heavily regulated and its trading transparent ([Meat Institute](https://mail.meatinstitute.org/node/917)); Glynn Tonsor, an economist at Kansas State, points to strong demand for beef and said in November 2025 that the ownership of meatpacking had changed little in four years ([Farm Policy News](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/2025/11/doj-usda-investigating-meatpackers-over-beef-prices/)). The department's own review found both market power and cost savings, and a change after 2015 that it ties mainly to plant capacity.

**Cash trade.** Supporters of requiring packers to buy a minimum share of their cattle in negotiated cash trades say it would improve price discovery for independent cattle feeders; Senator Chuck Grassley's "50/14" bill, first introduced in 2020, would have required the major packers to buy at least half their cattle that way, and it didn't advance ([DTN](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/livestock/article/2026/10/26/cash-trade-shrinks-usda-expands)). The department's 2020 report suggested considering such minimums ([USDA AMS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/CattleandBeefPriceMarginReport.pdf)). The Texas A&M economists say more cash trade wouldn't necessarily raise cattle prices and could impose costs that are passed down to cattle producers ([Texas A&M](https://afpc.tamu.edu/research/publications/710/cattle.pdf)), and the American Farm Bureau Federation's delegates have opposed a mandatory minimum; its economist wrote in 2020 that better price discovery doesn't necessarily mean higher prices ([American Farm Bureau Federation](https://www.fb.org/market-intel/examining-cattle-transactions-in-the-u-s)).

**Inputs.** The administration says high and volatile costs for seed, fertilizer, equipment and other inputs burden farmers, and it has made them a joint concern of the Agriculture and Justice departments ([USDA](https://www.usda.gov/node/66573)). A CHS board member who farms in Minnesota and an agricultural economist say consolidation reflects the cost of building plants, and that prices follow world supply and shipping ([Fertilizer Daily](https://www.fertilizerdaily.com/20260310-u-s-justice-department-opens-antitrust-probe-into-fertilizer-producers-amid-price-concerns/)).

**Chicken contracts.** Supporters of the delayed poultry rule, such as Food & Water Watch, say it would stop unfair and deceptive treatment of growers ([FoodNavigator](https://www.foodnavigator.com/Article/2026/07/07/usda-delays-poultry-rule-proposes-rescinding-packers-stockyards-reforms/)). The department, in delaying it, cited its costs and the legal and policy concerns raised in comments ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/01/2026-10880/poultry-grower-payment-systems-and-capital-improvement-systems-delay-of-effective-date)).

**Foreign land.** The 46.3 million acre figure is often quoted as foreign ownership of farmland. The Illinois economists say most of it is leases and unconfirmed records, and that confirmed ownership is well under 1% of farmland, mostly forest ([farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2026/09/foreign-ownership-of-us-agricultural-land.html)).
