## Short version

Amazon Prime can save a household money. For someone who orders a couple of times a month, can't wait a week, and would pay for a streaming service anyway, $139 a year is easy to earn back. For someone who orders now and then and can group items into one order over the minimum, Prime mostly buys speed. The part the break-even math misses is the spending. Prime members order about twice as often as other Amazon customers, and in card data from 2015–2018, first-time members doubled their Amazon spending in their first year. Some of that is likely shopping moved from other stores. Some is probably buying more. If even a small share of the extra is things you wouldn't otherwise have bought, it can cost more than the fee. The test is simple: write down what Prime is actually saving you, and see whether it beats what it may be costing.

## The trap in one sentence

You judge a membership by whether you get your money's worth out of the fee, and miss that a membership built to make you shop more can cost more through the shopping than through the fee.

## How it works

**The fee.** [Prime costs $139 a year, or $14.99 a month](https://recurdash.com/subscription-pricing/amazon-prime). Paying monthly comes to $179.88 a year, about $41 more for the same thing. Discounted plans exist: [Prime for Young Adults](https://aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-prime-for-young-adults) (ages 18 to 24, or college students) is $7.49 a month or $69 a year, and [Prime Access](https://aboutamazon.com/news/retail/amazon-prime-access-discounted-membership), for people on programs such as SNAP or Medicaid, is $6.99 a month. The US price [hasn't changed since 2022](https://www.thestreet.com/retail/amazon-prime-is-overdue-for-a-price-increase), but Amazon raised Prime prices in [Mexico](https://www.xataka.com.mx/streaming/amazon-prime-sube-precio-mexico-primera-vez-2016-estos-nuevos-costos) and [Australia](https://www.finder.com.au/news/amazon-prime-just-got-more-expensive-australia-2026) in September and October, and [analysts have predicted a US increase](https://www.foxbusiness.com/retail/amazon-prime-members-brace-sticker-shock-analysts-predict-imminent-price-hike).

**What you'd pay without it.** Without Prime, Amazon ships most of its own items free once an order reaches a minimum. [Amazon doesn't publish the number](https://www.amazon.com/gp/help/customer/display.html?nodeId=GZXW7X6AKTHNUP6H); since 2023 it has been [$35 in most places and $25 in some ZIP codes](https://www.checkbook.org/national/consumers-notebook/articles/Amazon-Raises-Minimum-Purchase-Requirement-for-Free-Shipping-in-Some-Zip-Codes-7817), with [books at $25](https://www.dontpayfull.com/explore/amazon-free-shipping). Standard delivery is slower, [often about a week](https://parcelpath.com/ecommerce/amazon/amazon-fees/how-do-i-get-free-shipping-on-amazon/). Orders below the minimum [pay a shipping charge](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/amazons-free-shipping-minimum-shoots-up-10-for-some-non-prime-customers-083123.html). So the shipping value of Prime depends on two things: how many small orders you place that you couldn't combine, and how much faster delivery is worth to you. If you can wait and group items, the shipping value can be close to zero.

**The perks count only if you'd pay for them anyway.** Prime includes Prime Video, but with ads; [removing most of them costs $4.99 a month more](https://www.aboutamazon.com/md/news/entertainment/prime-video-ultra-ad-free-streaming-subscription) since April 2026 ($45.99 a year for annual members). [Prime Video alone](https://www.dealnews.com/features/amazon/prime-video-cost/), without shipping, is $8.99 a month. If you'd otherwise pay for that, it's real money. If you watch Prime Video because it's there, it isn't a saving. The same goes for the music service, the food-delivery perk and the rest.

**The spending.** Research firms that track Amazon customers have found the same pattern for years. Consumer Intelligence Research Partners (CIRP) [estimates](https://cirpamazon.substack.com/p/prime-drives-annual-spending-and) that Prime members spend about twice as much at Amazon as other customers, mostly because they order about twice a month instead of about once. Bloomberg Second Measure, which studies card transactions, [found](https://secondmeasure.com/datapoints/amazon-prime-day-target-walmart/) that people who joined Prime for the first time between 2015 and 2018 more than doubled their Amazon spending in their first year, from about $600 to over $1,200. People who joined in 2018 also spent more at Walmart and Target than the year before, not less, so at least at those two stores the Amazon jump didn't come at their expense. Second Measure's own guess is that people tend to join Prime when they have more money to spend.

None of this proves Prime makes any one person spend more. People often join when they expect to buy more anyway (a move, a new baby, a new business). CIRP [has asked](https://cirpamazon.substack.com/p/what-makes-amazon-prime-members-so) whether Prime makes heavy shoppers or heavy shoppers join Prime, and says the answer is good news for Amazon either way, though its more recent reports lean toward Prime being the cause. But the mechanisms are familiar: once the fee is paid, there's a pull to "get your money's worth"; free, fast shipping removes the pause that used to stop a small purchase; and when one store is the default, comparing prices fades.

**Leaving was made hard, too.** In September 2025, Amazon [agreed to pay $2.5 billion](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-secures-historic-25-billion-settlement-against-amazon) to settle Federal Trade Commission charges that it signed people up for Prime without clear consent and made canceling difficult. Amazon did not admit wrongdoing. More on that below.

## A worked example

Two people, each paying $139 a year for Prime. The shipping charge, the number of small orders and the extra spending are made up for the example.

**Sam** places about 24 orders a year. Ten are small things needed quickly that couldn't wait to be grouped into an order over the minimum; say each would have cost $6 to ship. Sam would pay for Prime Video on its own if Prime didn't include it.

**Pat** places about 8 orders a year, almost all over the minimum, and doesn't watch Prime Video.

{{illustration name=prime-ledger caption="What Prime replaces for Sam and Pat, against the $139 fee, and Sam again once a share of the extra spending counts."}}

For Sam, Prime replaces $60 of shipping (10 × $6) and $107.88 of streaming ($8.99 × 12), $167.88 in all. On the fee alone, Sam comes out about $29 ahead. For Pat it replaces nothing: Pat is paying $139 for speed.

Now the part the fee math leaves out. Suppose Sam's Amazon spending rises by $600 a year after joining, as in the Second Measure data, and a quarter of that ($150) is things Sam wouldn't otherwise have bought. Sam is now $121.12 behind, even though the fee "paid for itself."

The quarter is a guess. Nobody can measure that share for a stranger. That's the point: it's the number that decides the answer, and only you can check it.

## How to avoid it

**Do the math once, honestly.** List what Prime replaces: shipping on small orders you couldn't have combined, and services you'd pay for anyway. Leave out perks you use only because they're included.

**Pay annually if you keep it.** Monthly billing costs about $41 more a year. If you only want Prime for a holiday rush or a sale, one month and a reminder to cancel is cheaper than a year.

**Check for a discounted plan.** Young adults and people on certain assistance programs can pay about half.

**Run a three-month test.** Cancel, or let a trial end. Keep a list of everything you would have ordered. At the end, look at what you bought anyway, what you bought somewhere else, and what you decided you didn't need. The third column is the number the fee math can't give you.

**Put a pause back in.** If you keep Prime, leave things in the cart for a day, or batch orders to one day a week. Fast shipping is a convenience; the pause before buying is what Prime takes away.

**Compare the alternatives.** [Walmart+ costs $98 a year ($12.95 a month)](https://clark.com/shopping-retail/walmart-plus/), and some stores ship free above a minimum without any membership. The cheapest membership is the one that matches how you already shop.

**Read the statement.** A membership you meant to cancel keeps charging (see [reading your statements](reading-your-statements.html), and [subscriptions](subscription-creep.html) for the rest of them). If you signed up through certain Amazon pages, or tried and failed to cancel, between June 23, 2019 and June 23, 2025, and used few Prime benefits, you may be owed an [FTC refund](https://ftc.gov/Amazon) (more below). Refunds are automatic; there is no form, and anyone asking you to pay to get one is a scammer.

## The bigger picture

The argument over Prime isn't about whether it's a good product. Most members renew, and CIRP [describes Prime's retention](https://cirpamazon.substack.com/p/amazon-prime-retention-rates-are) as extraordinarily high. It's about how subscriptions are sold and how hard they are to leave.

The FTC [sued Amazon in 2023](https://amp.claimsjournal.com/news/national/2025/09/25/333169.htm), saying it used confusing "dark pattern" designs to enroll shoppers in Prime and a hard-to-finish cancellation process to keep them. Shortly before trial, [a judge ruled](https://www.thestar.com.my/tech/tech-news/2025/09/18/us-ftc-wins-key-rulings-against-amazon-ahead-of-trial-on-prime-signups) that Amazon broke the law by collecting billing information before disclosing Prime's terms. In September 2025, days into the trial, Amazon settled for $2.5 billion: a $1 billion civil penalty, [the largest the FTC has won in a rule-violation case](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-secures-historic-25-billion-settlement-against-amazon), and $1.5 billion in refunds. Amazon also agreed to add a clear button for declining Prime and to make canceling no harder than signing up. Amazon did not admit wrongdoing and said its practices had always been lawful. In September 2026 a court approved a [revised order](https://www.ftc.gov/node/336860) that raised the maximum refund from $51 to $200 and extended automatic refunds to more members starting October 1, 2026. As of September 2026, the FTC said, Amazon had paid more than $845 million. Members who accepted a refund may get up to $149 more beginning by April 2027 if payouts fall short of the required total by February 2027.

The settlement drew both praise and criticism. Former FTC Chair Lina Khan, who brought the case, [called it](https://theweek.com/politics/amazon-prime-ftc-settlement) a "drop in the bucket for Amazon," and Sen. Elizabeth Warren [said it failed](https://www.foxbusiness.com/technology/amazon-settles-ftc-lawsuit-prime-subscription-practices-record-2-5b-settlement) to hold Amazon executives accountable. The FTC's current chairman, Andrew Ferguson, called it a record-breaking win for consumers.

The wider rules are unsettled. The FTC finished a "click-to-cancel" rule for all subscriptions in 2024, but [a federal appeals court threw it out](https://natlawreview.com/article/eighth-circuit-vacates-ftcs-click-cancel-rule) on procedural grounds in July 2025, days before it took effect. The FTC [began the process again](https://www.cov.com/en/news-and-insights/insights/2026/03/ftc-launches-new-rulemaking-on-the-negative-option-rule) in early 2026, and more than half the states have their own automatic-renewal laws in the meantime. The [subscriptions page](subscription-creep.html) has more on those rules.

**Defenders of memberships like Prime** argue that people choose them freely and renew at high rates because they value them; that free, fast shipping costs real money to provide, and a flat fee is an efficient way to pay for it; and that convenience is worth something even when it doesn't show up as savings. Amazon [has said](https://theweek.com/politics/amazon-prime-ftc-settlement) its sign-up and cancellation process is clear and simple and that it has always followed the law.

**Critics** argue that a membership built to raise how much people spend isn't a savings program, whatever the ads say; that the FTC alleged the company knew sign-up and cancellation were confusing; and that a payment equal to a small fraction of a year's revenue doesn't change incentives.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 14, 2026.
