## The short version

Under net metering, a home with solar panels gets credit for the power it sends to the grid at the same price it pays for power it takes back, so the meter in effect runs backward. Forty-three states and Washington, D.C., have required it of their regulated utilities at some point. By the end of 2024, twelve states had replaced it with arrangements that pay less for exported power, and Illinois followed in January 2025. The share of U.S. households living in a state that requires net metering fell from about 84% in 2014 to about 57% at the end of 2023. California made the biggest change: since April 2023, new solar customers of its three big utilities are paid for exports at rates that average about 75% less than retail, while earlier customers keep their old terms for 20 years. The argument is about who pays for the grid. California's state ratepayer advocate says solar owners' bill savings pushed about $8.5 billion of grid costs onto everyone else in 2024, and the state utility commission's October 2026 report, using the utilities' own estimates, put it at about $8.3 billion in 2025. Solar groups say the math ignores what rooftop power saves the system. A national laboratory study found the possible effect on other customers' bills was under $1 a month in most states, but about $19 a month in California.

## The analogy

A bank that takes deposits of electricity. Under net metering, the bank credits a kilowatt-hour you deposit at the same price it charges for one you withdraw (on a time-of-use plan, the price for that time of day), and lets you carry a balance from sunny months to dark ones. Under the newer rules, the bank still sells you a kilowatt-hour at the retail price but pays you closer to what it would have cost the bank to get that power elsewhere, which is often much less, and more or less depending on the hour.

The fight is over the bank's overhead. Much of what a utility spends on poles, wires and power plants is recovered in the price of each kilowatt-hour. A household that buys fewer kilowatt-hours pays less toward that overhead, and someone else picks up the difference, unless the power it sends back saves the utility enough to make up for it.

## How net metering works

A solar system makes the most power in the middle of the day, often more than the house is using, and nothing at night. The extra flows out to the grid and is used by the neighbors; at night the house draws power back. Under net metering, the utility subtracts what went out from what came in and bills the difference, usually rolling any extra credit forward month to month and settling up once a year, at what's called the true-up.

The policy began as a way to get a young industry started. Forty-three states and Washington, D.C., have required regulated utilities to offer it at some point, according to a 2025 study by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory, which the Energy Department renamed the National Laboratory of the Rockies in December 2025 ([NREL](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy25osti/91888.pdf); [Energy Department](https://www.energy.gov/eere/articles/energy-department-renames-nrel-national-lab-rockies)). In about the past decade, more than a third of states have restructured their rules ([The New York Times, via The Planning Report](https://www.planningreport.com/2026/08/04/california-has-reset-economics-rooftop-solar-math-less-appealing)). The lab counted twelve that had replaced net metering outright by the end of 2024, most with some form of what's called net billing: power used on the spot still saves the full retail price, but power sent to the grid earns a lower credit. Illinois became the 13th in January 2025 ([NREL](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy25osti/91888.pdf); [220 ILCS 5/16-107.5](https://ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/documents/022000050K16-107.5.htm)). As states made those changes, the share of U.S. households in a state requiring net metering fell from about 84% in 2014 to about 57% by the end of 2023, before Arkansas's change took effect in September 2024 and Illinois's in January 2025 ([NREL](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy25osti/91888.pdf)).

## What changed in California

California's three investor-owned utilities, Pacific Gas and Electric, Southern California Edison and San Diego Gas & Electric, offered net metering from 1996 ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/NEM)). In December 2022 the state's Public Utilities Commission replaced it for new customers with what it calls the Net Billing Tariff, often called NEM 3.0, for interconnection applications from April 15, 2023 ([PG&E advice letter](https://www.pge.com/tariffs/assets/pdf/adviceletter/ELEC_6848-E.pdf)). Exports are now credited at hourly values from the commission's Avoided Cost Calculator, its estimate of what the power is worth to the utility at that hour. Across most hours of the year that comes to about 75% less than the retail price ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/NEM); [Canary Media](https://canarymedia.com/articles/batteries/california-regulators-cut-incentives-for-rooftop-solar-even-further)). The credit is highest on late-summer and early-fall evenings, when the grid is most strained and other solar has faded, so power stored in a battery at midday and used or sent out in the evening is worth more than power sent out at noon. Installers have sold batteries on exactly that argument ([Canary Media](https://canarymedia.com/articles/batteries/california-regulators-cut-incentives-for-rooftop-solar-even-further)), and California and Hawaii now have the highest shares of new home systems sold with one ([Berkeley Lab, via pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/?p=22399)). In effect, net metering let owners use the grid as a battery: power sent out at midday came back as a bill credit at night, or months later. Net billing pays far less for that stored power, so a real battery became worth more, and nearly 70% of net billing customers had paired one with their solar by the end of 2024 ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/NEM)).

Earlier customers keep their old terms. Under a 2014 decision, customers on the earlier tariffs can stay on them for 20 years from the date they were connected, then move to the net billing tariff ([PG&E](https://PGE.com/assets/pge/docs/clean-energy/solar/nem-faq.pdf)), and a new customer on the net billing tariff is guaranteed it for nine years ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/NEM)). The 20 years go with the system, not the owner, so a buyer of a house with older panels inherits them ([PG&E](https://PGE.com/assets/pge/docs/clean-energy/solar/nem-faq.pdf)). Those earlier customers aren't all on the same terms. Both earlier tariffs credit exports at the retail price the customer pays for power at that time of day, so on a time-of-use plan the credit rises and falls with that plan's prices. The original tariff, known as NEM 1.0, which closed at each utility in 2016 or 2017, works with any rate plan, and the small public-program charges every customer pays are figured on what the house uses, net, over a year. NEM 2.0, which ran from then until April 2023, requires a time-of-use plan and a one-time interconnection fee of $94 to $145, and those charges apply to the power drawn from the grid each hour ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/NEM)). The state's Public Advocates Office has proposed moving older customers to the new tariff after 10 years or when the house sells ([Reuters, via ThePrint](https://theprint.in/environment/california-rooftop-solar-subsidy-to-cost-8-5-billion-a-year-says-ratepayer-advocate/2234683/)). A 2025 bill that would have moved buyers of solar homes to the new tariff was amended that July to drop the idea, and it never became law ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2025/07/16/california-scores-victory-for-rooftop-solar-owners-retaining-contracts-on-home-sale/); [AB 942](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB942)).

## What it means for a household

For a house that already has solar, the date it was connected usually decides the deal, often for 10 to 20 years. In California, Nevada and Arizona, neighbors with the same panels can be on three or four different sets of terms, depending on when each connected. For a house getting solar now in a net billing state, the power the house uses as it's made is worth the most, and the power it sends out is worth the least, so a battery that stores midday power for the evening, or panels facing west, can matter more than adding panels.

Part-time residents face a version of this of their own. A system sized for a house that is lived in all year sends most of its output to the grid in the months the house is empty. Under retail net metering, those credits roll forward to the months the owners are home, at least until the annual true-up. Under net billing, power sent out while the house is empty earns the lower export credit, so the same panels save less. Where leftover credits are settled at the true-up, the month that settlement falls in matters too: in Oregon, unused credits go to low-income assistance at the true-up, usually in March.

For households without solar, the question is what share of grid costs they end up covering, which is the argument below.

## Where the analogy breaks

A bank holds your deposit until you take it out. The grid doesn't store anything: the power your panels send out at noon is used by a neighbor within seconds, and its value depends on what it replaces at that moment. When a lot of rooftop solar is sending power at once, on a mild spring afternoon, the next kilowatt-hour is worth little; on a hot evening, when demand peaks and other solar has faded, it can be worth a lot. Net metering credits power at the retail price, which is either the same all day or set in a few broad time-of-use blocks, not at what the power is worth at that moment. That gap is the heart of both sides' case.

## What people argue about

**Whether there is a cost shift, and how big.** California's Public Advocates Office, the utility customers' advocate inside the state's Public Utilities Commission, estimates that net metering cost customers of the three big utilities without solar about $8.5 billion in 2024, up from $3.4 billion in 2021, because solar households' bill savings exceed what their power saves the utilities ([Public Advocates Office](https://www.publicadvocates.cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cal-advocates-website/files/press-room/reports-and-analyses/241216-public-advocates-office-nem-history-fact-sheet.pdf)). The commission's October 2026 report to the Legislature, drawing on the three big utilities' own estimates, put the cost at about $8.3 billion in 2025 ([California Public Utilities Commission, 2026 SB 695 report](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cpuc-website/divisions/office-of-governmental-affairs-division/reports/2026/2026-sb-695-report---final.pdf)). The Utility Reform Network, a nonprofit consumer group, urged regulators in the 2022 case to move existing solar customers to the new tariff eight years after they connected ([California Public Utilities Commission, D.23-10-013](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/Published/G000/M520/K547/520547180.PDF)). Severin Borenstein, a University of California, Berkeley, economist, puts the 2024 figure nearer $4 billion, still a cost to other customers ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/californias-rooftop-solar-debate-is-raging-again)). A study by the consulting firm M.Cubed, written with and paid for by the California Solar & Storage Association, found that rooftop solar saved other customers about $1.5 billion in 2024 once its savings to the system are counted; M.Cubed's Richard McCann corrected an earlier figure of $2.3 billion ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/californias-rooftop-solar-debate-is-raging-again); [M.Cubed for CALSSA](https://mcubedecon.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/calssa_rooftop-solar-reduces-costs-for-all-ratepayers-2025.pdf)). The Public Advocates Office, responding to the study's first version in November 2024, said it misunderstands what the cost shift measures ([Public Advocates Office](https://www.publicadvocates.cpuc.ca.gov/press-room/commentary/241125-nem-cost-shift-rebuttal)), and M.Cubed's author answers that nearly half of the advocate's figure counts power that owners use themselves ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/californias-rooftop-solar-debate-is-raging-again)).

**Whether it matters outside California.** The national laboratory estimated how much net metering could raise other customers' bills in each state, measured at the end of 2023 for states that kept net metering and in the year of the change for the 12 that revised it. It found less than $1 a month in 38 of the 44 places studied (43 states and D.C.), including 9 of the 12 states that revised; the estimates were above $5 a month only in Connecticut ($6), Hawaii ($7) and California ($19) ([NREL](https://docs.nrel.gov/docs/fy25osti/91888.pdf)). The authors found no consistent link between how big the effect was and when states changed their rules ([O'Shaughnessy and others, Environmental Research: Energy](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/2583547)). Utilities in states that cut credits early argued for acting before the shift grew: an Arizona Public Service executive wrote in 2016 that, if it wasn't fixed, "that number will only grow" ([The Arizona Republic](https://www.azcentral.com/story/opinion/op-ed/2016/11/15/aps-solar-net-metering/93064656/)). In California, a 2013 study for the state commission projected a shift of about $1.1 billion a year by 2020 counting all rooftop output, or about $370 million counting only exports ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cpuc-website/files/uploadedfiles/cpuc_website/content/utilities_and_industries/energy/reports_and_white_papers/nemreportwithappendices.pdf)).

**What rooftop power is worth.** Solar groups say rooftop power has held down demand on the grid, avoiding new power plants and transmission lines, and that cutting credits has slowed adoption of something that benefits everyone ([California Solar & Storage Association](https://calssa.org/press-releases/2025/2/18/new-data-reveals-the-real-source-of-record-high-energy-rates-in-california-is-out-of-control-utility-spending-and-profits); [M.Cubed for CALSSA](https://mcubedecon.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/calssa_rooftop-solar-reduces-costs-for-all-ratepayers-2025.pdf)). The Public Advocates Office says that value has shrunk as midday solar outstrips demand, and that rooftop solar under net metering cost 3.6 times as much in 2024 as power from large renewable projects did in 2023 ([Public Advocates Office](https://www.publicadvocates.cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cal-advocates-website/files/press-room/reports-and-analyses/241216-public-advocates-office-nem-history-fact-sheet.pdf)).

**Who benefits.** Critics, including Borenstein, say solar owners are more likely to own their homes and have higher incomes, so the shift runs from renters and lower-income households toward better-off ones ([IVN](https://ivn.us/how-californians-without-solar-got-stuck-paying-those-who-do-2025-08-19/)); the state commission reached a similar finding in its 2022 decision ([California Public Utilities Commission, D.22-12-056](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/Published/G000/M500/K043/500043682.PDF)). Berkeley Lab's data show adopters' median income, measured in 2025, falling from $145,000 for 2010 installations to $122,000 for 2024 ones, though that is still well above the typical household's ([Berkeley Lab](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/distributed_solar_storage-2025_data_update.pdf)). The California Solar & Storage Association says the real driver of rising bills is utility spending on the grid, on which utilities earn a return ([California Solar & Storage Association](https://calssa.org/press-releases/2025/2/18/new-data-reveals-the-real-source-of-record-high-energy-rates-in-california-is-out-of-control-utility-spending-and-profits)).
