## Short version

Consumer protection in the United States is not one agency; it's a hundred years of patches. The Federal Trade Commission polices most advertising and sales practices, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau covers financial products, bank regulators watch the banks themselves, and every state has its own laws and an attorney general to enforce them. Since early 2025 the federal side has been reshaped: the CFPB has been cut back sharply while courts sort out how far that can go, the FTC remains active on hidden fees and subscriptions, and states have been taking on more of the work. This page keeps track of who does what, and it is dated — check the date stamps.

## A century of referees, briefly

**1914: unfair competition.** Congress created the [Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/about-ftc/history) to police "unfair methods of competition." In 1938, the Wheeler-Lea Act extended that to ["unfair or deceptive acts or practices"](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/45) — the first general federal rule that you can't trick a customer, known since as Section 5. It is still the workhorse of federal consumer protection.

**1968–1977: the disclosure decade.** A wave of laws built the modern rulebook: the [Truth in Lending Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1601) (1968) made lenders state a loan's cost as an annual percentage rate; the [Fair Credit Billing Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1666) (1974) created the billing-dispute rights on every card statement; the [Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/2301) (1975) governed warranties and anchored the special procedures the FTC must follow when it writes certain rules — a framework, tightened in 1980, that still decides cases today; and the [Fair Debt Collection Practices Act](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1692) (1977) limited what collectors can do. In the same years, nearly every state passed its own unfair-and-deceptive-practices law, enforced by its attorney general.

**2010: a referee just for money.** After the 2008 financial crisis, the Dodd-Frank Act created the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/about-us/the-bureau/) and moved most consumer-finance rulewriting from [seven agencies](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R42572.html) into one. Its funding comes from the Federal Reserve rather than annual appropriations — a design choice meant to insulate it, which is now at the center of the fight over it.

**The private route, narrowed.** The laws above also let consumers sue. But most financial and subscription contracts now route disputes to individual arbitration, which the Supreme Court [upheld in 2011](https://www.oyez.org/cases/2010/09-893); class actions are often unavailable. That makes the public referees matter more, not less.

## Who does what today

**The FTC** covers most advertising, pricing and sales practices by companies that aren't banks: hidden fees, fake reviews, subscription traps, scams. Complaints go to [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/).

**The CFPB** covers consumer financial products — credit cards, mortgages, loans, bank accounts, payment apps — and runs a [public complaint database](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/) that forwards each complaint to the company for a response (since August 2026 the bureau [no longer publishes the written narratives](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2026/08/18/cfpb-ends-publication-of-consumer-complaint-narratives-and-data-visualizations/) that used to accompany them).

**Bank regulators** (the Federal Reserve, the OCC, the FDIC and, for credit unions, the NCUA) examine the institutions themselves.

**The states.** Every state has an unfair-and-deceptive-practices law and an attorney general who enforces it, plus its own rules on specific practices — [auto-renewal laws in roughly 30 states](https://merchantriskcouncil.org/learning/resource-center/member-news/blog/2026/chargebacks911-auto-renewal-laws-are-expanding), junk-fee and pricing laws in a growing number. State AGs can act whether or not Washington does, and [in 2025 and 2026 they have ramped up enforcement against hidden fees](https://www.troutman.com/insights/state-attorneys-general-and-continued-enforcement-against-junk-fees-in-2026/).

## What changed in 2025 and 2026

**The CFPB was cut back sharply — and is still operating.** Since February 2025 the administration has moved to shrink the bureau: dropped enforcement cases, attempted mass layoffs, and a funding request of zero. Courts have set the limits so far: [a judge froze workforce cuts until the Senate confirms a director](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/cfpb-job-cuts-frozen-until-a-new-director-is-confirmed) — a freeze that lasts only until that confirmation; a nominee cleared committee in September 2026 and awaits a floor vote — while [staff had fallen to roughly 1,100 by spring 2026](https://www.bankingdive.com/news/cfpb-workforce-reduction-plan-vought-cut-53-percent-618-nteu-farman-big-beautiful-fed-funding/816489/), down from about 1,700; [federal judges in three cases rejected the argument that the bureau could stop requesting funding](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/another-judge-rejects-trumps-attempt-to-halt-cfpb-funding); and in July 2025 [Congress cut the most it can draw from the Federal Reserve by about 46%](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12578.html). The results show in the bureau's own [semiannual report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/semi-annual-report-january-2026-june-2026/): [one new public enforcement action between July 2025 and June 2026](https://receivablesinfo.com/2026/11/19/cfpb-nearly-15-months-without-enforcement-action/), [86% of open supervisory actions closed and scheduled exams down about half](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/cfpb-closes-supervisory-actions/) — though [examinations resumed in 2026 at a smaller scale](https://www.insidearm.com/news/00096402-cfpb-examinations-are-smaller-not-gone-wh/). The complaint line still runs. Eliminating the bureau outright would take an act of Congress.

**The FTC stayed on the field.** Under its current leadership the FTC [collected a $2.5 billion settlement from Amazon](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-secures-historic-25-billion-settlement-against-amazon) over Prime sign-ups and cancellation (September 2025), [enforced its new hidden-fees rule for tickets and lodging](https://www.bakerlaw.com/insights/ftc-v-stubhub-10-million-settlement-signals-aggressive-enforcement-of-new-fees-rule/) (in effect since May 2025; StubHub paid $10 million), [restarted the subscription rulemaking](https://gibsondunn.com/ftc-restarts-negative-option-rulemaking-after-eighth-circuit-vacatur-enforcement-under-rosca-continues/) a court had thrown out on procedural grounds, and [opened an inquiry into personalized pricing and pay practices](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-launches-personalized-pricing-compensation-inquiry) (October 2026). Deregulation in one building and active enforcement in another is the current shape of the federal referee.

**The states moved up.** With fewer federal cases, [state attorneys general have pressed hidden-fee and subscription cases under their own laws](https://www.troutman.com/insights/state-attorneys-general-and-continued-enforcement-against-junk-fees-in-2026/), and new state statutes carry requirements federal rules no longer do. Where you live now matters more to which protections you have.

## The argument

**Supporters of a smaller federal role** argue that banks already answer to their own regulators and nonbanks to the FTC and the states, so the CFPB duplicates other agencies; that its Federal Reserve funding made it unaccountable to Congress; and that enforcement had become regulation by lawsuit. The bureau's acting director [made that case to Congress in July 2026](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2026-07-24/cfpb-acting-director-defends-downsizing-calls-for-structural-reform-in-house-senate-hearings/), calling for appropriations and a narrower mandate.

**Critics of the pullback** argue that no other agency actually picks up the dropped work — [an American Banker review questioned whether other regulators could pick up the CFPB's authorities](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/if-the-cfpb-goes-away-what-happens-to-its-authorities) — that [states can't replace a federal supervisor of national companies](https://consumerfed.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/States-Cannot-Replace-the-CFPB_Blog.pdf), and that [complaints keep arriving while consumer groups count hundreds of thousands unresolved](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/cfpb-on-life-support-one-year-after-it-was-targeted-for-shutdown). Both sides made these arguments in 2025–26; the court cases that will settle the bureau's size are still going.

## What you can do, whoever is refereeing

- **A problem with a financial product:** complain to the company first, then the [CFPB's complaint database](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/complaint/) — companies still respond — and your [state attorney general](https://www.naag.org/find-my-ag/).
- **A scam, a hidden fee, a subscription that won't cancel:** [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) and your state attorney general.
- **Know your state.** Protections increasingly differ by state; your attorney general's consumer page lists what applies where you live.

Status notes on specific rules (overdraft, late fees, subscriptions, junk fees, payday) live on the pages about those products; see [reading your statements](reading-your-statements.html), [subscriptions](subscription-creep.html) and [the same groceries, a different price](same-groceries-different-price.html).
