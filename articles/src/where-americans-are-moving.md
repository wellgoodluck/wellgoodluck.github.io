## The short version

Once a year — most recently on January 27, 2026 — the Census Bureau estimates how each state's population changed, and splits the change into three parts: births minus deaths, net moves from other countries, and net moves from other states. The latest estimates cover the year through June 2025 — and they broke the pattern most people still carry around. North Carolina, not Texas or Florida, gained the most movers from other states. Florida's gain from other states fell to eighth, less than a tenth of what it was three years earlier. Immigration fell by more than half. And the Midwest, which had sent more people to other states than it took in for years, came out slightly ahead. The numbers are below; what the moves mean is argued about, and both sides of that argument are too.

## Three ways a state's population changes

The Census Bureau counts everyone once a decade. In between, it estimates each state's population every July, and publishes the change as three components.

**Births minus deaths** — the bureau calls it natural change — comes from vital records, the most solid of the three. Nationally it added 518,858 people in the year through June 2025: 3,620,461 births against 3,101,603 deaths. Some states run negative on this component alone; West Virginia's deaths have outnumbered its births in all five years the current estimates cover.

**Net international migration** counts moves between the United States and other countries, in both directions. It is estimated, not counted, and it swings with policy and world events: about 376,000 in the pandemic year through June 2021, then 1.7 million, 2.3 million and 2.7 million in the three years after, then 1,262,202 in the year through June 2025 — a drop of more than half, which is most of why the country's growth slowed to 0.5%.

**Net domestic migration** counts moves between states. Across all fifty states it sums to roughly zero: one state's gain is another's loss. That is what makes it the component people watch. Births happen where people already are, and immigration lands mostly in big gateway states, but a moving truck crossing a state line is a household comparing two states and picking one.

A caution before the scoreboard: the two migration components are the softest numbers in the set, pieced together from tax returns, Medicare records and surveys, and they are revised with every annual release. The components also need not add exactly to the change in a state's total; the method leaves a small residual.

## What the 2025 estimates showed

For most of the 2020s the story was simple: the South gained, the coastal Northeast and California lost, and Texas and Florida led everything. The estimates through June 2025 kept the South on top but shuffled the order.

North Carolina gained the most people from other states: 84,064, net. Texas was second at 67,299 — a third of its 2022 pace of 218,840. South Carolina was third at 66,622, and relative to its size that was the biggest gain in the country; it was the nation's fastest-growing state, up 79,958 people, or about 1.5%.

Florida's net gain from other states was 22,517 — eighth place, down from 310,892 in the year through June 2022. Florida still grew by 196,680 people, second only to Texas's 391,243, but the growth came mostly from abroad: its net international migration, 178,674, was the country's largest. Texas, likewise, grew mostly on immigration and births.

The Midwest's turn is the quieter surprise. The region as a whole gained about 16,000 people from other states — modest, but the first positive year this decade, and a senior demographer at the bureau singled it out. Ohio, which lost a net 32,482 people to other states in the year through June 2021, gained 11,926 in the latest year. Michigan went from losing 28,290 to gaining 1,796.

The big losers are familiar, with losses far below their 2021 peaks. California's net loss to other states was 229,077, its smallest of the decade and half its 2021 peak of 470,759; counting births and immigration, its total population still slipped by 9,465. New York lost a net 137,586 to other states — a bit more than the year before, though well under 2021's 326,190 — and ended the year essentially flat overall.

## What people argue about

**Why people move.** The Tax Foundation, which favors lower taxes, points out that the states gaining movers are mostly low-tax states and argues taxes are part of the draw. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, which favors maintaining state revenues, counters that decades of research show most interstate moves follow jobs, housing costs and family, and that taxes move the needle very little. Demographers tend to land on the mundane side: North Carolina's state demographer credited its gains to high-paying jobs in banking and tech and to big cities that are still smaller than Florida's or Texas's. One reading is that housing fits the 2025 shuffle best — the states that gained the most are ones where a paycheck still covers a house — but no single cause explains every state, and the data here can't settle it.

**Whether the numbers are right.** Demographers in Florida and Texas told the Associated Press they were not entirely sold on the accuracy of the bureau's migration estimates — the component hardest to pin down and the one that swings most from year to year — though they did not question the rigor of its work. The bureau itself revised its immigration method this cycle. Treat any one year's figure, and especially any one state's migration figure, as an estimate with error bars, not a count.

**What a loss means.** The idea that people "vote with their feet" — pick the state whose services, costs and rules suit them — goes back to a 1956 paper by the economist Charles Tiebout, and it is why domestic migration gets read as a verdict. The analogy breaks in two places. Moving is expensive, so the vote is cast mostly by people with the means and the job flexibility to move. And a state can lose movers because it is expensive, which is partly a sign that many people want to live there and it hasn't built enough homes for them — a shortage, not only a failure. By the same token, a state can gain movers while its wages trail. The moving trucks are real information; they are just not the whole report card.
