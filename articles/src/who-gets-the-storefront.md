## The short version

A shopping center owner isn't only renting out space. It's putting together a set of leases, and that set is what the property is worth, what a bank will lend against it, and what a buyer will pay for it. Two tenants paying the same rent can make the property worth very different amounts, depending on how long each is committed and how sure the owner is that the rent will keep coming. A national chain with a long lease and a strong balance sheet usually wins that comparison against a local store whose owner has signed a personal guarantee. Big stores that bring in shoppers also help the other tenants in the center, so owners often charge them less per square foot than small shops pay. Right now owners have more choice than usual: retail space is about as scarce as it has ever been, and discount chains are among the retailers opening the most stores. None of this guarantees the chain was the better bet. Chains close too, and 2025 brought a wave of chain bankruptcies. A commercial lease is mostly a private deal, and a city has few ways to change who gets the space.

## The analogy

A landlord choosing a tenant is like a bank choosing a borrower. Two people can ask to borrow the same amount at the same rate, and the bank still prefers one of them. That isn't about who's nicer. It's about who's more likely to keep paying for the whole term, and what the bank can do if they stop.

## What a shopping center sells

Many shopping center leases are "triple net." The tenant pays base rent plus its share of the property tax, the insurance and the upkeep of the parking lot and common areas. So the owner's costs are largely passed through, and what the owner keeps is the base rent. That stream of rent, after whatever costs the owner still carries, is called net operating income.

Owners and buyers commonly value a property by dividing that income by a rate investors call the capitalization rate, or cap rate ([PropertyMetrics](https://propertymetrics.com/blog/how-commercial-real-estate-loan-underwriting-works/)). The cap rate is the yearly return a buyer accepts for that income stream. A safer stream gets a lower rate, and a lower rate means a higher price.

Credit and lease length drive the rate. In its first-quarter 2026 report, The Boulder Group, a brokerage that tracks the single-tenant net-lease market, found that buildings with 15-year leases to tenants such as Chase (rated AA-) and Wells Fargo (BBB+) were listed at cap rates of about 4.9% to 5.3%. Buildings with 5-year leases to tenants that are unrated or rated below investment grade were listed at higher rates: Family Dollar at 8.4% to 8.8%, Walgreens at 8.6% to 9.0% ([Boulder Group](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/618489/the-boulder-group-releases-q1-2026-net-lease-tenant-profiles-report)). Those are whole buildings leased to one tenant, not spaces in a center, but buyers of shopping centers weigh the same things, tenant credit and how long leases run, when they price the whole property. The firm expects credit quality and lease length to stay the main things that set prices. In the second quarter, it reported that fewer than 10% of single-tenant retail net-lease listings had investment-grade tenants on long leases, and that investors were competing hard for those that did ([CRE Daily](https://www.credaily.com/?p=220006)).

## A worked example

*Illustrative numbers, not any real property.*

A 30,000-square-foot space rents for $22 a square foot a year, triple net: $660,000 a year.

| | Tenant A | Tenant B |
|---|---|---|
| Who | National chain, strong credit | Local store, owner's personal guarantee |
| Lease | 10 years | 5 years |
| Rent | $660,000 a year | $660,000 a year |
| Cap rate a buyer might use | 6.5% | 7.5% |
| What that rent is worth to a buyer | about $10.15 million | about $8.80 million |

Same space, same rent, and Tenant A's lease is worth about $1.35 million more, roughly two years of rent. For the two to come out even, Tenant B would have to pay about $761,500 a year, about 15% more. The one-point gap here is smaller than the roughly 3.5-point gap in the Boulder figures, though those also compare different kinds of buildings, so the example is on the cautious side.

The same arithmetic runs through the owner's loan. Lenders size a commercial mortgage on the property's income. Before they do, they may mark that income down for the chance that tenants leave when their leases end ([PropertyMetrics](https://propertymetrics.com/blog/how-commercial-real-estate-loan-underwriting-works/)). The fewer leases that end during the loan, and the stronger the tenants, the less there is to mark down.

## Why small tenants are asked for more

Landlords treat large, creditworthy national tenants as the safest bet ([New England Real Estate Journal](https://nerej.com/pdf/60110)). A small business is usually asked to back its lease with something else. The usual one is a personal guarantee, which makes the owner personally liable for the rent if the business can't pay, and more landlords insist on one ([FindLaw](https://www.findlaw.com/legalblogs/small-business/what-is-a-personal-guarantee-commercial-lease-and-how-to-get-out-of-one/)). Landlords may also ask for a larger security deposit, though many small businesses can't spare that much cash. A guarantee helps, but it's only as good as the person behind it, and collecting on one can mean a lawsuit ([New England Real Estate Journal](https://nerej.com/pdf/60110)).

Commercial tenants get far fewer legal protections than people who rent their homes; the law mostly assumes the two sides will bargain for what they want ([G&G Law Offices](https://www.illinoislawyernow.com/?p=65421)). California added a few in 2025 for very small businesses, such as notice before a rent increase on a month-to-month lease, but none gives a right to renew ([Cox, Castle & Nicholson](https://www.coxcastle.com/publication-sb-1103-new-california-laws-impacts-certain-commercial-leases-with-qualified-commercial-tenants)). When a lease ends without a renewal option written into it, the tenant generally has no right to stay, which is why an option to renew is one of a tenant's best protections ([G&G Law Offices](https://www.illinoislawyernow.com/?p=65421)). Without one, the renewal is a new negotiation, and the landlord can take other offers.

## The store that brings the crowd

The biggest stores in a center often pay the least per square foot. In a 1998 study of malls, economists Peter Pashigian and Eric Gould found that department-store anchors paid at least 72% less per square foot than the other stores. Smaller shops paid more because they benefited from the shoppers the anchors drew ([Journal of Law and Economics](https://doi.org/10.1086/467386)). The owner, in effect, collects from the small shops for the traffic the big store brings in.

The traffic is real. Economists Daniel Shoag and Stan Veuger looked at what happened around stores that closed when national chains like Borders, Circuit City and Mervyn's went bankrupt. Nearby businesses declined, with fewer establishments and fewer workers, partly because shoppers who stopped going to the big store also visited the shops around it less often ([Review of Economics and Statistics](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:100:y:2018:i:3:p:440-453); [Cato Institute summary](https://cato.org/publications/research-briefs-economic-policy/shops-city-evidence-local-externalities-local)).

Leases often put that dependence in writing. Many retail leases, especially those of tenants with enough leverage to negotiate them, have a co-tenancy clause: if a named anchor closes, or the center's occupancy falls below a set level, the tenant may pay reduced rent and sometimes may leave ([Seyfarth Shaw](https://www.seyfarth.com/dir_docs/documents/misc/rln-johnson-030625.pdf); [PropertyMetrics](https://propertymetrics.com/blog/cotenancy/)). In December 2024 the California Supreme Court enforced one of these clauses as written, in a case between a shopping center owner and Jo-Ann Stores, saying sophisticated businesses that negotiate a lease should be held to its terms ([JJD-HOV Elk Grove v. Jo-Ann Stores](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2024/s275843.html)). One empty anchor space can cut the rent of every tenant whose lease has such a clause. That's why owners think hard about who fills a large space and how likely that tenant is to stay.

## Why owners have more choice right now

Retail space is scarce. CBRE put the national retail availability rate at 4.9% in the second quarter of 2026 and 4.8% in the third, with new construction at the lowest levels in its records ([CBRE, Q2 2026](https://mktgdocs.cbre.com/2299/4f31b682-cf01-4e1a-a559-8583e519d83f-692872684/Q2_2026_U.S._Retail_Market_Rep.pdf); [CBRE, Q3 2026](https://www.cbre.com/insights/figures/q3-2026-us-retail-market-report)). Colliers put retail vacancy at 4.3% in the third quarter, near record lows, and shopping center vacancy at 5.4% ([CRE Daily](https://www.credaily.com/?p=223969)). When few spaces are empty, the tenant whose lease is ending has less bargaining power, and the owner can shop the space around.

Discount chains are among the retailers still expanding. Coresight Research found store openings in the first half of 2026 at their lowest midyear count since 2020, but value and off-price chains kept adding stores ([Retail TouchPoints](https://www.retailtouchpoints.com/?p=621322)). Ross and Burlington each planned about 110 new stores for 2026 ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/1661519462/retail-off-price-giants-ross-tj-maxx-parent-ring-up-more-store-openings)). TJX said its openings were beating its own expectations, and Nordstrom Rack planned 25 openings between Aug. 20 and Nov. 5 ([Modern Retail](https://www.modernretail.co/operations/tjx-to-accelerate-store-openings-as-off-price-continues-its-hot-streak/)). Off-price chains have also moved into big spaces that bankrupt retailers left behind. Burlington took over the leases of 45 Joann stores in 2025 ([Retail Dive](https://www.retaildive.com/news/burlington-45-joanns-store-leases-bankruptcy/746764/)), and a Florida retail broker told the Business Observer that off-price chains lined up to take over former Big Lots stores ([Business Observer](https://www.businessobserverfl.com/news/2026/mar/06/despite-store-closures-hope-for-retail/)).

## Where the analogy breaks

A bank's borrower doesn't change the neighborhood, but a tenant does. A landlord isn't only judging who will pay. It's choosing what the center will be: which shoppers it draws, what the other tenants can charge, what the place feels like. And the chain isn't always the safe borrower. In 2025 bankruptcies emptied thousands of chain stores. Joann closed all of its roughly 800 stores and Party City about 800; Big Lots, bankrupt since late 2024, planned to close 340; and Rite Aid said in May that about 1,000 would close. Coresight counted about 6,000 closings announced in the first half of the year ([Talk Business & Politics](https://talkbusiness.net/?p=287941)). Rite Aid later closed all of its stores ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/companies-bankruptcy-store-closing-out-of-business-2025)). Walgreens has long been one of the most common tenants in single-tenant net-lease portfolios ([Northmarq](https://www.northmarq.com/insights/research/walgreens-after-sycamore-what-shrink-core-means-retail-real-estate)). In October 2024 it announced plans to close about 1,200 stores over three years ([Walgreens Boots Alliance, SEC filing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1618921/000119312524237203/d870298d8k.htm)), and in August 2025 the private equity firm Sycamore Partners completed its purchase of the company ([Sycamore Partners](https://secure.businesswire.com/news/home/20250827891313/en/Sycamore-Partners-Completes-Acquisition-of-Walgreens-Boots-Alliance)). Cap rates on its buildings rose from the mid-6% range in 2024 into the 7s and higher in 2025 ([Northmarq](https://www.northmarq.com/insights/research/walgreens-after-sycamore-what-shrink-core-means-retail-real-estate)). In early 2026, sellers of buildings with five years left on a Walgreens lease were asking about 8.6% to 9%, and 9% or more when less time was left ([Boulder Group](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/618489/the-boulder-group-releases-q1-2026-net-lease-tenant-profiles-report)). For the owners of those buildings, that meant lower values. A credit rating is an opinion about the risk of default. It isn't a guarantee for the length of a lease.

## What cities can and can't do

A city generally can't make a private owner renew a lease or choose one retailer over another, and some states rule it out by law. California bars cities and counties from controlling commercial rents, the length of commercial leases, or which tenant an owner must negotiate with ([Cal. Civ. Code §§ 1954.26–1954.27](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1954.27)). A city's leverage is zoning, permits and money, and a few cities use it.

**Limits on chains.** San Francisco voters approved Proposition G in November 2006, which requires a special permit, a conditional use authorization, for "formula retail" in most neighborhood commercial districts ([SPUR](https://www.spur.org/publications/voter-guide/2006-11-01/proposition-g-limitations-formula-retail-stores)). The city's planning code treats a store as formula retail if the company has 11 or more other locations anywhere in the world and the stores share at least two standardized features, such as merchandise, decor, signage or a trademark. A few districts, including North Beach, Hayes-Gough and part of Chinatown, ban them outright ([SF Planning](https://sfplanning.org/permit/chain-stores)). Other cities do something similar. Coronado, California, for example, limits chain fast-food restaurants to 10 sites and requires a special use permit for chain businesses in some zones ([Coronado Municipal Code, ch. 86.55](https://www.codepublishing.com/ca/coronado/html/Coronado86/Coronado8655.html)).

**Paying landlords to keep local tenants.** San Francisco's Legacy Business Program has paid landlords that give a registered legacy business a lease of at least 10 years. Under its Rent Stabilization Grant rules, the grant was up to $4.50 per square foot a year on up to 5,000 square feet, or $22,500 a year for one location, and could be less when the program's money ran short ([San Francisco Office of Small Business](https://www.sf.gov/file/rsg-rules-and-regs-20220926)). New applications now go to a separate program, the Business Stabilization Grant. It pays landlords up to the same rate but requires them to pass at least half to the tenant, and as of November 2026 the city said it wasn't taking new applications because of funding limits ([sf.gov](https://sf.gov/information--business-stabilization-grant); [Business Stabilization Grant rules](https://media.api.sf.gov/documents/BSG_Rules_and_Regs_8.24.26_Clean.pdf)).

**Help finding the next space.** Cities can also help after a lease is lost: an economic development office that tracks open spaces, and faster permit and plan review for a business that's moving.

## What people argue about

**Whether cities should limit chains.** Supporters say limits keep neighborhoods distinctive and money circulating locally. The Institute for Local Self-Reliance, a group that supports such limits, said in 2013 that the rules helped San Francisco keep a relatively strong independent retail sector, noting the city had twice as many independent bookstores per person as New York ([Institute for Local Self-Reliance](https://ilsr.org/?p=30745)). Critics say the rules leave storefronts empty, raise prices and penalize successful local businesses once they pass 11 locations. GrowSF, a San Francisco advocacy group, makes that case. It counts a 97.6% approval rate for chain applications since 2014 and argues the delay itself is the cost, citing one broker's estimate of 7 to 8 months and up to $80,000 ([GrowSF](https://growsf.org/research/2025-06-16-formula-retail/)). A 2013 review by the law firm Hanson Bridgett found the city had approved about three in four such applications over the previous nine years, and said the cost and delay discouraged many retailers from applying ([Hanson Bridgett](https://hansonbridgett.com/Publications/articles/2013-12-formula-retail-changes)).

**Whether the owner chose well.** One side: a long lease to a strong tenant protects the property's value, its loan and the other tenants, and an owner who passes that up is taking a risk with its lender's and investors' money as well as its own. The other side: a long-time local tenant has already proved it can pay and draws loyal customers, while the chain's credit can fade, as 2025 showed. Both sides are pricing the same risk. They disagree about which tenant carries more of it.

**Whether neighbors have a say.** One side: it's private property, and the owner bears the risk, so it makes the call. The other: a neighborhood shopping center works as shared infrastructure, and the people who shop there have a stake in what it carries. Under current law, the owner generally makes the call; the second view shows up at the edges, in zoning, design review and programs like San Francisco's.
