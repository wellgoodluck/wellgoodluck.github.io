## In one sentence

The almanac's mortgage rate is Freddie Mac's weekly average of what lenders quoted for a 30-year fixed loan to a borrower with good credit and a 20% down payment; your own quote moves with your credit score, down payment, points and lender, but it moves with this line.

## The analogy

A markup on a wholesale price. Lenders don't hold your loan for thirty years with their own money. Most mortgages are sold into bonds, and the investors who buy those bonds price them against the 10-year Treasury, the safest long loan there is. So the mortgage rate is the 10-year yield, the wholesale price of ten-year money, plus a markup for the things that make a mortgage riskier than a Treasury: you might refinance the moment rates fall, someone has to service the loan, the government agency guaranteeing it takes a fee, and the lender takes a margin.

The markup is normally 1.5 to 2 points. In 2022–23 it widened to nearly 3, because rates were swinging wildly and the Fed had stopped buying mortgage bonds. By late 2026 it is back to about 1.9. That means today's 7.3% rate is not a markup problem. It is a wholesale problem: the 10-year is at 5%.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**If you're buying: the day you lock.** On a $400,000 loan, the monthly payment for principal and interest is about $1,690 at 3% and about $2,740 at 7.3%. Same house, $1,050 a month more, over $12,000 a year, for thirty years. That arithmetic is why home sales fell to 1995 levels when rates doubled.

**If you already own with a fixed rate: never.** Your payment was set the day you closed. This is the lock-in effect: most outstanding mortgages carry rates well below 5%, taken out or refinanced in 2020–21, and the people holding them don't sell, because the next house would come with a rate two or three points higher. Fewer listings, fewer sales, and prices that held up through a doubling of rates.

**If you rent: with a lag, two ways.** Would-be buyers who can't make the payment stay renters, which pushes on rents. And builders borrow at these rates too, so fewer apartments get started, which pushes on rents again two years later when they would have opened. [Housing starts](../chart.html?id=housing_starts&range=10y&ov=single_family_starts) is the almanac's line for that.

**If you're hoping to refinance: when rates fall a point below your rate.** Refinancing waves happen when the market rate drops about a point under what most people locked. There has not been one since 2021, and there won't be until rates are in the fives.

## What moves it

The 10-year yield, which means the market's view of the Fed's path, of inflation, and of how much extra to charge for a decade of uncertainty. And the spread, which widens when rates are volatile, when the Fed is shrinking its holdings of mortgage bonds, and when banks pull back. Not the Fed funds rate, except through the 10-year's guess about where it is headed. The late-2024 episode is the clean example: the Fed cut by a full point, the 10-year rose by a point on inflation and deficit worries, and the mortgage rate went up.

## How to read this week's number

**Against the 10-year.** If the mortgage rate moved and the 10-year didn't, the spread moved, which means stress in the mortgage market. If both moved together, it is the bond market's view of the Fed and inflation, and the mortgage rate is just carrying it.

**Against its history.** Seven percent is where the rate lived in the 1990s and much of the 2000s. Three percent was a once-in-history low. The 2015–19 average was 4.0%. So "rates are high" is true against the last fifteen years and false against the last fifty; both halves of that sentence drive the arguments below.

**Against what people already have.** The gap between today's rate and the average rate on existing mortgages is the lock-in. The wider it is, the fewer homes come up for sale, whatever the Fed does.

## Where the analogy breaks

A markup on a wholesale price assumes the wholesale price sits still. The 10-year moves every minute, and the markup itself can double in a stressed year. And unlike a store's markup, this one depends on the customer: the right to refinance is an option you hold, lenders price it, and so part of your rate is the cost of your own future refinancing. The survey rate is also a benchmark, not an offer. Borrowers with weaker credit or smaller down payments are quoted more, and in a tight market a lot more.

## What people argue about

**Whether rate cuts fix affordability.** One side: lower rates lower payments and let more people qualify, which is the whole point of cutting. The other: the Fed doesn't control the 10-year, as 2024 showed; cheaper money raises prices as much as it lowers payments, as 2020–21 showed, when rates under 3% came with prices up 45%; and the shortage is houses, not loans. Both cite the same two episodes. Rates doubled in 2022 and prices didn't fall; rates halved in 2020 and prices soared. Each side reads that as proof.

**Who is to blame for 7%.** The Fed, for hiking. Congress and the Treasury, for deficits that lift the 10-year. The Fed again, for letting its mortgage bonds run off and widening the spread. Or nobody, because the 2010s were the anomaly and 7% is normal. The chart shows the 10-year and the spread separately, which at least says how much of the rate is each.

**The 30-year fixed itself.** Almost no other country offers one; it exists here because the government guarantees the bonds. One side says it is why Americans could lock 3% for life, and also why the market is frozen now. The other points to Britain and Canada, where adjustable-rate mortgages delivered the central bank's hikes straight to existing homeowners' kitchen tables, and asks which cohort you would rather be. Both are right about the mechanism. It is a design choice with winners and losers by vintage.
