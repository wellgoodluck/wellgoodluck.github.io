## The short version

About 78 million Americans live in one of the country's [roughly 373,000 homeowners' associations, condo associations and co-ops](https://foundation.caionline.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/2025StatisticalReviewFoundation.pdf), and each one runs on a budget its board adopts every year. The monthly fee tells you what the association charges now. The documents behind it tell you what it will need to charge: whether it's saving enough for the roof, the paving and the elevators it knows it will have to replace, whether owners are paying, what the insurance leaves uncovered, and what the board has been discussing that hasn't reached the budget yet. Every association keeps two kinds of money: an operating account for the year's bills, and a reserve fund for big repairs years away. Most surprises come from the second. Starting with loan applications dated January 4, 2027, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac will expect condo associations whose units they finance to put [at least 15% of their budgeted dues income into reserves](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/44986/display), up from 10% (small projects can be exempt), which makes the reserve line the one that buyers, lenders and owners will all be reading in 2027. The rules on what an association must disclose vary by state; this page uses California and Florida as examples and says where the rest of the country differs.

## The analogy

Think of buying a seat in a carpool that owns its own van. Everyone chips in each month. Part of the money pays for gas and insurance. Part of it goes into a jar for tires, brakes and, someday, a new transmission. A careful carpool knows roughly when each of those is due and puts enough in the jar each month to cover them. A carpool that keeps the monthly payment low by skimping on the jar is fine, until the transmission goes, and then every rider gets a bill the same week. Before you buy a seat, you'd want to see the jar, the repair schedule and whether everyone is paying.

## What's in the packet

An owner can usually ask the association for its records, and a buyer is handed a stack of them during the sale. The names differ by state; the contents mostly don't:

- **The annual budget.** The year's expected income and spending, including how much goes into reserves. In California, the board must send owners an annual budget report [30 to 90 days before the fiscal year ends](https://www.davis-stirling.com/HOME/A/Annual-Budgets), and it must include a summary of the reserves, any repairs the board has deferred, whether special assessments are expected for major repairs or reserves, how reserves are being funded and calculated, any loans with an original term of more than a year, and a summary of the insurance. If the board doesn't distribute the report as required, it can't raise regular dues that year [without approval from a majority of a quorum of owners](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5605), and any increase of more than 20% always needs owners' approval.
- **The reserve study.** A professional's list of everything the association must eventually repair or replace, with each item's remaining life and cost, the money on hand, and a plan for funding the rest, usually over 30 years. California requires most associations to have a [visual inspection at least every three years](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5550); Florida requires a separate structural study for condo buildings of three or more habitable stories [every 10 years](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/718.112). Most states require none.
- **Financial statements.** Last year's actual results, ideally reviewed or audited by an outside accountant. Smaller associations may produce only an internal report.
- **The insurance summary.** What the association's master policy covers, its limits and its deductible.
- **The delinquency report.** How many owners are behind on dues, and by how much.
- **Board minutes.** The record of what the board discussed and decided, often the only place an engineer's report, an insurer's warning or a lawsuit shows up before it shows up in the budget.
- **The governing documents.** The declaration (often called the CC&Rs), bylaws and rules, which say who is responsible for what, from roofs to windows to plumbing inside the walls.

## The two accounts

**Operating** pays for the year: management, landscaping, utilities for shared areas, insurance, pool service, gate repairs. If it runs short, the board can trim, raise dues next year, or levy a special assessment.

**Reserves** are the jar. The reserve contribution is one line in the budget, and it's the line most tempting to cut when owners push back on an increase, because nothing breaks the year it's cut. The cost shows up later, as a special assessment or a loan when something big comes due. Some associations also borrow from their own reserves to cover operating shortfalls. [California lets a board do that](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=5515) only after notice to owners and a written finding explaining why and how the money will be repaid. Repayment is due within a year, unless the board, after another notice, documents that a short delay is in the association's best interest. If needed, the board must levy a special assessment to repay it.

## Seven questions to ask

**1. What share of the budget goes to reserves?** Divide the reserve contribution by the dues income. For condo buildings, this is now a lending question: from January 4, 2027, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac look for [at least 15% of budgeted assessment income](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/44986/display), unless the association has a reserve study from the past three years and funds it at the study's highest recommended level ([Freddie Mac's rule matches](https://guide.freddiemac.com/ci/okcsFattach/get/1010529_7)). In a building that falls short, lenders won't make loans they can sell to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, so buyers may need costlier loans or cash, which shrinks the pool of buyers for every unit. Fifteen percent is a lender's floor, not a measure of what any particular association needs.

**2. How old is the reserve study, and how well funded is the reserve?** A study's main summary number is "percent funded": the cash in reserves divided by what the study says should be there by now, given how worn everything is. One large reserve-study firm, Association Reserves, calls [under 30% weak, 30% to 70% fair, and 70% or more strong](https://www.reservestudy.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/2026-04-29-100000-Industry-Insights-Report-FINAL.pdf). Among its clients, about a third were in the weak range and about a quarter in the strong range. Those bands come from a company that sells reserve studies, and percent funded is a snapshot: a fund that's 40% funded with a realistic plan and steady contributions can be in better shape than one that's 60% funded and cutting its contribution. A study more than three years old may no longer match prices or the property.

**3. What's coming due soon, and is the money there?** The reserve study lists each component and the year it's expected to need work. Compare the next five years of big items with the reserve balance plus five years of contributions. If they don't cover it, the difference has to come from somewhere.

**4. Has the board deferred repairs, or said a special assessment is likely?** Some states require the budget to say so. Elsewhere, look in the minutes and ask directly. A repair put off to keep dues flat doesn't go away; it usually costs more later.

**5. How many owners are behind?** A high delinquency rate means the paying owners are covering for the others, and it can make the building harder to finance. Fannie Mae won't buy loans in a condo project where [more than 15% of units are 60 or more days behind](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-2.2-01/full-review-process) on their dues.

**6. What does the insurance cover, and who pays the deductible?** In a condo, the master policy usually covers the building, so its limits and deductible matter to every owner. Many associations pass a large deductible on to owners after a loss, as an assessment. California's budget report must warn owners that they may have to pay all or part of a deductible. Associations have been hit by large premium increases and nonrenewals; in a 2025 Foundation for Community Association Research survey of board members, managers and insurance professionals, [93% said their association's premium rose at its last renewal](https://foundation.caionline.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Hanke2026FinalReportClimateRiskResilience.pdf). [Why HOA dues keep going up](why-hoa-dues-go-up.html) covers what that has done to budgets.

**7. What do the last year of minutes say?** Look for engineer or inspection reports, insurer letters, lawsuits by or against the association, large bids, loan discussions and owner complaints about leaks or failures. Board minutes are written by the board, so the absence of trouble in them isn't proof there is none, but trouble in them is hard to miss.

## A worked example

Maple Court, a made-up 40-unit condo building, charges $400 a month. Its numbers (all invented for the example):

In two years, the reserve will hold about $218,400: today's $180,000 plus two years of contributions, before interest. That's $21,600 short of the roof alone, about $540 per unit, and nothing left for anything else that comes due. The board's choices are a special assessment, a loan repaid out of higher dues, a larger contribution starting now, or putting the roof off, which tends to cost more later.

Raising the contribution to 15% of today's dues income, the lenders' new floor, would take another $9,600 a year, $20 per unit per month, if the board found the money by trimming other spending. Paying for it with higher dues would take about $23.50 per unit per month instead, because higher dues also raise the income the 15% is measured against. Either way, the roof gap would shrink to a few thousand dollars or less, with little or nothing left for anything else, and the building would meet the lending standard. ## Why this matters more now

**Lenders.** Since August 3, 2026, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac have [ended their lighter-touch reviews](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/44986/display) (Fannie's "Limited Review," [Freddie's "Streamlined Review"](https://guide.freddiemac.com/ci/okcsFattach/get/1010529_7)). Condo loans they buy now generally require a full review of the association's budget, reserves and delinquencies, unless the project has 10 or fewer units or otherwise qualifies for a waiver. The 15% reserve floor applies from January 4, 2027. The industry calls a building that doesn't meet the standards "non-warrantable": lenders won't make loans there that they can sell to Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac, so buyers may need costlier loans or cash, which can lower what owners can sell for. FHA and VA loans have rules of their own.

**States.** After the 2021 collapse of Champlain Towers South in Surfside, Florida required structural studies and inspections for condo buildings of three or more habitable stories, and for budgets adopted since the end of 2024, [owners in those buildings generally can no longer vote to waive or reduce the structural reserves](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2025/718.112). Exceptions include a pause of up to two budgets after a milestone inspection, available through 2028. California passed a law in September 2026 that will require associations to fund reserves to a minimum level starting in 2032; [Why HOA dues keep going up](why-hoa-dues-go-up.html) describes it. Most states still require neither a study nor a minimum.

## Where the analogy breaks

You can quit a carpool by finding another ride. Leaving an association means selling your home, and a weak reserve fund or a pending assessment can follow the unit into the sale price. A carpool's riders all agree on the van; an association's owners elect a board that decides for them, within limits set by state law and the governing documents. And a carpool's money is simple: an association's budget, reserve study and audit can disagree with one another, because they're prepared by different people, for different purposes, at different times.

## What people argue about

**The lenders' 15% floor.** Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac [say underfunded reserves go hand in hand with critical repairs](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/44986/display), and that unexpected special assessments can push owners toward mortgage default; Fannie Mae says the higher floor means [on average an additional $13 to $14 a month](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/partnering-streamline-condo-mortgage-process) in dues. A reserve-study firm has said that for most associations the change [won't make condo living more expensive](https://www.reservestudy.com/resources/article/fannie-mae-freddie-mac-reserve-funding-standards/). Critics, including the National Association of Mortgage Brokers, have [asked for at least a one-year delay](https://namb.org/condo-financing) of the 15% floor and of the end of the lighter review. They say they support the safety goals but warn the timeline will shut first-time buyers out of condos, and that associations following their own states' reserve-study schedules can still fail the lenders' tests through no fault of their own. In a [joint letter with the Community Associations Institute and the Community Home Lenders of America](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fhfa-gse-condo-rule-changes/), NAMB argued that a uniform increase doesn't account for different projects' risks.

**Whether owners should be able to vote to underfund.** In most states, owners or boards decide how much to save, and some associations let owners vote to fund less than a study recommends. Supporters of that freedom say owners are best placed to weigh their own budgets and that fixed requirements fall hardest on owners with fixed and moderate incomes; in Florida, a 2026 bill would have restored an owner vote to waive or reduce reserves in smaller buildings, and it died in committee. Supporters of mandates, such as Florida's for structural items, say underfunding pushes costs onto future owners who had no vote, and onto neighbors when a building fails, the lesson the state drew from Surfside.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of mid-November 2026.
