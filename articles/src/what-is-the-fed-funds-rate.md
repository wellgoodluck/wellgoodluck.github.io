## In one sentence

The federal funds rate is the interest banks charge each other to borrow money overnight, and the Federal Reserve steers it to a target range it announces eight times a year.

That's all it is. Nobody you know borrows at this rate. It matters because every other interest rate in the country is priced off it, the way every price in a grocery store is quietly priced off the wholesale cost of food.

## The analogy

Think of the Fed as controlling the thermostat in a very large old house. It sets one number in one room. The heat reaches the near rooms in minutes, the far rooms in hours, and the attic by next week. Nobody in the far rooms can see the thermostat; they just notice, eventually, that it's warmer.

The Fed turns the dial up to cool the economy (higher rates, less borrowing, less spending) and down to warm it. The dial is blunt: it can't heat one room and cool another. And the house takes a long time to respond, so the Fed is always setting the thermostat for the temperature it expects a year from now, not the one it feels today.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

In order of how fast you feel it:

**Savings accounts and money market funds: days.** Online high-yield savings accounts and money market funds reprice within a day or two of a Fed decision, up or down. The big traditional banks mostly don't: their savings rates barely moved through the 2022–23 hikes and barely moved back, which is why the gap between a 0.01% account and a 4% one is a choice, not a market condition. If your savings earned nothing from 2009 to 2015 and again in 2020–21, that was the thermostat set to zero.

**Credit cards, car loans, home equity lines: one or two statements.** Most of these carry a variable rate written as "prime plus something," and prime moves the same day the Fed does. A one-point Fed increase shows up as roughly one point on your card.

**Mortgages: it's complicated, and that's the point.** A 30-year mortgage rate is not set by the Fed. It follows the 10-year Treasury yield, which is set by bond buyers guessing where rates and inflation will be over the next decade. So mortgage rates move *before* the Fed does, when the market sees a change coming, and sometimes move the opposite way. In the first half of 2022, mortgage rates rose about two and a half points while the Fed's own rate rose one and a half. In late 2024 the Fed cut three times and mortgage rates went up a full point.

**Your job: six months to two years.** Higher rates mean businesses borrow less to expand, build less, and hire less. This is the slowest channel and the one the Fed is actually aiming at: cooling the job market enough to slow wage and price increases, without cooling it into a recession. The lag is why the Fed is always accused of doing too much or too little. By the time the effect is visible, the decision is a year old.

**Prices: a year or more.** Rates don't lower prices directly. They lower the demand that lets prices rise. Rates can't make oil, grow wheat, or unclog a port, which is why raising them during a supply shock is a judgment call, not a fix.

## What moves it

The Fed's committee sets the target, and it says why each time: it is legally charged with two goals, stable prices and maximum employment, and it moves the rate toward whichever is further off. In practice: inflation running above its 2% target pushes the rate up; a weakening job market pushes it down; a financial panic pushes it down fast. Politics is not supposed to move it, and the record on that is argued about below.

## How to read this month's number

The almanac shows the *effective* rate, the average of what banks actually paid, which sits inside the target range. A quarter-point move is the normal step. A half point is a statement. A cut between scheduled meetings means something is on fire.

The level by itself tells you little. What matters is the level *minus inflation*, the real rate. At 4% with 2% inflation, money has a price. At 4% with 6% inflation, borrowing is free in real terms, whatever the headline says. The chart below has that line.

## Where the analogy breaks

A thermostat is precise and the house doesn't talk back. The economy anticipates the Fed: markets move on what they expect it to do, so by the time it acts, part of the effect has already happened, and if it surprises them, the effect can be larger or smaller than intended. And a thermostat reads the current temperature. The Fed reads last month's inflation and last month's jobs, both of which get revised, and sets a rate for a year from now. It is steering by the rearview mirror, on purpose, because there is no windshield.

## What people argue about

**Whether it works at all on supply shocks.** Everyone agrees rates can't produce oil. One side says raising them anyway keeps a one-time price jump from becoming a wage-price spiral, as in the 1970s, and points to 2022–24 as proof. The other side says the 2022 inflation faded mostly because supply chains healed, and the hikes mainly raised mortgage rates and slowed homebuilding, which made the shelter part of inflation worse. The data can show what happened; it can't show what would have happened without the hikes.

**Independence.** The Fed is designed so that elected officials can't lower rates before an election. Presidents of both parties have pressured it anyway, in public and in private. Critics of independence say an unelected committee shouldn't have this much power over jobs; defenders say the countries where politicians set rates have the inflation records to show for it. In 2026 the question is live, with a new chair and public disagreement between the White House and the committee over the direction of rates. The site shows the rate, the chair, and the dates; it does not referee.

**Whether 2% is the right target.** Some economists argue for 3%, which would give the Fed more room to cut in a downturn. Others say moving the goalposts after missing them destroys the credibility that makes the target work. The Fed has said it isn't changing it.
