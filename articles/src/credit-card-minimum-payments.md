## Short version

A credit card is free to use if you pay the full statement balance by the due date every month. Carry a balance and you pay interest, and the average rate on accounts being charged interest is now about 22% a year. Paying only the minimum keeps you current, but it can stretch a $2,000 balance across more than a decade and cost more in interest than you originally borrowed.

## The trap in one sentence

The minimum payment is set to keep you paying for as long as possible, not to get you out of debt.

## How it works

Every month your card sends a statement with two important numbers: the statement balance and the minimum payment.

**Pay the statement balance in full by the due date** and you pay no interest on purchases. The time between a purchase and the due date is called the grace period, and it's what makes a card a free short-term loan for people who pay in full.

**Pay less than the full balance** and interest is charged on what's left, usually starting on new purchases too. The rate is called the APR, or annual percentage rate. The average APR on accounts being charged interest [rose to 22.36% in the third quarter of 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/).

**The minimum payment** is typically a small slice of your balance plus that month's interest. It's designed to be affordable, which also means most of each payment goes to interest early on.

Card rates aren't set in a vacuum. Your APR is [usually the prime rate plus a markup your card company chooses](https://www.wowt.com/bankrate-article/2026/10/21/current-credit-card-interest-rates/). When the Federal Reserve raises or lowers its rate, the prime rate follows, and so does your card's APR, usually within a billing cycle or two.

## A worked example

You owe $2,000 on a card charging 22.36% a year, and you stop adding to it. Your card uses a common minimum-payment formula: that month's interest plus 1% of the balance, with a $25 floor. Your first minimum is about $57.

{{illustration name=card-three-ways caption="The same $2,000 balance, three ways to pay it. Interest is the cost of going slower."}}

Paying only the minimum, you'd pay back more than $4,600 for $2,000 of spending. Doubling to $100 a month saves about $2,100 and nine years.

Your own statement already shows this math. [Since 2010, federal law has required card statements to include a box](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1026.7) showing how long payoff takes if you pay only the minimum, and how much you'd pay in total. It's easy to overlook.

## How to avoid it

**Pay the statement balance in full, every month.** If you can't, the card is lending you money at about 22%, and you should treat it that way.

**Set up autopay for at least the minimum.** A late payment usually costs a fee of about $30 for a first offense and up to $41 for a repeat within six months, and late payments damage your credit score. Autopay makes missing a due date nearly impossible.

**If you carry a balance, pay a fixed amount well above the minimum.** The minimum shrinks as your balance shrinks, which stretches the payoff. A fixed payment doesn't.

**Read the minimum-payment box on your statement.** It's the fastest reality check there is.

**Stop adding to a balance you're paying down.** New purchases on a card with a balance usually start charging interest right away.

**Ask for a lower rate.** If you've paid on time for a while, call and ask. It doesn't always work, but it costs nothing to try.

## The bigger picture

The fight over credit cards has focused on late fees.

Rules carrying out the 2009 Credit CARD Act set up "safe harbor" amounts that card companies can charge for a late payment without having to justify the cost. In March 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau [finalized a rule cutting that amount to $8](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2024-03-15/html/2024-05011.htm) for the largest card companies. A federal court blocked it before it took effect, [the rule was vacated, and the older amounts still apply](https://www.consumerfinancemonitor.com/2025/04/16/federal-judge-voids-cfpb-credit-card-late-fee-rule/).

**Supporters of a lower cap** argue that a $30 or $40 fee is far more than it costs a card company to process a late payment, and that the fees fall hardest on people already struggling.

**Opponents** argue that late fees discourage late payments, that lower fees would mean more missed payments, and that card companies would make up the money with higher interest rates or tighter credit for riskier borrowers.

These rules have changed before and may change again. This page reflects federal law as of November 8, 2026.
