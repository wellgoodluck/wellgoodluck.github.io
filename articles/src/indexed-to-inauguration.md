## The question

Who did better on the economy? The almanac shows the party of the president as a lane under every chart, and it stops there, because the next step, lining up each term and scoring it, is not a chart setting. It is a set of choices, and every choice moves the answer. This article makes the choices in the open, shows the numbers both ways, and leaves the verdict where it belongs.

The figures below are computed from the almanac's own data: payroll jobs, the unemployment rate, the Consumer Price Index and real GDP, for every president since Reagan, with the current term counted through September 2026.

## Four choices that decide the answer

**When the clock starts.** The obvious start is inauguration day. But nothing a new president does reaches the economy on January 20; the first budget takes effect in October, tax laws take a year to show up in paychecks, and [the Fed's rates](what-is-the-fed-funds-rate.html) take a year or more. The common alternative is to start the clock twelve months in and run it twelve months past the term, giving each president the year after they leave and taking away the year they inherited. Both are defensible. The tables show both, and the ranking changes between them.

**What to count.** Jobs added in total favors eight-year terms over four; jobs per month fixes that. The unemployment rate is a level, so what matters is where it started as much as where it ended: Reagan took office with it at 7.5%, Obama at 7.8%, and both left it lower; Trump took office with it at 4.7% and had little room to improve it. Inflation and growth are rates per year and compare cleanly, with the caveat that [rate vs. level](rate-vs-level.html) explains: a president can bring inflation down and leave prices up.

**What to do with a shock.** The pandemic erased 22 million jobs in March and April of 2020 and the rebound added 12 million back by December. Counted from inauguration, Trump's first term shows jobs falling by 58,000 a month; counted through February 2020 it shows them rising by 180,000 a month. Counted from inauguration, Biden's term shows 321,000 a month, the fastest on record; counted from one year in, after the rebound, 179,000. Both pairs of numbers are true. The article you read decides which one you see.

**What the president controls.** Interest rates, which move more than any law, are set by the Fed. Taxes and spending need Congress, and the almanac's event markers show how few major laws pass in a term. Oil prices are set abroad. The business cycle is mostly inherited: a president who takes office at the bottom (Reagan, Obama) gets the recovery; one who takes office at the top (G.W. Bush, Trump in 2017 and again in 2025) gets the next downturn or the slowdown. The lane on the chart shows who was in office. It does not show who was driving.

## What the tables show

Counted from inauguration, jobs per month rank Biden, Clinton, Reagan, Obama, G.H.W. Bush, the current term, G.W. Bush, Trump's first term. Shift the clock a year and the top four are Reagan, Clinton, Obama and Biden, within 15,000 jobs a month of each other, an order that one month's revision could change; G.W. Bush drops below zero because his lagged window ends in the depths of 2009, and Trump's first term rises to positive because his lagged window includes the 2021 rebound. The ranking is not a fact about presidents. It is a fact about the clock.

Unemployment tells the same story from the other end. Every president who inherited a rate above 7% left it lower. Every president who inherited one near 4% left it higher or the same. That is less a verdict on any of them than a description of where in the cycle they arrived.

Inflation is the one measure where the lag does the least damage to the ordering and the most to the magnitude: Biden's 5.0% a year becomes 3.7% with a year's lag, because the 2021 surge is shifted to his predecessor's account, where some economists put it and some don't. Growth per year runs from 1.7% to 3.7%, with the two Bush terms, each of which held a recession, at the bottom and Clinton's at the top.

## What the data can't settle

Whether the lag should be a year, two, or none; whether the 2020 shutdown belongs to the president who was in office, to the virus, or to the governors; whether the 2021 inflation was the relief bills, the Fed, the supply chains, or all three; and whether a president deserves credit for a Fed cycle that began before the election. Each of those is argued about in good faith, and each one flips a ranking. The honest use of these tables is to notice how little daylight separates most terms once the clock and the shocks are handled the same way, and how much of what separates them is where the cycle stood on inauguration day.
