## Short version

A lot of advertising for money and health products follows the same script: name a problem you didn't know you had, make it urgent, offer a free first step, then sell you something, sometimes just your contact information. The pattern shows up in supplements, debt relief, credit repair, Medicare plan ads and "unclaimed money" offers. In almost every case there is a free or cheaper way to get the same thing, usually from a government office or a nonprofit.

## The trap in one sentence

The ad's first job is to make you worried; selling you the cure is its second.

## How it works

The script has four beats:

- **A problem you didn't know you had,** named in alarming terms: "toxins," "brain fog," "your credit is holding you back," "you could be missing out on benefits."
- **Urgency:** a deadline, a "limited time," a counter running down.
- **A free first step:** a quiz, a quote, "see if you qualify," a free trial.
- **The real product:** a paid service, a subscription, or your name and phone number. [The FTC has described how information typed into online forms can be sold](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/documents/reports/staff-perspective-follow-lead/staff_perspective_follow_the_lead_workshop.pdf) to other companies, sometimes again and again, without the person knowing who ends up with it.

{{illustration name=worry-ad caption="The four steps on a made-up ad for an invented product."}}

None of this is new. Listerine was sold for decades as a surgical antiseptic and floor cleaner. In the 1920s, [its makers began running ads about](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/marketing-campaign-invented-halitosis-180954082/) "halitosis," a medical-sounding name for bad breath, showing the lonely, unmarried people it supposedly ruined. The campaign worked so well that marketing historians still call fear-based selling "the halitosis appeal."

## Where you'll see it

**Supplements.** Vitamins, pills and powders don't need FDA approval before they're sold. When a label claims a supplement supports some part of the body, [federal law requires the small print](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/21/343) you've probably skipped: "This statement has not been evaluated by the Food and Drug Administration. This product is not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any disease." Advertising is the FTC's job, and the FTC expects health claims to be backed by ["competent and reliable scientific evidence,"](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/Health-Products-Compliance-Guidance.pdf) generally meaning clinical trials in people. It brings cases when they aren't. [In July 2026 it finalized an order](https://www.ftc.gov/node/334533) against a company selling height-growth supplements for children and teenagers, over growth claims it said lacked that evidence and over fake reviews. Some supplements are recommended for specific people, like folic acid before pregnancy or vitamin D for a diagnosed deficiency. The question to ask isn't whether supplements work; it's whether this one does what the ad says, for you, and a doctor or pharmacist can answer that for free.

**Debt relief.** Ads promise to cut your debt in half. Debt settlement companies [typically charge 15% to 25% of the debt you enroll](https://www.moneylion.com/learn/debt/relief/how-much-do-debt-settlement-companies-charge), and many tell you to stop paying your creditors while you save up, which damages your credit and can bring late fees and collection calls. [Forgiven debt can count as taxable income](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc431). Since 2010, companies that sell debt relief by phone [have been barred from charging fees before they actually settle a debt](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-310/section-310.4). The free or cheaper alternative is a nonprofit credit counselor, who can often set up a repayment plan with lower interest.

**Credit repair.** No company can legally remove accurate negative information from your credit report, and under federal law [credit repair companies can't charge you until they've done the work they promised](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/1679b). Anything they can do about mistakes, you can do yourself for free (see [how credit scores work](how-credit-scores-work.html)).

**Medicare plan ads.** Each fall, TV fills with ads promising extra benefits if you call a number. In 2023, after complaints, federal officials began reviewing Medicare Advantage TV ads; [they rejected more than 300 of about 1,700 insurer ads](https://kffhealthnews.org/news/article/medicare-advantage-deceptive-sales-tactics-federal-crackdown/) in the first season as misleading, and 192 of 250 from marketing firms. Free, unbiased help comes from [the official Medicare plan finder](https://www.medicare.gov/plan-compare/) and your state's health insurance assistance program, [known as SHIP](https://www.shiphelp.org/).

**"You may be owed money."** States hold forgotten bank balances, refunds and deposits until their owners claim them. Searching for yours and claiming it is free [through your state's official program or MissingMoney.com](https://unclaimed.org/?p=1103). Finder services that contact you offering to recover it typically keep a percentage, and some letters that look official are scams.

## A worked example

An ad offers a supplement free for 14 days: "just pay $4.95 shipping." The fine print says that if you don't cancel, you'll be enrolled in a monthly plan at $79.95.

## How to avoid it

**Notice the worry first.** If an ad's opening is about a problem you'd never heard of, slow down. That's the script working.

**Find the free version.** Before you call a number or fill in a form, search for the government or nonprofit option: a doctor or pharmacist, a nonprofit credit counselor, AnnualCreditReport.com, Medicare's plan finder or SHIP, your state's unclaimed property office.

**Read the small print on "free."** Look for "if you don't cancel," "auto-ship," "membership" or "continuity plan." Cancel a trial the day you start it if the company allows it.

**Don't fill in the form to "see if you qualify."** Your name and number may be the product. Expect calls from companies you've never heard of.

**Ignore the countdown.** Real deadlines, like Medicare's enrollment periods, are set by law and published by the government, not by a timer on a web page.

**Check for a license or a record.** Your state attorney general and the FTC publish actions against companies; the CFPB has a public complaint database for financial products.

## The bigger picture

Different agencies police different ads. The FTC covers most advertising, including supplements, debt relief and credit repair, and the CFPB also polices debt relief; the FDA covers supplement labels; the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services covers Medicare plan marketing. How tight the rules should be is a live argument.

For Medicare plans, federal officials tightened marketing rules in 2023 and 2024 and began screening TV ads. [In April 2026, CMS rolled back several of those rules](https://www.kff.org/medicare/changes-to-the-medicare-advantage-program-enhance-some-consumer-protections-but-roll-back-others/), including limits on superlatives like "best" and a 48-hour wait before an agent's sales meeting, arguing that the rules added burden and delay, and that the general ban on misleading marketing already covers claims like "best." Critics say the rollbacks weaken protections for older people who are easy targets for confusing pitches.

For supplements, the 1994 law that governs them lets products reach shelves without approval and puts the burden on the FDA to show one is unsafe.

**Supporters of tighter rules** argue that fear-based ads target people who are anxious, older or short on money, that enforcement after the fact comes too late for most victims, and that pre-approval or stronger disclosure would prevent harm rather than punish it. In July 2026, [the American College of Physicians called for](https://www.nutraingredients.com/Article/2026/07/17/not-prescription-drugs-industry-pushes-back-on-acp-proposal/) the FDA to approve supplements before they're sold.

**Those wary of tighter rules** argue that truthful advertising is protected speech and lets people learn about options they wouldn't otherwise hear about, that most products and companies follow the law, and that existing laws already ban deception. The supplement industry points out that supplements are regulated, including by manufacturing standards the FDA enforces, and argues that requiring drug-style approval would limit access to safe products, and that better enforcement of existing law is the answer.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 9, 2026.
