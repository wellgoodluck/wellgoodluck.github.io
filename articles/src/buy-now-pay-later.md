## Short version

Buy now, pay later splits a purchase into a few payments, usually four, often with no interest. Used once, for something you could afford anyway, it's harmless. The trap is stacking several plans at once until the small payments add up to a big one. Missed payments bring late fees, and some newer credit scores now count them.

## The trap in one sentence

Each plan looks small on its own, and nobody adds them up for you.

## How it works

At checkout, you pick "pay in 4." You pay a quarter of the price today and the rest in three more installments, usually every two weeks, charged automatically to a debit or credit card.

If every payment goes through on time, you typically pay no interest and no fees. That's the appeal, and for a single, planned purchase it can be cheaper than carrying a credit card balance.

The problems come from how easy it is:

- **Plans pile up.** Each one lives in a different app with its own schedule. In a LendingTree survey this spring, [63% of users said they had held more than one](https://www.lendingtree.com/personal/buy-now-pay-later-loan-statistics/) buy now, pay later loan at the same time.
- **Late payments are common.** In the Federal Reserve's latest survey of households, [26% of users said they had paid late](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-credit.htm), and some private surveys put it near half.
- **It's moving to everyday spending.** In the same LendingTree survey, [29% of borrowers said they'd used these loans for groceries](https://www.lendingtree.com/personal/buy-now-pay-later-loan-statistics/), up from 14% two years earlier.

## A worked example

Over one month, you use buy now, pay later four times:

None of these payments feels big. But if their schedules line up, $142.50 can come out of your checking account in a single week, on top of rent and everything else. If one of those automatic charges hits an empty account, you can end up with a late fee from the payment company and an overdraft fee from your bank for the same missed payment (see [overdraft fees](overdraft-fees.html)). In the Fed's survey, [11% of users said a buy now, pay later payment had set off an overdraft](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/2026-economic-well-being-of-us-households-in-2025-credit.htm) or similar fee.

## How to avoid it

**One plan at a time.** If you already have a plan running, wait until it's paid off before starting another.

**Only for things you could buy today.** If you couldn't pay the full price right now, splitting it into four doesn't make it affordable. It just moves the problem two weeks out.

**Put every due date on one calendar.** The apps won't do this for you. Your phone's calendar will.

**Pay from a credit card you pay in full, or keep a cushion in checking.** A failed debit payment can mean two fees instead of one.

**Know the credit score stakes.** [FICO announced scores that include buy now, pay later history](https://investors.fico.com/news-releases/news-release-details/fico-unveils-groundbreaking-credit-scores-incorporate-buy-now) in June 2025, available to lenders that fall. Most lenders still use older scores that don't, but that's changing, and a missed payment sent to collections hurts any score.

## The bigger picture

Buy now, pay later grew up outside most of the rules that cover credit cards, and Washington hasn't settled what to do about it.

In 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau issued an interpretation saying providers had to give borrowers credit-card-style rights, including dispute and refund protections. That interpretation was withdrawn in 2025. Credit reporting is also uneven: the Congressional Research Service found that [only one major company, Affirm, reports all of its pay-in-4 loans](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-02-18_R48858_ff6a61f61022347480affb93afe0f3c6ca2be927.html) to the credit bureaus. Some states have stepped in; [New York passed its own buy now, pay later law in 2025](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/06/new-york-enacts-first-of-its-kind-law-to-license-buy-now-pay-later-lenders), though it doesn't take effect until the state finishes writing the rules.

**Supporters of more regulation** argue these are loans and should come with the same disclosures, dispute rights and credit reporting as other credit, so borrowers and future lenders can see the whole picture.

**Opponents** argue that interest-free installment plans are cheaper than credit cards for most users, that heavy rules would make them less available to people with thin credit, and that existing consumer protection law already covers deceptive practices. They also point out that, counted loan by loan rather than by user, [only about 4% of pay-in-4 loans were charged a late fee](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-02-18_R48858_ff6a61f61022347480affb93afe0f3c6ca2be927.html) in CFPB data, averaging under $10.

These rules may change. This page reflects the situation as of November 8, 2026.
