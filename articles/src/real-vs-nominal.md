## In one sentence

A nominal figure is measured in dollars as they are; a real figure is the same thing with inflation taken out, so that it measures goods rather than money.

## The analogy

A ruler that shrinks. Dollars are the ruler everything gets measured with, and since 2019 the ruler has shrunk by about a quarter. Measured with it, your pay grew a lot. Measured in what it buys, it grew a little. "Real" means re-measuring with a ruler that stays the same length: this year's dollars, all the way back.

The pair turns up everywhere, and the nominal one is usually the headline:

- **Wages.** Typical hourly pay for production and nonsupervisory workers is up about 38% since September 2019. Prices are up about 31%. The real raise over seven years is about 5%, less than 1% a year, which is why a 38% raise feels like treading water.
- **Interest.** A 4% savings yield with 3.7% inflation earns about 0.3% real. In 2021–22 the Fed's rate was zero and inflation ran 7%, so holding cash lost 7% a year. The real Fed funds line on the almanac is this subtraction done every month.
- **Debt.** A fixed mortgage payment is nominal and never changes, so inflation shrinks it in real terms. That is the saver's loss arriving on the borrower's side of the ledger. The federal debt works the same way, which is why inflation is sometimes called the quiet default.
- **Growth.** The almanac's GDP figure is real. Nominal GDP grew about 9% in 2022; real GDP grew about 2%. The difference was prices, not production.
- **Your memory.** A 2% raise with no inflation reads as stingy; a 5% raise with 4% inflation reads as generous. The first is worth more. Economists call this money illusion, and it is part of why the 2020s feel worse than the real figures say.

## Where the analogy breaks

A ruler shrinks the same for everyone. Inflation doesn't. "Real" wages deflated by the CPI assume you buy the average basket; deflate by a renter's basket and the raise shrinks, by a homeowner's and it grows. The choice of ruler is itself an argument, taken up below. And the shrinking ruler matters most for things measured in dollars and held for years: cash, bonds, fixed pensions, fixed loans. The house a fixed mortgage bought is a real asset whose price rose with everything else.

## What people argue about

**Whether workers are better off than in 2019.** The averages above say yes, by a little. One side says a real gain of 5% in seven years is a lost period, and that the averages hide the people whose raises came in below the headline. The other says the gains went to the bottom: the lowest-paid quarter of workers saw real raises of several percent through 2023 while the top lost ground, the largest compression in forty years, and that benefits and the ease of finding work count too. Both are reading the same tables with different averages.

**Which ruler.** The CPI and the Fed's preferred PCE index differ by a few tenths a year, which compounds: real wages since 2019 look a few points better deflated by PCE than by CPI. Other rulers (chained CPI, CPI-W for Social Security, a basket without shelter) each move the answer, which is why a technical dispute has a political edge: the ruler you pick decides whether the 2020s read as a raise or a cut.

**Whether the Fed should think in real rates.** The Fed sets a nominal rate but judges whether policy is tight by the real one, nominal minus expected inflation, against a "neutral" real rate it cannot observe. In late 2026 the real Fed funds rate is a little over 1%. One camp says that is barely restrictive with inflation rising; the other says neutral has fallen and 1% is plenty for a job market already soft. Neither can see the neutral rate, which is why the argument never ends.
