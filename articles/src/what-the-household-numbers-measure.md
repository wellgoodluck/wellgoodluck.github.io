## In one sentence

These four numbers measure, in turn, how much of their after-tax income households set aside, how much credit card debt has fallen behind, how much of income goes to required loan payments, and what the household in the exact middle took in for the year.

They come from three agencies and four different methods. The Bureau of Economic Analysis builds the saving rate from the national accounts. The Federal Reserve Board reports card delinquency from what banks file each quarter and estimates debt payments from credit bureau records. The Census Bureau measures income once a year with a household survey. Three of the four are totals for the whole country; only income is a middle.

## The analogy

Picture every household in the country as one very large family going over its books at the end of the month. The saving rate is what's left in the envelope after the bills and the shopping. Delinquency is the stack of bills that went past their due date. Debt payments are the share of the paycheck already spoken for by the mortgage and the car loan before anything else is bought. Median income is the paycheck, except that it isn't one family's: line every household up from smallest income to largest, and it's the one in the middle.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**The saving rate: the cushion.** When households save little, spending rides on this month's paycheck, so a layoff or a price jump reaches the checkout line quickly. When they save more, there's room to absorb it. The rate was {{v saving_rate}} in {{v saving_rate period}}. Its record high was 31.8% in April 2020, when relief checks arrived and there was little open to spend them on; its low was 1.4% in July 2005, at the height of the housing boom, when rising home values did the saving for many owners.

**Card delinquency: the early warning.** A credit card is usually the bill people let slide first, so card balances fall behind before mortgages do. In {{v card_delinquency period}}, {{v card_delinquency fmt=pct2}} of card balances at commercial banks were 30 or more days past due. The rate peaked at 6.77% in the second quarter of 2009 and reached its low, 1.53%, in the third quarter of 2021, when relief money and a year of staying home let people pay balances down. What falling behind costs is set by the card rate: banks' average rate on card accounts was {{v card_rate fmt=pct1}} in {{v card_rate period}}.

**Debt payments: the share already spoken for.** Required payments on mortgages and consumer loans took {{v debt_payments}} of after-tax income in {{v debt_payments period}}. The highest since the measure begins, in 2005, was 15.8% at the end of 2007, just before the housing crash; the lowest was 9.1% in early 2021. Most mortgages carry fixed rates, so the higher rates since 2022 reach new borrowers and leave most existing payments where they were.

**Median income: the middle paycheck.** The Census Bureau reported in September 2026 that the middle household took in $87,460 in 2025, 2.6% more than in 2024 after inflation and the highest since its records begin in 1967. The previous high, in 2019, was $85,320 in 2025 dollars. That is income before taxes. After taxes and tax credits, the Census Bureau put the middle at $76,060, which was not a record: it was higher in 2020 and 2021, when pandemic relief added to it.

## What moves them

**Saving:** income minus spending. Tax cuts and relief payments push it up for a while; so does fear of a layoff. Rising stock and home prices tend to pull it down, because people who feel wealthier save less out of each paycheck. Interest rates cut both ways: savings accounts pay more, and borrowers have more to pay.

**Delinquency:** job losses first, then what debt costs. A card balance at a rate above 20% compounds quickly, so a missed month becomes harder to catch up on.

**Debt payments:** new borrowing, the rates on new loans, and income growth. Income rising faster than debt pulls the share down, as it did in 2021.

**Median income:** pay, hours and jobs, set against prices. A year with fast inflation can shrink it even as paychecks grow, as in 2022.

## How to read this quarter's numbers

**The saving rate gets revised, sometimes a lot.** It is income minus spending, two large numbers, so a small revision to either moves it. In September 2024 the government's annual update raised the second quarter of that year from 3.3% to 5.2%. Read the direction over several months, not the tenths.

**Delinquency is a bank figure, two months late.** It covers commercial banks only and arrives about sixty days after each quarter ends. The New York Fed's quarterly household debt report counts from credit records across all lenders and uses different thresholds, so its card figures run on a different scale; compare each with its own past, not with the other.

**Debt payments start in 2005.** The Federal Reserve rebuilt the measure from credit bureau records starting with its 2024 releases and publishes it from 2005 on, so it can't be lined up against the 1980s or 1990s.

**Median income is a year late and a survey.** The 2025 figure carries a margin of error of about $1,000 either way, so a change smaller than that may not be a change. It counts cash income only: not health insurance from an employer, not food assistance, not the value of a home someone owns.

**Read them together.** A low saving rate with falling delinquency suggests confidence; a low saving rate with rising delinquency suggests strain. In the middle of 2026 the first was low and the second was drifting down.

## Where the analogy breaks

There is no one family. There are more than 130 million households, and three of these four numbers add them all together. A household that saves a great deal and one that saves nothing average out to a household that saves some, which describes neither. Federal Reserve Board economists, looking at households' whole working lives, found in 2025 that the highest earners save a far larger share than the bottom half does, so a national rate can hold steady while many households have no cushion at all.

Delinquency is a share of dollars, not of people: one large balance going bad weighs as much as several small ones. And the median is a middle, not a typical life. A household of one and a household of five both count once.

## What people argue about

**Whether the low saving rate is a warning.** In September 2026 the rate was lower than in nine of every ten months since 1959. One side reads that as a thin cushion: households are spending most of what comes in, and a downturn would cut spending faster than usual. The other side points out that the rate has been revised up sharply before, and that households whose stocks and homes gained value since 2020 have less reason to save out of income. Both can be true at once for different households; the national figure can't say how many are in each group.

**Whether incomes have kept up.** One side: the median household's income after inflation reached a record in 2025, above the 2019 peak. The other side: the after-tax middle was not a record, and the costs that loom largest for many families, such as rent and car insurance, rose faster than prices overall in 2021 through 2024 (see [Four prices inside inflation](four-prices-inside-inflation.html)). Which measure fits depends on which household you mean.
