## Short version

Car loans have stretched to keep monthly payments manageable: [the average new-car loan now runs about 70 months](https://www.edmunds.com/car-news/edmunds-insights-q1-2026-report.html), close to six years. A longer loan lowers the payment but raises the total cost, and because cars lose value fast, it raises the odds you'll owe more than the car is worth. Nearly three in ten trade-ins toward new cars this year were "underwater," and rolling that debt into the next loan makes it worse.

## The trap in one sentence

The dealer asks what monthly payment you want, then stretches the loan until you hear that number.

## How it works

A car loan has three dials: the price, the interest rate and the length. Change the length and the monthly payment moves the most, which is why it's the dial salespeople like to turn.

{{illustration name=car-price-tag caption="The monthly payment is on the front of the tag. The total is on the back."}}

The catch is that a car is a shrinking asset. A new car typically loses a large share of its value in the first few years. A short loan pays down the balance faster than the car loses value. A long loan pays it down slowly, and you can spend years owing more than you could sell the car for. That gap is called negative equity, or being upside down.

Negative equity becomes a real problem when you want a different car. If you trade in a car worth $15,000 that you still owe $21,000 on, the $6,000 difference doesn't disappear. Dealers will often add it to your new loan, so you start the next car already behind.

## A worked example

You borrow $35,000 for a new car at 6.9%, [about the average rate for new-car loans earlier this year](https://www.edmunds.com/car-news/edmunds-insights-q1-2026-report.html).

The seven-year loan saves $164 a month and costs $2,745 more in interest. After five years, the five-year borrower owns the car outright. The seven-year borrower still owes nearly $12,000 on a car that has [typically lost around 40% of its value](https://tflcar.com/2026/03/used-car-depreciation-five-years-iseecars-study/) by then.

Now suppose you trade that car in early and roll $6,884 of negative equity, the average for underwater trade-ins in the second quarter of this year, into a new loan. [Edmunds found buyers who did that](https://www.edmunds.com/press/q2-new-vehicle-purchases-with-negative-equity-trade-ins-hit-record-monthly-payments-and-interest-costs-edmunds-reports.html) had an average payment of $944 a month, compared with $777 for new-car buyers overall, and were projected to pay $16,270 in interest over the loan, compared with $9,811.

## How to avoid it

**Shop the total price and the loan separately.** Get a loan offer from a credit union or bank before you visit the dealer. Then negotiate the car's price as if you're paying cash.

**Pick the shortest loan you can afford.** Five years or less is a good target. If only a seven-year loan makes the payment work, the car probably costs more than your budget.

**Put real money down.** A down payment of 20% or so helps keep the loan below the car's value from the start.

**Don't roll old debt into a new loan.** If you're underwater, keeping your current car longer while you pay it down is usually the cheapest move.

**Read the add-ons.** Extended warranties, paint protection and similar extras are often folded into the loan, where you pay interest on them for years. You can usually say no.

## The bigger picture

Longer loans are a response to prices. Edmunds put the average amount financed for a new car at [a record $43,759 at the end of 2025](https://www.carscoops.com/2026/01/more-americans-than-ever-are-paying-over-1000-in-monthly-car-payments/), and interest rates rose too. Stretching the loan was the way lenders and buyers kept payments within reach.

**Defenders of longer loans** argue they make reliable new cars affordable for people who need them for work, and that a buyer who keeps a car for ten years can come out fine.

**Critics** argue that long loans hide the real price, trap buyers in negative equity, and leave them exposed if the car is totaled or they need to sell. Edmunds found that [over 40% of new-car purchases with negative equity](https://www.edmunds.com/car-news/edmunds-q4-2025-insights-report.html) late last year were financed for 84 months.

Both are right about different buyers. The deciding question is whether you'll keep the car well past the end of the loan.
