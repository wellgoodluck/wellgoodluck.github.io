## Short version

Mistakes and fraud show up on statements all the time: a charge billed twice, a refund that never posted, a few dollars from a store you've never visited. Federal law gives you strong rights to get that money back, but only if you speak up in time, usually within 60 days of the statement that shows the problem. Wait longer and some of that protection goes away. The fix is cheap: look at every account at least once a month, and turn on alerts so the big surprises reach you within minutes.

## The trap in one sentence

Your protections run on a deadline, and the deadline doesn't care whether you opened the statement.

## How it works

**Credit cards: the billing-error clock.** The Fair Credit Billing Act of 1974 covers mistakes on a credit card bill: a charge you didn't make, the wrong amount, a duplicate charge, something you paid for that never arrived, a payment or refund that wasn't credited. To use it, you send the card company a written notice that reaches it within 60 days after the first statement showing the error was sent. The company has 30 days to acknowledge it and two billing cycles (no more than 90 days) to fix it or explain why the charge is correct. While it investigates, you don't have to pay the disputed amount, and it can't report it as late. Calling the number on the card often works too, and many issuers handle disputes online, but the written notice is what triggers the legal rights.

**Credit cards: theft.** If your card or card number is stolen and used, federal law caps what you owe at $50. Visa, Mastercard and the big issuers go further with their own "zero liability" policies, so most people owe nothing. Those policies are company promises, not law, and they come with conditions. [Visa's](https://www.visa.com/en-us/personal/security/zero-liability-policy.html) and Mastercard's also cover most debit cards, which narrows the gap below, though it doesn't close it.

**Debit cards: a different law, and a stricter clock.** Debit cards, ATM withdrawals and other electronic transfers out of a bank account fall under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and its rule, Regulation E. The more you wait, the more you can lose:

<table><thead><tr><th>If your debit card or login is stolen and you tell the bank...</th><th>The most you can lose</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Within 2 business days of finding out</td><td>$50</td></tr><tr><td>After that, but within 60 days of the statement showing the theft</td><td>$500</td></tr><tr><td>More than 60 days after that statement</td><td>Up to $500, plus everything taken after the 60 days, until you report it</td></tr></tbody></table>

If no card or login was lost, only your card number or account details, and you report it within 60 days of the statement, you owe nothing.

**Whose money is missing.** This is the part people don't expect. When a thief uses your credit card, the money that's gone is the card company's, and you dispute a bill you haven't paid yet. When a thief uses your debit card, the money is gone from your checking account. The bank generally has 10 business days to investigate. If it needs longer, it generally has to put the money back temporarily within those 10 days (it can hold back up to $50, and if you reported by phone it can ask you to confirm in writing within 10 business days), and it can take up to 45 days (longer for new accounts and some card purchases) to finish. Visa's and Mastercard's policies push banks to move faster on their debit cards, but until the money is back, it's yours that's missing. Meanwhile your rent check or car payment can bounce. That's why so much money advice says to use a credit card for online shopping and travel, and to pay it off in full every month ([the minimum payment trap](credit-card-minimum-payments.html) explains why the paying-off part matters).

**What hides in a statement:**

- **Duplicates.** A restaurant runs your card twice, or a gym bills you twice a month.
- **Refunds that never post.** You returned it; the store said five to seven days; it never showed up.
- **Test charges.** Thieves who buy stolen card numbers often try a tiny charge first, a dollar or two, to see if the card works. A small charge you don't recognize is a reason to call, not to shrug.
- **Bank and card fees.** [Overdraft fees](overdraft-fees.html), late fees, foreign transaction fees and annual fees you forgot about.
- **Subscriptions.** Not errors if you signed up, but this is where [they pile up](subscription-creep.html).

## A worked example

**The stolen debit card.** Your wallet is stolen on a Monday and you notice that night. By Tuesday the thief has spent $300 with your debit card, and over the next three weeks another $900.

<table><thead><tr><th>When you report it</th><th>What you can be held responsible for</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Tuesday or Wednesday (within 2 business days)</td><td>$50</td></tr><tr><td>Ten days later</td><td>Up to $500</td></tr><tr><td>If the same $1,200 were charged to a credit card</td><td>$50 by law, usually $0 under the card's policy</td></tr></tbody></table>

In the debit card rows, whatever the thief took is out of your checking account until the bank finishes its investigation or gives you a temporary credit: about $300 if you report it right away, and more each day you wait.

**The duplicate gym charge.** Your gym starts billing your credit card $29 twice a month instead of once. You don't look at a statement for six months.

<table><thead><tr><th></th><th>Amount</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>Extra charges over six months</td><td>6 × $29 = $174</td></tr><tr><td>On statements sent within the last 60 days, so covered by your billing-error rights</td><td>about 2 × $29 = $58</td></tr><tr><td>Older charges</td><td>Up to the card company's or the gym's goodwill</td></tr></tbody></table>

Each statement starts its own 60-day clock, so the latest charges are still protected. The older ones may still be refunded if you ask, and card network rules sometimes reach back further, but the law no longer makes the card company step in.

## How to avoid it

**Pick a day each month.** Ten minutes with each statement, checking account and card, is enough. Look for charges you don't recognize, charges that appear twice, and refunds you're still waiting on.

**Turn on alerts.** Most banks and card apps can text or email you for every purchase, or for any purchase over an amount you choose, and when your balance falls below a set level. Alerts catch theft in minutes instead of weeks.

**If something looks wrong, act right away.** For a stolen card or login, call the number on the back of the card that day and lock or freeze the card in the app. For a billing error on a credit card, call, and also send the written notice to the address the statement lists for billing errors (not the payment address). Keep a copy.

**Look up what you don't recognize before assuming it's fraud.** Many charges show a parent company's name, not the store's. Your card app can often show the merchant's full name and location.

**Use a credit card for risky purchases if you can pay it off.** Online shopping, hotels, gas pumps and rental cars are where card numbers most often get stolen or charges go wrong. If you don't trust yourself with a credit card yet, a debit card is fine: just watch it more closely.

**Go paperless only if you'll actually look.** An e-statement nobody opens starts the same 60-day clock as a paper one.

## The bigger picture

Card fraud is common. The Federal Trade Commission received [more than 449,000 reports of credit card identity theft in 2024](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/csn-annual-data-book-2024.pdf), the latest year with a full breakdown and its largest identity theft category, and those are only the people who reported it. [The Federal Reserve's survey of 2025](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/files/other20260513a1.pdf) found that among adults with incomes under $50,000, the typical person who lost money to fraud lost $400, and 4 in 10 adults at that income said they could not cover even a $100 emergency expense from savings.

The deadlines themselves aren't in dispute: they've been in the law since the 1970s. The current argument is about who makes companies follow them. One place people turn after a bank says no is a complaint to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, which forwards it to the company for a response. Since February 2025, the administration has stopped most of the bureau's enforcement work, tried to lay off most of its staff and stopped requesting its funding, while the employees' union, consumer groups, 21 states and the District of Columbia have fought those moves in court. In June 2026 the full federal appeals court in Washington [kept in place an order blocking mass layoffs](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/appeals-court-sides-with-cfpbs-union-blocks-job-cuts) while the case goes on, and as of September 2026, [federal judges in three cases](https://www.americanbanker.com/news/another-judge-rejects-trumps-attempt-to-halt-cfpb-funding) had rejected the administration's argument that the bureau couldn't legally be funded. In July 2025, Congress cut the most the bureau can draw from the Federal Reserve [by about 46%](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12578.html).

{{due 2027-02-15 "CFPB: check the en banc layoff case, the funding cases and the bureau's staffing; update The bigger picture"}}

**Supporters of a smaller bureau** argue that banks already answer to their own federal regulators, that nonbank companies answer to the FTC and the states, so the bureau duplicates other agencies, and that its funding, which comes from the Federal Reserve rather than from Congress each year, made it unaccountable. They say Congress should fund it through regular appropriations and narrow what it can regulate.

**Critics of the cuts** argue that states can't supervise national banks, so no one else can do much of what the bureau did, that consumer groups counted [more than 830,000 unresolved complaints](https://advocacy.consumerreports.org/press_release/cfpb-on-life-support-one-year-after-it-was-targeted-for-shutdown) in early 2026, and that with less enforcement, banks and financial companies may have less incentive to take complaints seriously.

Either way, your rights under the Fair Credit Billing Act and Regulation E remain law, and you can still use them directly with your bank or card company.
