## The short version

[Part one](how-a-property-tax-rate-is-set.html) showed how a rate comes out of a budget and a digest, and how a rollback rate holds revenue from existing property flat. States put limits on that process at three different points. Some limit the decision: a government may raise more than last year, but must call it a tax increase in public and sometimes get a supermajority or voters' approval (Georgia, Florida, Texas, Utah). Some limit the total: revenue can grow only by a set percentage plus new construction unless voters agree (Massachusetts, Illinois, Washington, Michigan). And some limit each property: its taxable value can rise only so fast, whatever the market does (California, Michigan, Florida, and from 2027 Georgia). The first two hold the total; only the third protects a single homeowner from a fast-rising value, and it shifts taxes toward newer buyers and other kinds of property. In 2026, Florida voters approved much larger homestead exemptions, and Georgia made its cap on homestead value growth apply everywhere in the state.

## The analogy

Think of three ways to keep a household from overspending. One makes it announce every raise in its allowance at a family meeting. One caps the allowance at last year's plus a fixed amount. One caps what each family member can be asked to chip in. The first relies on embarrassment and votes, the second on a formula, and the third protects individuals but says nothing about how the total gets made up.

## Limit the decision: rollback rules

These are often called truth-in-taxation laws. The government can still raise more from existing property. The law requires it to say so, and sometimes to clear a higher bar.

Two details in the table changed recently. Florida's majority-vote ceiling used to grow each year with Floridians' per capita personal income; the law signed in June 2026 ties it to the rolled-back rate instead, so any increase on existing property now takes a supermajority ([Florida Senate, SB 4-F](https://www.flsenate.gov/Committees/BillSummaries/2026F/html/4); [CBS Miami](https://www.cbsnews.com/miami/news/ron-desantis-bills-florida-city-county-property-taxes-local-june-24-2026/)). In Texas, a 2025 bill to lower the voter-approval margin from 3.5% to 2.5% for cities and counties of 75,000 or more passed both chambers in different versions and died when the House rejected the compromise, so 3.5% still applies ([Bond Buyer](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/texas-bill-to-curb-property-tax-revenue-increases-fails-to-pass)). Texas school districts use a separate formula ([Texas Comptroller](https://www.comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/property-tax/truth-in-taxation/index.php)).

What these laws don't do: none stops a government from collecting more. They make it say so, and the Texas election and the Florida supermajorities make it harder.

## Limit the total: revenue caps

Here the law caps the levy itself. New construction is usually added on top, so a growing town can still collect more; existing property, taken together, can't be asked for much more.

- **Massachusetts**, Proposition 2½ (1980): a town's levy can grow 2.5% a year plus new growth, and can't exceed 2.5% of the town's total value. Voters can approve an override to raise the yearly limit up to that ceiling, or exclude a specific debt or project, which can take the levy above both ([Massachusetts Division of Local Services](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/property-taxes-and-proposition-2-12-training-and-resources); [Massachusetts Municipal Association](https://www.mma.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/prop_2_overview_0.pdf)).
- **Illinois**, the Property Tax Extension Limitation Law: in the counties where it applies, a district's taxes can grow by the lesser of 5% or the rise in consumer prices, with new property excluded. Going further takes a referendum ([Illinois Department of Revenue](https://tax.illinois.gov/localgovernments/property/property-tax-extension-limitation-law-changes.html)).
- **Washington**: most local levies can grow 1% a year plus new construction. Voters can approve a "lid lift"; a 2026 law lets a lid lift run up to ten years instead of six ([MRSC](https://mrsc.org/stay-informed/mrsc-insight/april-2026/levy-lid-lift-changes)). A 2025 plan to raise the 1% limit to 3% collapsed in the legislature ([Washington State Standard](https://washingtonstatestandard.com/2025/04/21/washington-democrats-abandon-property-tax-hike/)).
- **Michigan**, the Headlee Amendment (1978): when the value of existing property in a community grows faster than inflation, its governments must cut their rates so revenue from that property grows no faster than inflation. The cuts accumulate year after year in a "millage reduction fraction," and only a vote of the people can restore the original rate ([Michigan Municipal League](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/x-FS-Headlee-Plus-2026.pdf)).

Michigan's rule is a rollback rate with no way to exceed it except at the ballot box. That's the line between the first two families: in Georgia or Utah a council can vote to go above its rollback rate; in Michigan or Massachusetts it needs voters.

## Limit each property: assessment caps

The first two families protect the total. An assessment cap protects the individual owner by limiting how fast one property's taxable value can rise.

- **California**, Proposition 13 (1978): a property is taxed on its purchase price, which can rise no more than 2% a year or inflation, whichever is lower, and is reset to market value when it sells. The rate is capped at 1% plus voter-approved debt. California's local governments don't set an annual property tax rate at all; the state divides the 1% among them in shares based on what each collected before 1978 ([Legislative Analyst's Office](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/3497)).
- **Michigan**, Proposal A (1994): a property's taxable value can rise by the lesser of 5% or inflation each year, and resets toward market value when it sells. For 2026 the inflation figure is 2.7% ([Michigan Municipal League](https://mml.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/x-FS-Headlee-Plus-2026.pdf)). Michigan has both a revenue cap and an assessment cap, so a home's taxable value is capped and its community's rates still roll back if values on existing property grow faster than inflation.
- **Florida**: a homestead's assessed value can rise no more than 3% a year or inflation, whichever is lower, under the 1992 Save Our Homes amendment; other property's assessed value is capped at 10% a year for non-school taxes ([Pinellas County Property Appraiser](https://www.pcpao.gov/learn-about/save-our-homes)). Amendment 3, approved by 62% of voters on November 3, 2026, cuts that 10% cap to 5% starting in 2027 ([Florida Chamber](https://www.flchamber.com/amendment3); [Bay News 9](https://baynews9.com/fl/tampa/news/2026/11/04/florida-voters-approve-amendment--providing-property-tax-relief-for-homeowners)).
- **Georgia**: a 2024 law created a statewide "floating" homestead exemption that holds a home's taxable value to its 2024 value plus inflation, resetting when the home sells and adding substantial improvements ([Newton County](https://www.newtoncountyga.gov/695/House-Bill-581---Save-the-Homes-Act); [Fair Assessments](https://www.fair-assessments.com/blog/georgias-home-act-senate-bill-33)), but let each county, city and school district opt out, and 316 did, including 47 counties and 123 school districts ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/blog/georgia-property-tax-reform/)). A law signed in May 2026, SB 33, ends the opt-out, so the cap applies everywhere; in the places that had opted out, the base year is 2026 and the cap takes effect with the 2027 tax year ([Georgia Municipal Association, SB 33 guide](https://www.gacities.com/articles/sb-33-the-homeownership-opportunity-and-market-equalization-act-of-2026); [Eversheds Sutherland, State and Local Tax](https://www.stateandlocaltax.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/525/2026/06/Tax-Highlights-From-Georgias-2026-Legislative-Session.pdf)). It also creates a 1% local sales tax whose proceeds pay for a further homestead exemption, with any money left over used to lower rates on all property. A county needs a local act passed by two-thirds of the legislature and a referendum, each city that wants a share needs its own, and collections can begin no earlier than January 1, 2028 ([Georgia Municipal Association, SB 33 guide](https://www.gacities.com/articles/sb-33-the-homeownership-opportunity-and-market-equalization-act-of-2026)). It is an alternative to the sales tax the 2024 law created for relief on all property, whose proceeds lower each government's rollback rate; SB 33 makes every jurisdiction eligible for that one, and a county can levy one or the other, not both ([Georgia Municipal Association, FLOST and LHOST chart](https://www.gacities.com/articles/sb-33-the-homeownership-opportunity-and-market-equalization-act-of-2026)). State Rep. Scott Holcomb, an Atlanta Democrat, has argued the law is unconstitutional because a revenue bill must start in the House and SB 33 started in the Senate ([The Current](https://thecurrentga.org/2026/05/12/governor-kemp-signs-property-tax-measure-igniting-constitutional-debate/)). {{due 2027-03-01 "Georgia SB 33: any court challenge on the origination clause; first tax year under the mandatory cap"}}

An assessment cap does not cap a bill. The rate can still rise. And because it limits one property's value but not the total levy, a government that needs the same money from a smaller capped digest raises the rate, which falls on capped and uncapped property alike. Where the rate rises to make up the difference, the added tax falls more heavily on property whose value isn't held down: recently sold homes, rentals and businesses.

## Exemptions: the other lever

Homestead exemptions take a fixed amount off a home's value before the rate applies. They don't limit what a government collects; they decide who pays it. Florida's Amendment 3 is the year's big example: the homestead exemption from non-school taxes, now $50,000, rises to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028, and is adjusted for inflation after that. New residents must live in the state five years to qualify for the full amount, though counties and cities can shorten that by a two-thirds vote. The amendment also limits what counties and cities may spend property tax revenue on to listed categories such as public safety, infrastructure and debt, unless their governing bodies approve other uses, and directs the Legislature to set a schedule for eliminating the remaining non-school homestead tax ([Florida Chamber](https://www.flchamber.com/amendment3); [Bay News 9](https://baynews9.com/fl/tampa/news/2026/11/04/florida-voters-approve-amendment--providing-property-tax-relief-for-homeowners)). Florida school taxes keep the existing $25,000 exemption. {{due 2027-05-15 "Florida Amendment 3 implementing bill (2027 session) and phase-out schedule"}}

When homesteads are exempted more, the digest shrinks, and a government that keeps its budget must raise its rate on what remains. That's why Craig Kincheloe, a real estate executive, told Bay News 9 after the vote that the amendment may turn out to be "a tax shift instead of a tax cut" ([Bay News 9](https://baynews9.com/fl/tampa/news/2026/11/04/florida-voters-approve-amendment--providing-property-tax-relief-for-homeowners)). Whether it does depends on how much local governments cut and how much they raise rates on rentals, second homes and businesses, which the 5% cap on their assessments partly limits.

## What people argue about

**Assessment caps.** One side: they make tax bills predictable and keep long-time owners, especially retirees on fixed incomes, from being taxed out of homes whose market value soared. The other: two neighbors in identical houses can pay very different taxes depending on when they bought, newer buyers and businesses carry more of the load, and owners have a reason not to move. California's Legislative Analyst's Office counts Prop 13 among the factors behind Californians staying in their homes longer, and notes that an owner's relief under it grows the longer they stay ([LAO](https://lao.ca.gov/Publications/Report/3497)).

**Rollback rules or hard caps.** One side: rollback rules leave the decision with elected officials, who answer for it at the next election, and let a growing place pay for growth. The other: a notice and a hearing are easy to ignore, and only a limit that voters must lift, as in Massachusetts or Michigan, actually restrains spending.

**Exemptions.** One side: homeowners vote, own the community's property and shouldn't pay taxes that rise with paper gains in value. The other: exempting homesteads moves the tax onto renters through their landlords, and onto businesses and second homes, without lowering what local government costs.

## Where the analogy breaks

A household's members don't move away when the allowance rules change. People and businesses do: a cap or exemption that favors owners who stay can change who moves in, and a tax shifted onto businesses can show up in prices and rents. None of the three limits settles what local services should cost. They settle who decides, and who pays.
