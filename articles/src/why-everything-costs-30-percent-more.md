## The number

Consumer prices rose about 28% from 2019 to early 2026, and this year's energy surge has pushed that to about 31%. Most of it happened in 2021 and 2022, when inflation ran 5–8% a year. The rest of the time, prices crept up at the 2–3% pace people barely notice.

That average hides a lot. Eggs are up 84%, ground beef 79%, restaurant meals 51%, electricity 48%, gasoline 47%, rent 41%, auto insurance 32%, and used cars 30%. Chicken wings are cheaper than they were in 2019. If you rent, drive, and eat beef, your personal inflation ran well above the headline figure.

How we got here is really four overlapping stories, plus a fifth that started this year.

## Wave 1: Supply broke (2020–21)

The pandemic shut down the machinery that makes and moves things, and it took two years to restart. Factories closed in waves as outbreaks moved around the world. Ports backed up, with dozens of container ships anchored off Los Angeles and Long Beach waiting weeks to unload.

Shipping a container from Asia to the U.S. West Coast went from under $2,000 to over $20,000 at the 2021 peak. A shortage of semiconductors idled auto plants, and with few new cars to buy, used car prices jumped 30–40% in a year. Lumber, appliances, bikes, and furniture all had their moment.

At the same time, demand shifted violently. People who couldn't travel, eat out, or go to concerts spent that money on goods instead. The supply of goods was shrinking at exactly the moment demand for goods was surging. Goods prices moved first, and they moved hard.

## Wave 2: The money arrived (2020–22)

Into that broken supply chain, Washington poured roughly $5 trillion. Three relief packages between March 2020 and March 2021 sent stimulus checks to most households, added $600 and then $300 a week to unemployment benefits, forgave hundreds of billions in PPP loans to businesses, and expanded the child tax credit. The Federal Reserve cut interest rates to zero and bought about $4.5 trillion of bonds.

The broad money supply (M2) grew about 40% between early 2020 and early 2022. Households, with nowhere to spend, built up more than $2 trillion in savings beyond what they would normally have held. When the economy reopened, that money came out.

This is the part of the story that fits the textbook definition: too many dollars chasing too few goods. Demand that would have been absorbed by a healthy economy ran into one that couldn't produce enough. Prices did the balancing.

The Fed called it "transitory" through most of 2021 on the theory that once supply chains healed, inflation would fade on its own. That would have been right if supply were the only problem. It wasn't. Inflation peaked at 9.1% in June 2022, the highest since 1981, and the Fed spent the next year raising rates faster than at any time since the 1980s.

## Wave 3: Energy and food (2022–23)

Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, just as inflation was already running hot. Oil went above $120 a barrel and the U.S. average for gasoline passed $5 a gallon that June, a record. Natural gas prices in Europe went up tenfold, which pushed up the cost of fertilizer, which is made from it.

Russia and Ukraine together supplied about a quarter of the world's wheat exports and a large share of sunflower oil and corn. Ukrainian ports were blockaded for months. Food prices respond to energy and fertilizer with a lag of roughly a year, so grocery inflation peaked in 2023 even as energy was already cooling.

A few things made this worse in the U.S. specifically. Avian flu wiped out tens of millions of laying hens in 2022 and again in 2024–25, which is most of why eggs are up 84%. Drought shrank the cattle herd to its smallest since the 1950s, which is why beef is up 79%. Those were separate shocks that happened to land on top of everything else.

## Wave 4: The second round (2021–24)

The first three waves explain why prices spiked. This one explains why they stayed up. Once a shock raises some prices, it works its way into wages, rents, and contracts, and those don't come back down.

**Housing.** Mortgage rates fell below 3% in 2020–21, and remote work let people move. Home prices rose roughly 45% in three years. Rents follow home prices with a lag of a year to eighteen months, and shelter is about a third of the Consumer Price Index. Rent kept inflation above the Fed's target through 2023 and 2024, long after goods prices had settled down.

{{chart id=cpi range=5y ov=core_cpi_on_cpi,wages,real_wages caption="Headline and core inflation against wage growth. Real wages (the green line) fell below zero for two years from mid-2021, gained ground from spring 2023 to early 2026, and went negative again this year as energy prices rose."}}

**Wages.** The labor market got very tight in 2021–22, with two job openings for every unemployed person. Average wages rose about 25% cumulatively. That's good for workers, but it flows straight into anything made of labor: restaurants, haircuts, repairs, childcare, medical care. Services inflation was the last piece to cool.

**Auto insurance** is a good example of how the waves compound. Cars became worth more, so claims cost more. Repairs got more expensive because parts and mechanics did. Accident severity rose. Premiums went up more than 50% from 2019 to 2024, and insurers were still catching up to costs they'd already absorbed.

## Why prices never came back down

Inflation is a rate, not a level. When the Fed "beat inflation" in 2023–24, that meant prices stopped rising fast, not that they fell. A 3% inflation rate on top of a 30% increase is still a 30% increase, now growing more slowly.

Getting prices back to 2019 would require deflation, a sustained fall in the general price level. Outside the Great Depression and a few months in 2008–09, that hasn't happened in modern America, and nobody in charge wants it to. Deflation means wages fall, debts get heavier in real terms, and people delay purchases waiting for lower prices, which deepens the slump. The Fed's job is to hold inflation near 2%, not zero and not negative.

There's also a ratchet. Wages rose along with prices, and wages almost never fall in nominal terms. Every business's cost base is permanently higher, so its prices are too. In aggregate, average pay has roughly caught up with prices since 2019, but unevenly, and the memory of what things used to cost doesn't adjust. The $4 coffee feels expensive because you remember when it was $3, even if your paycheck is 25% bigger too.

## What's happening now (2025–26)

Inflation is rising again, but for a different reason than in 2021. The September 2026 report showed consumer prices up 3.7% from a year earlier, after 3.4% in August, with a 0.6% jump in the month alone. Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, was 2.6%.

The gap between those two numbers is oil. The war with Iran has disrupted tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, and gasoline is up 34% over the past year, fuel oil 74%. Gasoline alone accounted for more than half of September's monthly increase. Before that, the 2025 tariffs had already added to the price of imported goods.

This is a supply shock, closer to 1973 than to 2021. Money growth is running near its long-run average of about 6% a year, not the 25%-plus of 2020–21. A supply shock raises prices once; it only becomes lasting inflation if it spreads into wages and expectations. There are early signs of that: core prices rose 0.3% in each of the last two months, airfares are up 23% year over year as jet fuel feeds through, and producer prices excluding food and energy are up 5%.

The Fed raised rates in September and held them at 3.75–4% on October 28, with two of twelve members voting for another increase. Raising rates can't make oil, so the point isn't to lower gas prices. It's to keep the oil shock from turning into the kind of broad inflation that took three years to unwind last time.

## Who's to blame

Economists still argue about the split between supply shocks and excess demand, and the honest answer is both. Ben Bernanke and Olivier Blanchard, in a widely cited 2023 study, found that the initial surge in 2021 was mostly supply: shipping, chips, energy, and food. But they found the labor market's tightness, which came from stimulus-driven demand, is what kept inflation elevated through 2022 and 2023. Other estimates put demand closer to half the total.

A fair summary: supply shocks started it, and the fiscal and monetary response turned it from a spike into a plateau. Without the pandemic, there's no inflation. Without $5 trillion in relief and two years of zero rates, there's a shorter and smaller one.

What the evidence doesn't support is a single-cause story. "Corporate greed" doesn't explain why inflation hit every country at once, including ones with very different corporate cultures. "Printing money" doesn't explain why core inflation stayed under 3% in 2026 while gas rose 34%. The dollar's lost a quarter of its purchasing power since 2019 for about five reasons, and they all count.
