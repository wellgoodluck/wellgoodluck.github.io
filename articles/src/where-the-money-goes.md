## The short version

The money that buys vet clinics, dental offices and plumbing companies comes largely from institutions: state and local pension funds are one of the biggest investor groups, alongside foreign sovereign funds, insurers, endowments and wealthy individuals, much of it held through other funds that hide the final owners. More than 37 million Americans belong to state and local pension plans, so many teachers, police officers and firefighters are indirect owners. The managers who run the funds are paid an annual fee, about 1.6% on average for buyout funds in 2025, plus, by convention, about a fifth of the profits, which is taxed at the lower capital-gains rate. Since about 2006, buyout funds have returned roughly what the stock market did after fees, by most measures, though economists dispute it; this year private equity trailed stocks at California's two biggest pension funds. Retiring owners who sell are paid for businesses that would otherwise lose much of their value when they stop working. Deals are financed with debt, and companies bought that way go bankrupt more often. Wealth in America has grown more concentrated since 1989: the top 1% hold about a third of it. Private equity's managers are among the people at the top, but the Federal Reserve's data don't separate private equity from other wealth, and the small purchases this series has described have barely been studied. The evidence supports "the managers do very well" more clearly than it answers whether Main Street, as a whole, is better or worse off.

## The analogy

A cooperative orchard. Many families put money in, a hired manager runs it and keeps a yearly fee plus a fifth of any profit above a set level, and the orchard borrows to buy neighboring farms. If the harvest is good, everyone gains, the manager most of all for his share of the money. If it's ordinary, the families earn about what they would have elsewhere and the manager still earns his fee. If the debt can't be paid, the lenders and the farmworkers feel it first.

The families are the pension funds and other investors. The manager is the private equity firm. The neighboring farms are the clinics and shops it buys.

Figures below are as of early November 2026.

## Who puts money in

The most complete public breakdown comes from filings that large private fund managers make with the Securities and Exchange Commission. At the end of 2025, of the money in private equity funds run by large managers:

- 24.4% came through other private funds, such as funds of funds, which hide the ultimate owners;
- 14.6% from state and local government pensions;
- 10.5% from sovereign wealth funds and other foreign government investors;
- 6.7% from private pensions, 5.4% from insurers and 4.8% from nonprofits and endowments;
- 7.2% directly from individuals, and 20.8% from others ([SEC, private fund statistics, fourth quarter 2025](https://www.sec.gov/files/investment/private-fund-statistics-2025-q4.pdf)).

Wealthy individuals and family offices are also inside the "other funds" and "other" groups, so their true share is larger than the direct figure.

**Pensions.** Public pension funds tracked by the data firm Preqin, worldwide, had about $1.41 trillion allocated to private equity as of June 2023, more than any other type of investor ([Preqin](https://www.preqin.com/insights/research/reports/trending-data-public-pension-funds-are-a-key-investor-for-private-equity)). American public pensions put about 14% of their assets in it on average, a figure both the industry's trade group and its critics cite ([American Investment Council](https://www.investmentcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/AIC-2025-Pensions-Report.pdf); [Private Equity Stakeholder Project](https://pestakeholder.org/news/public-pensions-shrink-private-equity-as-headwinds-persist-returns-drag/)). More than 37 million people, including inactive members, belonged to state and local pension plans in 2025 ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026/2025-aspp.html)). Some plans are now cutting back after returns trailed stocks ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-02-13/pensions-cut-private-equity-exposure-as-returns-trail-stocks)).

**401(k)s.** In August 2025 an executive order directed the Labor Department to make it easier for 401(k) plans to offer private equity and other alternatives ([White House](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/08/democratizing-access-to-alternative-assets-for-401k-investors/)). The department withdrew its 2021 caution ([Labor Department](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/ebsa/ebsa20250812)) and in March 2026 proposed a rule giving plan fiduciaries a legal safe harbor if they document six factors, including fees, liquidity and valuation ([Labor Department](https://www.dol.gov/newsroom/releases/ebsa/ebsa20260330)). Separately, the SEC in September 2026 proposed changes to let funds sold to ordinary investors hold more private-market assets ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2026-96-sec-proposes-amendments-expand-responsible-retailization-private-markets)). Neither rule is final. {{due 2027-02-01 "Has the Labor Department finalized its March 2026 rule on investment choices in 401(k)s, or the SEC its September 2026 proposals? ## What the managers take

**Fees.** The average annual management fee for buyout funds was about 1.6% in 2025, according to Preqin data cited by Bain & Company, down from the traditional 2% ([Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/welcome-to-a-new-era-global-private-equity-report-2026/)). A study of 238 funds found that about two-thirds of what managers can expect to earn comes from fixed fees that don't depend on how the fund performs ([Metrick and Yasuda, Review of Financial Studies, 2010](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:23:y:2010:i:6:p:2303-2341)). Funds have also charged the companies they own transaction and monitoring fees, nearly $20 billion across 592 buyouts from 1995 to 2014, roughly 6% of the equity invested ([Phalippou, Rauch and Umber, via Harvard Law School Forum](https://corpgov.law.harvard.edu/2016/01/13/private-equity-portfolio-company-fees/)).

**Carried interest.** Managers also typically keep about 20% of profits, and buyout funds commonly set a minimum return to investors of 8% a year before they do ([Yale Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/refining-revenue-estimates-taxing-carried-interest); [American Investment Council](https://www.investmentcouncil.org/faqs/)). Oxford economist Ludovic Phalippou estimated that funds started from 2006 to 2015 generated about $230 billion of this "carried interest," and counted private equity multibillionaires rising from 3 in 2005 to 22 in 2020; his paper includes rebuttals from the four largest firms and the industry's main lobbying group ([Phalippou, 2020](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3623820)).

**Disclosure.** An SEC rule that would have required private fund managers to give investors quarterly statements of fees was struck down by a federal appeals court in 2024 ([Paul, Weiss](https://www.paulweiss.com/insights/client-memos/fifth-circuit-vacates-private-fund-adviser-rule)). We found no credible industry-wide total of the fees investors pay.

## What investors get

Buyout funds beat the S&P 500 after fees in all but one year's funds before 2006, by about 3% to 4% a year on average; funds started after 2005 did roughly as well as the stock market ([Harris, Jenkinson and Kaplan, 2015](https://uncipc.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/SSRN-id2597259.pdf)). Phalippou found the same for 2006–2015 funds. Kaplan and Gregory Brown argue that U.S. buyout funds started after the 2008 crisis beat the S&P 500, by an estimated 3.9% a year as of 2018, with those funds not yet fully sold ([Brown and Kaplan, Journal of Private Equity, 2019](https://doi.org/10.3905/jpe.2019.1.087)). The industry's trade group reports median ten-year returns for pensions, mostly to June 2024, of 13.5% on private equity against 9.7% on public stocks, net of fees and without adjusting for risk ([American Investment Council](https://www.investmentcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/AIC-2025-Pensions-Report.pdf)).

The latest year went the other way. In the year to June 2026, CalPERS earned a preliminary 17.0% on private equity and 24.1% on public stocks ([CalPERS](https://www.calpers.ca.gov/newsroom/calpers-news/2026/calpers-posts-preliminary-investment-return-for-fiscal-year-2025-26)); CalSTRS earned 7.5% on private equity, matching its benchmark, and 25.1% on global stocks ([CalSTRS](https://www.calstrs.com/calstrs-earns-13-9-net-return-exceeds-benchmark-in-fiscal-year-2025-26)). Both report private equity a quarter behind, to March 2026, so the periods don't line up exactly.

**Waiting for the money.** Private equity funds returned cash to investors equal to 12% of their holdings' value in 2025, just over half the 20-year average ([MSCI](https://www.msci.com/data-and-analytics/private-asset-solutions/visualizations/the-distribution-drought-in-private-equity-persists)), and buyout funds held a record $3.8 trillion of companies they hadn't sold ([Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/welcome-to-a-new-era-global-private-equity-report-2026/)). Much of the past few years' returns are still estimates, not cash.

## Taxes

Carried interest held more than three years is usually taxed as a long-term capital gain, at 20% plus a 3.8% investment-income tax, rather than as ordinary income, where the top rate is 37% ([IRS, capital gains](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409); [IRS, investment income tax](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/net-investment-income-tax); [CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/60946)). The Congressional Budget Office estimated that taxing it as ordinary income would raise $13.0 billion over ten years ([CBO](https://www.cbo.gov/budget-options/60946)); the Yale Budget Lab estimated that a 2026 Senate bill would raise $87.7 billion, against $47.5 billion using older methods, and a broader version about $100 billion ([Yale Budget Lab](https://budgetlab.yale.edu/research/refining-revenue-estimates-taxing-carried-interest)). A change was proposed but not enacted in the 2022 Inflation Reduction Act (same source), and the 2025 tax law left it alone ([Kirkland & Ellis](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2025/07/final-one-big-beautiful-bill-act)).

## Sellers

For the vet, dentist or contractor who sells, a roll-up can turn a working business into a lump of cash. Economists studying top earners found that profits at the closely held businesses they own fall by about three-quarters after the owner retires or dies prematurely, and treated about three-quarters of such profit as a return to the owner's own skills and work ([Smith, Yagan, Zidar and Zwick, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2019](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:134:y:2019:i:4:p:1675-1745)). About half of the owners of businesses with employees were 55 or older in 2018 ([Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/content/dam/Census/library/visualizations/2020/comm/business-owners-ages.pdf)). We found no data on what sellers typically receive. Most pay capital-gains tax on the sale; a federal break that can exclude part of such gains applies only to stock in C corporations and excludes businesses performing health services, which generally covers vet and dental practices, and the excluded amount is capped ([26 U.S.C. § 1202](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1202); [Kirkland & Ellis](https://www.kirkland.com/publications/kirkland-alert/2025/07/final-one-big-beautiful-bill-act)).

## Debt and risk

Buyouts have long relied on borrowed money. From 1980 to 2008, debt averaged about 70% of the price in 1,157 buyouts studied, more when credit was cheap ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/digest/sep10/determinants-leverage-and-pricing-buyouts)); Bain estimates it is closer to 30% to 40% today ([Bain & Company](https://www.bain.com/insights/welcome-to-a-new-era-global-private-equity-report-2026/)). Much lending to midsize companies now comes from private lenders rather than banks: private credit reached about $1.34 trillion in the U.S. by mid-2024 ([Federal Reserve](https://fedinprint.org/item/fedgfn/101122)). Critics say some funds extract dividends by loading companies with debt ([Cornell University, on Appelbaum and Batt](https://catherwood.library.cornell.edu/book_of_the_month/private-equity-at-work-when-wall-street-manages-main-street)).

Companies bought this way fail more often. About 20% of large public companies taken private in leveraged buyouts from 1980 to 2006 went bankrupt within ten years, against about 2% of comparable public companies ([Ayash and Rastad, Finance Research Letters, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.frl.2020.101452)). S&P Global first counted a record 110 bankruptcies of companies backed by private equity or venture capital in 2024, later revised to 81, and counted 66 in 2025 through mid-December, as more distressed companies restructured out of court ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/market-intelligence/en/news-insights/articles/2026/1/us-bankruptcy-filings-drop-for-private-equity-backed-companies-in-2025-96404421)). Earlier parts of this series described an HVAC roll-up's bankruptcy and two dental groups whose lenders took over in 2026. When that happens, lenders take losses and the original investors can lose their stake, while management fees are paid regardless of how a fund performs ([Metrick and Yasuda](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:rfinst:v:23:y:2010:i:6:p:2303-2341)).

## Workers

The largest study, of about 9,800 buyouts from 1980 to 2013 using Census data, found that results depended on the kind of deal. When one private company was bought by a private equity fund, the pattern in this series, employment grew about 13% over two years compared with similar companies; when a public company was taken private, it fell about 13%. Productivity rose about 8%, and pay per worker fell about 1.7% ([Davis et al., via NBER](https://www.nber.org/digest/feb20/economic-effects-private-equity-buyouts)). Some of the most negative findings are in health care: after private equity bought nursing homes, deaths among short-stay Medicare patients during their stay and the following 90 days rose about 10% ([Gupta et al., via NBER](https://www.nber.org/digest/202104/how-patients-fare-when-private-equity-funds-acquire-nursing-homes)), and after it bought dermatology, gastroenterology and ophthalmology practices, payments per claim rose about 11% ([Singh et al., JAMA Health Forum](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9440392)). We found no large study of the small add-on purchases of clinics and shops that this series describes.

## Wealth at the top

The Federal Reserve's estimates show wealth growing more concentrated since 1989. In mid-2026 the top 1% of households held 32.5% of all net worth, up from 22.8% in 1989; the bottom half held 2.3%, down from 3.5% ([Federal Reserve, via FRED, top 1%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01134); [bottom 50%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBSB50215)). The top 1% held 50.9% of the stocks and mutual fund shares households own directly, up from 42.8%; stock held through pensions is counted separately ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WFRBST01122)). It also held about half of the equity in private businesses ([Federal Reserve, Distributional Financial Accounts](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/z1/dataviz/dfa/distribute/table/)). Finance professionals' share of the top 1% of earners rose 5.5 percentage points from 1979 to 2005 ([Bakija, Cole and Heim, via Conversable Economist](https://conversableeconomist.com/2012/04/20/occupations-of-the-1/)).

The Fed doesn't break out private equity, so these figures can't say how much of the trend it explains. What can be said is where its money goes: fees and carried interest to a relatively small number of managers, taxed at capital-gains rates; returns to investors that, since 2006, have roughly matched the stock market, shared in part by pension members; and, for sellers, a payout for businesses they built.

## Where the analogy breaks

An orchard's families can see the books and vote out the manager. Investors in a private equity fund commit their money for ten years or more, usually can't withdraw it, and see fees and valuations only as the manager reports them. And the orchard's neighbors don't usually rely on it for medical care.

## What people argue about

**Opportunity.** The American Investment Council, the industry's trade group, argues that private equity has given pensions their best long-term returns, benefiting about 34 million public workers and retirees, and says that in 2024 more than 85% of current private equity investments were in small businesses ([American Investment Council](https://www.investmentcouncil.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/AIC-2025-Pensions-Report.pdf)). Economists such as Kaplan point to returns above public markets over long periods, and the Davis study found jobs and productivity growing after purchases of private companies.

**Concentration.** Phalippou argues that since 2006 investors have earned about what a stock index fund would have paid while managers collected hundreds of billions of dollars in carried interest ([Phalippou, 2020](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3623820)). The publisher's description of a book by Eileen Appelbaum and Rosemary Batt says private equity's strategies "disproportionately benefit the partners… at the expense of other stakeholders and taxpayers," while noting positive effects on the operations and growth of some small and midsize companies ([Cornell University](https://catherwood.library.cornell.edu/book_of_the_month/private-equity-at-work-when-wall-street-manages-main-street)).

**Taxes.** Senators Ron Wyden, Sheldon Whitehouse and Angus King, who introduced a 2026 bill to tax carried interest as ordinary income, call its treatment a loophole ([Senate Finance Committee](https://www.finance.senate.gov/ranking-members-news/wyden-whitehouse-king-lead-introduction-of-bill-closing-carried-interest-tax-loophole)). The industry's trade group says it "is not a fee or a loophole" but profit from putting money at risk and investing for the long term ([American Investment Council](https://www.investmentcouncil.org/faqs/)).

**What's unsettled.** Whether funds started since 2006 beat the stock market depends on which index they're compared with. Whether pensions come out ahead after fees, risk and years of waiting is debated, and the latest year was weak. The returns on funds from 2019 to 2022 won't be known until their companies are sold. Opening private equity to 401(k)s could spread its gains to ordinary savers, or its fees and risks; the rules aren't final. And the small purchases of clinics, dental offices and contractors that this series has followed are the least studied part of private equity.
