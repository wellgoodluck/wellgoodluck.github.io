## Short version

A credit score is a three-digit number, usually between 300 and 850, that estimates how likely you are to pay back what you borrow. Lenders use it to decide whether to lend to you and at what interest rate, and landlords, insurers and some employers check your credit too. Most of the score comes from two things: paying on time and not using too much of your available credit. [You can check your credit reports for free every week](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/).

## The trap in one sentence

Nobody tells you you're being graded until you need the grade.

## How it works

**Reports and scores are different things.** Three companies, Equifax, Experian and TransUnion, keep credit reports: records of your loans, cards, balances and payment history. A credit score is a number calculated from a report. Because each company's report can be a little different, and because there are several scoring formulas, you don't have just one score.

**What doesn't go in:** your income, your job, your savings balance or how much is in checking. Lenders may look at those separately, but they aren't part of the score.

**Common myths, corrected:**

- *Checking your own score hurts it.* No. Checking your own credit is a "soft" inquiry and has no effect. Applying for new credit is a "hard" inquiry, which usually causes a small, temporary dip.
- *Carrying a balance builds credit.* No. Paying your card in full every month builds credit just as well and costs nothing (see [how card interest works](credit-card-minimum-payments.html)).
- *Closing old cards helps.* Often the opposite. Closing a card can raise the share of your credit you're using and shorten your history.

**How long mistakes last:** [late payments typically stay on your report for seven years](https://www.stlouisfed.org/open-vault/2026/sep/truth-about-common-credit-score-myths); bankruptcies for up to ten.

## A worked example

Two people buy the same $30,000 car with a five-year loan. One has a strong score and gets a 6.5% rate. The other has a weak score and gets 13.5%. (These rates are illustrative; actual rates vary by lender and market.)

## How to build and protect it

**Pay every bill on time.** It's the biggest single factor. Autopay for at least the minimum makes it automatic.

**Use a small share of your limit.** If your card limit is $2,000, a $1,800 balance means you're using 90% of your credit, which hurts your score even if you pay it off later. A common guideline is to keep it under 30%, and lower is better.

**Pull your free reports.** [AnnualCreditReport.com is the official free site](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/free-credit-reports) for all three companies' reports, now available weekly. Check for accounts you don't recognize and errors in your payment history. If you find a mistake, you have the right to dispute it with the credit bureau.

**Freeze your credit when you're not using it.** A credit freeze, [free by federal law at all three bureaus](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-know-about-credit-freezes-fraud-alerts), stops anyone from opening new accounts in your name. You can lift it temporarily when you apply for something.

**Starting from nothing?** With no credit history, you may not have a score at all. Common first steps are a secured card (you put down a deposit that becomes your limit), being added as an authorized user on a parent's well-managed card, or a small credit-builder loan from a credit union.

**Be careful with "credit repair" offers.** No one can legally remove accurate negative information from your report. Anything a credit repair company can do, you can do yourself for free.

## The bigger picture

What belongs in a credit report is a live policy question.

**Medical debt** is the clearest example. In early 2025, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finalized a rule removing most medical debt from credit reports. [A federal court vacated it later in 2025](https://abc7news.com/post/judge-nixes-biden-rule-order-keep-medical-debt-credit-reports/17132507/), ruling that the agency had exceeded its authority. The three credit bureaus still follow [voluntary policies they adopted in 2022 and 2023](https://investor.equifax.com/news-events/press-releases/detail/1222/equifax-experian-and-transunion-support-u-s-consumers): medical collections under $500 aren't reported, paid medical collections are removed, and unpaid medical debt doesn't appear until it's at least a year old. Some states have passed their own limits.

**New kinds of data** are another. Newer scoring models can include rent payments and buy now, pay later loans (see [buy now, pay later](buy-now-pay-later.html)), and mortgage lenders backed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac [can now use VantageScore 4.0 as well as FICO](https://sf.freddiemac.com/general/credit-score-models).

**Supporters of broader reporting** argue that more data, like on-time rent, helps people with thin credit files qualify for better rates. The credit-industry groups that challenged the medical debt rule argued that unpaid medical bills help lenders judge risk, and that removing them could raise costs or reduce credit for some borrowers.

**Critics** argue that some data, like medical debt, says little about whether someone will repay a loan and penalizes people for getting sick, and that more data means more chances for errors that consumers have to fix themselves.
