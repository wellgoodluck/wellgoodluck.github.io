## In one sentence

A health insurance premium pays for the care a pool of people uses plus what the insurer keeps to run the pool, and because the consumer price index counts only the insurer's part as "health insurance," its index can fall in a year when premiums rise.

## The analogy

A group dinner where the check is split evenly. Your share depends on three things: the prices on the menu, how much the table orders, and the service charge the restaurant adds. The consumer price index tracks the menu prices, dish by dish, each on its own line. Under "health insurance" it records only the service charge, measured against the food and adjusted for the menu prices. Your premium is your share of the whole check. When the table orders more, your share goes up, but the CPI counts that as more food, not a higher price. And when the restaurant's cut shrinks against a bigger food bill, the insurance line falls while the check still goes up.

Most figures below are as of late October 2026; the chart and the premiums line on it stay current.

## What coverage costs

**Through work.** KFF's annual survey of employers with ten or more workers put the average premium in 2026 at [$9,804 a year for one person and $28,303 for a family](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2026-employer-health-benefits-survey/), both 5% more than in 2025. Hourly pay rose 3.7% from April 2025 to April 2026, the middle of the survey's January-to-July fieldwork ([AHETPI](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/AHETPI)). Workers paid $1,672 of the single premium and $7,471 of the family premium on average, about 18% and 27%; employers paid the rest. At smaller firms, 10 to 199 workers, the worker's contribution for family coverage averaged $9,687 (37% of the premium), against $6,840 (25%) at larger ones. Seven in ten covered workers are in plans their employer pays for itself, settling claims directly instead of buying a policy; for them KFF reports a premium equivalent, the plan's cost per enrolled worker. The federal government's own survey of private employers, [AHRQ's MEPS-IC](https://datatools.ahrq.gov/meps-ic/meps-ic-whats-new/), samples employers of every size and publishes in the summer after the year it covers: [$26,281 for a family in 2025](https://www.kff.org/private-insurance/state-indicator/family-coverage/), $7,314 of it paid by the worker.

Over the long run premiums have outrun pay, though by far less than they used to:

Premiums are the five-year changes KFF reported in its [2016](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2016-employer-health-benefits-survey/), [2021](https://files.kff.org/attachment/Summary-of-Findings-Employer-Health-Benefits-2021.pdf) and [2026](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2026-employer-health-benefits-survey/) surveys. KFF has surveyed firms with ten or more workers since 2025, three or more before; it says the change moved the 2024 family premium by $13, and the earlier reports weren't revised.

**On the marketplace.** [About 19 million people](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/9-things-to-watch-for-the-2027-aca-open-enrollment-period/) buy their own coverage through HealthCare.gov and the state exchanges. A federal tax credit caps what a buyer pays for the benchmark plan, the second-cheapest silver plan in the area, as a share of income, and pays the rest. In 2021 Congress made the credit larger: [little or nothing to pay below 150% of the poverty line, no more than 8.5% of income for anyone, and no income ceiling](https://www.commonwealthfund.org/publications/explainer/2025/feb/enhanced-premium-tax-credits-aca-health-plans). Those additions expired at the end of 2025. What enrollees paid out of pocket rose [58% on average in 2026](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/press-release/in-preliminary-rate-filings-aca-marketplace-insurers-largely-propose-double-digit-premium-increase-for-2027-following-a-steep-climb-this-year/), and people with incomes above four times the poverty line (about $62,600 for a single person in most states) lost the credit altogether. Benchmark premiums before the credit rose [26% for 2026 and, in the final rates, 13% for 2027](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/9-things-to-watch-for-the-2027-aca-open-enrollment-period/). [Enrollment that people paid for was 19.2 million in February 2026](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/9-things-to-watch-for-the-2027-aca-open-enrollment-period/), down 12% from a year earlier, and CBO projects average enrollment of [13.1 million in 2027](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/62539). Open enrollment on HealthCare.gov runs [from November 1 to January 15](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/plan-year-2027-marketplace-plans-prices-fact-sheet-0). {{due 2027-02-28 "CMS's 2027 open enrollment report: replace the enrollment sentence"}}

## What the CPI counts as health insurance

The Bureau of Labor Statistics doesn't price insurance policies for the CPI, because it can't hold a policy's quality steady from year to year. [Instead it splits each premium in two](https://www.bls.gov/cpi/factsheets/medical-care.htm). The part insurers pay out in claims is counted where the care happens: in the indexes for doctors, hospitals, prescriptions and the rest, at the full price, including what the insurer paid. The part insurers keep, for administration and profit, is what the CPI calls health insurance. BLS measures it as the ratio of what insurers keep to what they pay out, from their financial reports, smoothed over two years, about ten months old and updated each April and October, then adjusts it for the month's changes in medical prices. It carried 0.89% of the CPI's weight as of December 2025, and that weight counts only premiums households pay themselves, Medicare premiums included; the employer's share is outside the CPI altogether.

Medical prices nudge the index up most months; what swings it is the ratio. It rises when insurers keep more of each premium dollar and falls when claims grow faster than premiums. [In the year to October 2023 it fell 34%](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SEME). In the year to September 2026 it fell 9.0%. Over the same twelve months, premiums insurers charged for the same policies rose 7.0%, as the [Producer Price Index for health insurance carriers](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCU524114524114) measures them. The PPI prices [the whole premium](https://www.bls.gov/ppi/factsheets/producer-price-index-for-the-direct-health-and-medical-insurace-carriers-industry-naics-524114.htm), employer's share included (plus the insurer's return on investing it), holding each policy's coverage and the group it covers constant. It covers policies insurers sell, not plans employers pay for themselves, and it is the measure the [inflation index the Fed targets](https://www.richmondfed.org/research/national_economy/macro_minute/2024/health_insurance_costs_inflation_20241203) uses for insurance.

The CPI's index ([CUUR0000SEME](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SEME)) is from BLS; it isn't on FRED, so the almanac can't chart it. The PPI ([PCU524114524114](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCU524114524114)) and all-items CPI ([CPIAUCSL](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CPIAUCSL)) are from FRED.

Neither index is wrong, and the CPI's isn't always the lower one: from December 2005 to December 2022 it rose 89% and the PPI 64%, [BLS economist Brett Matsumoto found](https://www.bls.gov/opub/mlr/2024/article/measuring-total-premium-inflation-for-health-insurance-in-the-cpi.htm). They measure different things, and only one of them is the price of a policy. Nor is the CPI missing the cost of care: hospital and doctor prices are in it at the full reimbursed price. What it leaves out by design is quantity. A premium rises when the pool uses more care or more expensive treatments, and an index that prices a fixed basket doesn't count that.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**Your paycheck: twice.** The worker's share comes out of each check, $7,471 a year on average for a family plan. The employer's share, about $20,800, never shows up on the stub, but it is part of what the job pays: for a full-time year of 2,080 hours, the family premium works out to about $13.60 an hour, against average pay of [$32.60 an hour](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/AHETPI) for production and nonsupervisory workers in September. Research finds much of the employer's share comes out of wages over time. In one widely cited study, Katherine Baicker and Amitabh Chandra estimated that [a 10% rise in premiums lowered the wages of covered workers by 2.3%](https://www.nber.org/papers/w11160), and also cut employment and hours. [Hourly pay](what-is-hourly-pay.html) never includes it.

**At the doctor.** Premiums are the price of the policy; deductibles and copays are the price of using it. Among covered workers with a deductible, the average for single coverage was $1,955 in 2026, up 18% in five years. When costs are projected to rise, employers have more tools than the premium: in 2026, among firms with 200 or more workers that offer benefits, 44% raised the share workers pay for their largest plan and 21% raised deductibles or other cost sharing ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/health-costs/2026-employer-health-benefits-survey/)).

**Buying your own.** On the marketplace the credit moves with the benchmark premium, so most enrollees with a credit are shielded from sticker increases and feel instead the changes in the credit itself, as in 2026. Those with no credit pay every increase. [You're looking for work](you-are-looking-for-work.html) covers COBRA and the marketplace after a layoff.

## Where the analogy breaks

The restaurant doesn't set the menu here. Insurers negotiate prices with hospitals and doctors and decide what is covered, so part of the service charge buys bargaining, and an insurer that bargains harder can keep more while the check grows less.

The table is enormous and mostly strangers. Your share depends on everyone in the pool, and when healthier people leave, everyone's share rises: marketplace insurers estimate that the enrollees who left after the credits expired added [about four percentage points to 2026 premiums and will add about four more in 2027](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/press-release/in-preliminary-rate-filings-aca-marketplace-insurers-largely-propose-double-digit-premium-increase-for-2027-following-a-steep-climb-this-year/).

And the check is set before the meal. Premiums are priced on expected costs for the coming year; the CPI's index is computed from what happened, months later. A surprise jump in claims lowers the CPI's index just as insurers are raising next year's premiums to cover it.

## What people argue about

**The extra marketplace credits.** One side: the additions kept coverage affordable for people the original credits left out, and ending them has shrunk and weakened the market. CBO estimated in September 2025 that making them permanent would [lower benchmark premiums 7.6% and leave 3.8 million more people insured in 2035](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61734), because healthier people would stay in the pool; after they expired, what enrollees paid rose 58% on average, deductibles rose by [about $1,000 per person](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/press-release/in-preliminary-rate-filings-aca-marketplace-insurers-largely-propose-double-digit-premium-increase-for-2027-following-a-steep-climb-this-year/) as people moved to cheaper plans, and New Mexico, which replaced the lost credits with state money, saw its 2026 sign-ups grow while the nation's fell ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/9-things-to-watch-for-the-2027-aca-open-enrollment-period/)). The other: the additions were pandemic measures written to expire, and making them permanent would cost [$350 billion over ten years](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61734) for 3.8 million more people insured by the end of it. Rep. Jodey Arrington, chair of the House Budget Committee, [argued in September 2025](https://budget.house.gov/news/press-releases/chairman-arrington-in-washington-examiner-covid-is-over-democrat-health-care-handouts-should-be-too) that removing the income limit let households earning far above the old ceiling collect credits, that zero-premium plans invited misreported incomes and enrollments people didn't know about, and that the original credits still cover most of the premium for low- and middle-income families. The Paragon Health Institute estimated improper sign-ups at [four to five million people in 2024, costing $15 to $20 billion](https://paragoninstitute.org/private-health/the-great-obamacare-enrollment-fraud/); in September 2026 HHS cancelled about 760,000 enrollments it called "phantom," people brokers may have signed up without their knowledge ([KFF](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/9-things-to-watch-for-the-2027-aca-open-enrollment-period/)). The credits are paid to insurers on enrollees' behalf, and CMS describes the administration's goal as sending money [to people rather than to insurance companies](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/plan-year-2027-marketplace-plans-prices-fact-sheet-0); it says more than three in four HealthCare.gov buyers will be able to find a plan for $50 a month or less in 2027. Both sides' figures are contested: critics say many enrollees never used their coverage, which Arrington put at 40% with no claims at all, so a lower enrollment count overstates the loss; supporters point to reports that some of the cancelled enrollees were legitimate and have had coverage reinstated, and note that Paragon's estimate rests on comparing reported incomes with survey counts of who could qualify.

**What's pushing premiums up.** Employers and researchers who study prices point to hospitals: RAND found that employers and private insurers paid hospitals [254% of what Medicare would have paid in 2022](https://www.rand.org/pubs/research_reports/RRA1144-2-v2.html), and that hospital market power explained more of the variation in prices than the share of patients on Medicare or Medicaid. The American Hospital Association answered that Medicare underpays, so benchmarking against it is [an apples-to-oranges comparison](https://www.aha.org/press-releases/2024-05-13-aha-statement-rand-50-hospital-pricing-study) that ignores insurers' role in costs, and that hospitals' own costs are rising faster than their prices: it reported [expenses up 7.5% in 2025, more than twice hospital prices](https://www.aha.org/press-releases/2026-03-11-new-aha-report-hospitals-face-increased-challenges-and-financial-pressures-they-care-patients), with more patients and sicker patients accounting for about 36% and 19% of expense growth from 2019 to 2024, and $43 billion spent in 2025 collecting from insurers. Marketplace insurers' 2027 rate filings name both price and use: the underlying cost of care rising [10%, above the 8% of recent years](https://www.healthsystemtracker.org/brief/how-much-and-why-aca-marketplace-premiums-are-going-up-in-2027), with providers seeking double-digit payment increases in some markets, GLP-1 drugs, more outpatient surgery and behavioral health visits, and bills coded for more complex care. Insurers' own cut is capped for the policies they sell: at least 80% of premiums must go to care and quality improvement for individuals and small employers, 85% for large employers, [or the difference is refunded](https://www.cms.gov/cciio/resources/fact-sheets-and-faqs/mlrfinalrule). The rule applies to [insurers selling coverage](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/158.102), so plans employers pay for themselves [aren't covered by it](https://www.haynesboone.com/news/blogs/guidance-clarifies-that-self-funded-group-health-plans-are-not-subject-to-medical-loss-ratio-rules).
