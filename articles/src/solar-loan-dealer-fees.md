## Short version

Many families who buy rooftop solar borrow through a loan the installer arranges, often at a low advertised rate. The low rate usually comes with a fee the lender charges the installer, and the installer raises the price to cover it. The federal consumer bureau found these "dealer fees" can raise the amount borrowed by 30% or more above the cash price, and that they often don't show up in the loan's annual percentage rate. Berkeley Lab's latest price survey found loan-financed home systems sold for a median $4.50 a watt in 2025, against $3.00 for systems bought with cash. In a made-up example, a family borrowing $30,030 at 1.99% for a system with a $23,100 cash price is really paying about 4.4% a year, and still owes more than the cash price five years later. Leases and power purchase agreements carry their own fine print: payments that often rise every year, and a public filing that has to be dealt with before a sale can close. And when the company that sold the system goes bankrupt, as several of the largest have since 2024, the payments are still due.

## The trap in one sentence

A low advertised rate on a solar loan can hide a markup built into the price, so you borrow more than the system costs, pay for it whether or not you keep the loan to the end, and can't see it unless you ask what the system would cost in cash.

## How it works

**The dealer fee.** When an installer offers its own financing, the loan usually comes from a lender it partners with, often a financial technology company, and is signed during the sales visit, often at the customer's home ([CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_solar-financing-issue-spotlight_2024-08.pdf)). To offer a low rate, the lender charges the installer a fee, called a dealer fee, platform fee or program fee, and the installer raises the price of the system to cover it. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, announcing a 2024 report on solar lending, said these fees "often increase the loan cost by 30% or more above the cash price," and that lenders frequently fold them into the amount borrowed without counting them in the annual percentage rate ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-finds-lenders-cramming-markup-fees-and-confusing-terms-into-solar-energy-loans/)). The report itself put the typical range at 10% to 30% of the cash price, and said some fees exceed 50% ([CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_solar-financing-issue-spotlight_2024-08.pdf)). The CFPB's consumer advisory says the fees are rarely shown to the customer ([CFPB consumer advisory](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/consumer-advisory-steer-clear-of-costly-and-complex-loans-for-solar-energy-installation/)).

**What the prices show.** Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory collects the prices paid for home systems each year from state agencies, utilities and other organizations, 72 of them in 31 states in its latest update ([Berkeley Lab](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2026-08/distributed_solar_storage-2026_data_update_final.pdf)). For systems installed in 2025, it found loan-financed home systems "considerably higher priced" than cash purchases, "potentially due in part to loan origination fees rolled into the up-front price" ([Berkeley Lab](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2026-08/distributed_solar_storage-2026_data_update_final.pdf)). Among systems where the financing type is known, the medians were about $3.00 a watt for cash purchases and $4.50 for loan-financed systems, in 2025 dollars ([Berkeley Lab, via Mercom](https://mercomindia.com/us-residential-solar-storage-adoption-jumps-to-37-in-2025)). The typical system installed that year was 7.7 kilowatts, and median prices among the 100 largest installers ran from $2.40 to $6.30 a watt ([Berkeley Lab](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2026-08/distributed_solar_storage-2026_data_update_final.pdf)). {{due 2027-08-31 "Berkeley Lab's 2027 distributed solar update: replace the 2025 medians and the worked example's $3.00 a watt"}}

**The payment that jumps.** Until the end of 2025, buyers could claim a federal tax credit of 30% of the system's cost. Many solar loans were built around it: the borrower was expected to pay a lump sum equal to the credit, often within about 18 months, and if they didn't, the monthly payment rose ([CFPB](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_solar-financing-issue-spotlight_2024-08.pdf); [Goodwin](https://goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/blogs/2024/08/cfpb-report-spotlights-consumer-risks-with-private-solarspecific-loans)). The CFPB listed those "ballooning monthly payments" among its four main concerns ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-finds-lenders-cramming-markup-fees-and-confusing-terms-into-solar-energy-loans/)). The credit ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025 ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/IN12611.html)), but many loans signed in 2024 and 2025 still carry that structure.

**Leases and power purchase agreements.** Instead of buying, a family can let a solar company own the panels on its roof. Under a lease, the family pays a monthly amount; under a power purchase agreement, it pays for each kilowatt-hour the panels make. Many include an annual increase, called an escalator, typically from 0% to about 2.9% a year ([EnergySage](https://www.energysage.com/solar/solar-leases/sunrun-solar-lease-ppa-solar-contract-agreement/)). At 2.9%, a payment roughly doubles over 25 years. The company usually records its ownership of the panels in the public property records with what's called a UCC-1 fixture filing, which a buyer's lender will want handled before closing: the buyer takes over the contract, or the seller buys it out ([Florida Realtors](https://www.floridarealtors.org/news-media/news-articles/2026/10/real-estate-qa-can-leased-solar-panels-derail-home-sale); [Solar United Neighbors](https://solarunitedneighbors.org/gd-selling-your-solar-home)). The homeowner tax credit that ended in 2025 never applied to leased systems. A separate business credit, under section 48E of the tax code, still covers company-owned systems that are running by the end of 2027, or that the company began building by July 4, 2026, subject to limits on equipment from certain foreign suppliers ([Solar Energy Industries Association](https://seia.org/research-resources/clean-energy-provisions-big-beautiful-bill/)).

**When the company goes under.** A household's loan or lease payments are the cash these companies borrow against and sell to investors ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/sunnova-mosaic-bankruptcies-industry-challenges)), so they go on after the company that sold the system fails. SunPower filed for bankruptcy in August 2024 and Sunnova in June 2025 ([Bloomberg, via Tiger Brokers](https://www-web.itiger.com/news/1165708419)). Mosaic, a solar lender, filed that June too ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/sunnova-mosaic-bankruptcies-industry-challenges)). Freedom Forever, an installer that said it had completed more than 150,000 installations ([Freedom Forever, via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/freedom-forever-ranks-no-3669-on-the-2025-inc-5000-list-of-americas-fastest-growing-private-companies-302528050.html)), filed in April 2026 ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/freedom-forever-files-bankruptcy-as-solar-industry-woes-deepen); [Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/06/what-homeowners-should-do-after-freedom-forever-filed-for-bankruptcy/)); in August the court converted its case to a Chapter 7 liquidation ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/freedom-forever-to-liquidate-after-insider-sale-effort-collapse); [pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/09/freedom-forever-bankruptcy-converted-to-chapter-7-liquidation-key-dates-set/)). After earlier failures, Bloomberg Law found thousands of homeowners left without repairs or support, many still paying on leases ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/solar-panel-bankruptcies-leave-frustrated-homeowners-in-limbo)). After Sunnova's, a Boston station reported customers left with systems unrepaired for months while their lease payments stayed due ([WCVB, via Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/06/what-homeowners-should-do-after-freedom-forever-filed-for-bankruptcy/)). The panel and inverter makers' warranties usually survive; the installer's own warranty on its labor may not ([Aurora Solar](https://aurorasolar.com/home-solar/blog/solar-101/your-solar-company-went-out-of-business-heres-what-to-do-next); [EnergySage](https://energysage.com/solar/solar-company-warranty-bankruptcy)).

## A worked example

A made-up family, a made-up system and made-up loan offers; only the price per watt comes from real data.

The system's cash price is $23,100, which is Berkeley Lab's 2025 median of $3.00 a watt on a 7.7-kilowatt system. The installer offers a 25-year loan at 1.99%. To get that rate, it adds a dealer fee equal to 30% of the cash price, so the family borrows $30,030.

Three things to notice. The real rate on the installer's loan, measured against what the system would have cost in cash, is about 4.4%, not 1.99%. After five years of payments the family still owes more than the system's cash price. And whether the installer's loan comes out ahead depends on how long they keep it: paid off at a sale after five years, it costs about $1,940 more than the credit union loan; kept for ten, it costs about $2,700 less. The crossover comes at about seven years. The family can't make that comparison without knowing the cash price, and the cash price usually isn't on the loan papers.

The method: standard monthly loan payments; the balance after each year; the rate against the cash price is the rate at which $23,100 borrowed for 25 years would carry the same $127 payment. With a 50% markup, about the size of the gap in Berkeley Lab's medians, which the lab says may be only partly due to fees, the same math gives about 5.8%. Lenders often state the fee as a share of the amount financed instead ([Michigan Attorney General](https://www.michigan.gov/ag/news/press-releases/2026/07/15/ag-nessel-files-lawsuit-against-solar-company-financial-institutions-for-alleged-deceptive-scheme)); by that measure, a 30% fee on this system would mean borrowing $33,000, and a rate of about 5.3% against the cash price.

## How to avoid it

- **Ask for the cash price, in writing, before talking about financing.** It's the only number that shows the fee. In California, contractors must give a Solar Energy System Disclosure Document showing the total cost of the system, including financing ([Contractors State License Board](https://web.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Solar_Smart/)).
- **Compare the installer's loan with one you arrange yourself.** A loan you arrange directly with a credit union or bank, or a home equity line, is usually based on the cash price, with no dealer fee. Compare payments, and what you'd owe if you sold the house in five or seven years.
- **For a lease or power purchase agreement, find the escalator and the buyout terms.** Work out what the payment will be in year 10 and year 20, and what it would cost to buy the system out or transfer it if you sell.
- **Ask who services the system if the company that sold it closes**, and which warranties come from the equipment makers rather than the installer.
- **Use the cooling-off period.** The Federal Trade Commission's Cooling-Off Rule gives three business days to cancel most sales of $25 or more made at your home ([FTC](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buyers-remorse-ftcs-cooling-rule-may-help)). Some states give more: Texas gives buyers and lessees of most home solar systems five business days, for contracts signed since September 2025 ([Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation](https://www.tdlr.texas.gov/residential-solar-retailers/effect.htm)), and California gives buyers 65 and older five business days ([California Public Utilities Commission](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/solarguide)).

How the panels will be credited for the power they send to the grid is a separate question, and often a bigger one: [Who Pays When Your Neighbor Goes Solar?](solar-net-metering.html) explains it, and [Interest on Interest](compound-interest.html) shows how a balance grows over a long loan.

## The bigger picture

The CFPB's 2024 report identified four risks in solar lending: hidden markups, misleading claims about what borrowers would pay, monthly payments that balloon, and exaggerated savings claims ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-finds-lenders-cramming-markup-fees-and-confusing-terms-into-solar-energy-loans/)). In May 2025, under new leadership, the bureau withdrew 67 guidance documents, saying it would issue guidance only where it reduced the burden on companies and that guidance going beyond the law was unlawful ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-05-12/html/2025-08286.htm)). The solar report and advisory weren't among the 67 ([Federal Register](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2025-05-12/html/2025-08286.htm)). In May 2026 the bureau removed all its press releases and consumer advisories from before February 2025, including the solar advisory and the report's announcement ([Bloomberg Government](https://news.bgov.com/banking-law/cfpb-deletes-all-public-statements-issued-before-trump-takeover)); both now appear in an archive section of its website, and the report itself is still posted ([CFPB archive](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/); [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/issue-spotlight-solar-financing/)). Lawyers on both sides note that states and private plaintiffs can still enforce the laws, which the withdrawal didn't change ([The Regulatory Review](https://www.theregreview.org/2025/05/17/frotman-lipton-what-does-the-cfpbs-mass-guidance-withdrawal-mean-not-much/); [Morgan Lewis](https://www.morganlewis.com/pubs/2025/06/cfpb-revokes-guidance-in-sweeping-rollback-of-agency-policies-and-priorities)).

States have their own rules, and some have gone to court. California requires the disclosure document on the cover of every residential solar contract ([Contractors State License Board](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Consumers/Solar_Requirements.aspx)). Minnesota's attorney general sued four solar lenders over dealer fees in 2024 ([Minnesota Attorney General](https://www.ag.state.mn.us/Office/Communications/2024/03/08_SolarLending.asp)), New York's sued an installer and two lenders in March 2026 ([New York Attorney General](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-sues-home-solar-power-company-and-lenders-cheating-new)), and Michigan's sued an installer and its lenders in July 2026 ([Michigan Attorney General](https://www.michigan.gov/ag/news/press-releases/2026/07/15/ag-nessel-files-lawsuit-against-solar-company-financial-institutions-for-alleged-deceptive-scheme)). In April 2026 Texas's attorney general opened an investigation into claims that several installers, including Sunrun and Freedom Forever, misled customers about bill savings and contract terms, and his office has since sued one of them, CAM Solar ([Texas Attorney General](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-launches-major-initiative-combat-widespread-fraud-companies-selling); [E&E News](https://www.eenews.net/articles/texas-attorney-general-targets-rooftop-solar/); [Texas Attorney General, lawsuit](https://www.texasattorneygeneral.gov/news/releases/attorney-general-ken-paxton-sues-san-antonio-based-solar-company-fraudulent-sales-solar-panel); [CBS News Texas](https://www.cbsnews.com/texas/news/with-months-left-in-office-paxton-files-first-suit-against-solar-company/)).

Lenders see dealer fees differently. GoodLeap has said the fee is how an installer buys a lower rate and monthly payment for its customer, a practice long used in auto lending, and Mosaic said the Minnesota complaint mischaracterized dealer fees and how they were disclosed ([Star Tribune](https://www.startribune.com/minnesota-homeowners-say-they-were-hit-with-massive-hidden-fees-when-going-solar/601177876)). Lenders point to federal lending rules that leave fees a lender charges the seller, called seller's points, out of the finance charge and so out of the annual percentage rate ([Mayer Brown](https://www.mayerbrown.com/en/insights/publications/2025/01/regulatory-clouds-on-the-horizon-for-solar-financing-programs-face-headwinds-but-the-future-still-looks-bright)); Michigan's attorney general argues the fees should have been disclosed as finance charges ([Michigan Attorney General](https://www.michigan.gov/ag/news/press-releases/2026/07/15/ag-nessel-files-lawsuit-against-solar-company-financial-institutions-for-alleged-deceptive-scheme)). The CFPB's position is that a consumer comparing loans by their annual percentage rate can't see the cost ([CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/newsroom/cfpb-report-finds-lenders-cramming-markup-fees-and-confusing-terms-into-solar-energy-loans/)).

For who enforces lending rules now, see [Who Referees the Marketplace?](who-referees-the-marketplace.html).
