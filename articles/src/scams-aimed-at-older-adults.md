## Short version

Scams aimed at older adults rarely look like a too-good-to-be-true offer. They look like a warning: your computer is infected, your bank account has been hacked, your grandson is in jail, your Social Security number was used in a crime. The caller sounds official, says it's urgent, tells you to keep it secret, and asks you to move money into something that can't be reversed: a wire, a cryptocurrency machine, gift cards, or cash or gold handed to a courier at your door. In 2025, people 60 and older told the FBI they lost [$7.7 billion](https://www.aarp.org/money/scams-fraud/fbi-ftc-report-2025-losses) to internet-enabled fraud and crime, up about 60% from the year before, and that's only what was reported. The defenses are simple, and they work best if they're agreed on before the call comes: no real bank, agency or tech company ever asks you to move money to keep it safe; hang up and call back on a number you look up yourself; and if money has already moved, the FBI says reporting it within hours, not days, gives the best chance of freezing it.

## The trap in one sentence

The scam tells you your money is in danger and has you "protect" it by sending it away, so the more careful and responsible you are, the more convincing it feels.

## How it works

**The scripts.** Most of the losses come from a few stories, retold with new details:

- **The "phantom hacker."** A pop-up or text says your computer or account has been compromised and gives a number to call. The "tech support" agent hands you to your bank's "fraud department," who hands you to a "federal agent," each confirming the last. The FBI [warned of this three-step scam in 2023](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2023/PSA230929), and in 2024 [warned that it increasingly ends with a courier at the door](https://www.ic3.gov/PSA/2024/PSA240129).
- **The government impersonator.** Someone from "Social Security," "Medicare," "the IRS" or a sheriff's office says there's a warrant, a frozen benefit or a crime tied to your name, and that paying or moving money makes it go away. In 2025, [government impersonation complaints from people 60 and older nearly doubled](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf), from about 4,500 to about 8,600.
- **The grandchild in trouble.** A frightened voice says he's been in an accident or arrested and begs you not to tell his parents; a "lawyer" or "bail bondsman" takes over. Cloned voices made from a few seconds of video posted online make the call harder to doubt.
- **The friend who knows investing.** A warm stranger met online, often over weeks or months, introduces a trading platform where the account appears to grow, until it's time to withdraw. Investment fraud was the largest single category of older adults' losses, [about $3.5 billion](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) reported to the FBI in 2025.
- **The rescuer.** After a loss, someone offers to get the money back for a fee. Older victims reported [$540 million](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fbi-seniors-cybercrime-2025/) in losses to these recovery scams in 2025, a figure the FBI says may include money lost to the earlier scam that led them there.

**The payment is the point.** A scammer's script always ends with a way to pay that can't be undone. In the FTC's data for 2024, [a third of older adults who lost $10,000 or more to an impersonator paid in cryptocurrency](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/seniors-face-surge-in-high-dollar-impersonation-scams-ftc-warns/261704), followed by bank transfers and cash. Cryptocurrency kiosks, the "bitcoin ATMs" in convenience stores, were used in scams that cost [more than $389 million in 2025](https://www.aarp.org/advocacy/states-crack-down-crypto-atm-2024), and where the victim's age was known, 76% of those losses were from people 60 and older. When the money is in cash or gold, a courier collects it in person; the FBI's Boston office counted [103 courier pickups in New England from 2023 into 2025](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/fbi-issues-warning-beware-devastating-195401160.html), more than $26 million in all, and 98% of the victims were over 60.

**Why the losses run so large.** Older adults [report losing money to fraud less often than younger adults](https://www.ftc.gov/node/338049), but the oldest lose the most when they do: the FTC found a median reported loss of $2,000 for people 80 and over. A small share of cases does most of the damage. In 2025, reports of losses over $100,000 were [6% of older adults' loss reports but 70% of the dollars](https://gizmodo.com/new-data-show-how-much-worse-scams-are-getting-for-americans-over-60-2000827886). Retirement savings and home equity give a scammer more to take, and a scheme that unfolds over weeks can empty several accounts in turn.

**The numbers undercount.** These are only reported losses. The FTC, which receives a different set of reports from the FBI, counted about [$3 billion in losses reported by older adults in 2025](https://www.ftc.gov/node/338049), and estimated that once unreported cases are counted, fraud may have cost older Americans [between $13.5 billion and $119.5 billion](https://gizmodo.com/new-data-show-how-much-worse-scams-are-getting-for-americans-over-60-2000827886), depending on the method. Many victims never report, out of embarrassment or because they don't know where to.

## A worked example

Ruth, 74, a made-up retiree, sees a pop-up on her laptop with a warning and a phone number. Over the next nine days (the amounts are invented, in the range the FBI reports):

{{illustration name=three-calls caption="The three-call script, made up for the example: each caller hands Ruth to the next, and each asks for something the last one didn't."}}

- The "tech support" agent connects to her computer and finds "suspicious activity" in her bank account.
- A "bank fraud investigator" calls. He knows her branch and her balance, because he watched her log in. He tells her the bank's own staff may be involved, so she shouldn't mention it there.
- A "federal agent" sends her a photo of a badge and tells her to wire $55,000 from savings to a "secure government account," then to withdraw $15,000 in cash for a courier, who will give a code word at the door. She also buys $2,500 in gift cards "to verify her identity."

Total: $72,500. Three months later a "recovery firm" calls, says it has traced her money, and asks for a $3,000 fee.

## How to avoid it

**Learn the four requests no real caller makes.** No bank, government agency, tech company or police department will ask you to move money to protect it; to pay in gift cards, cryptocurrency, gold or cash handed to a courier; to keep it secret from your bank or your family; or to stay on the line while you go to the bank. The FBI and FTC say any one of these is a sign of a scam, however real the caller sounds or whatever caller ID shows.

**Hang up and call back.** Look up the number yourself, from the back of your card, a statement or the agency's own website, not from the pop-up, text or voicemail. A real problem will still be there when you call back.

**Set the family rules before the call comes.** Agree, out loud and in advance: "We never move money because of a call, text or pop-up. We hang up and call each other first." For the grandchild call, pick a family code word that's never been posted online. Saying it ahead of time takes away the embarrassment of checking later.

**Name a trusted contact.** Brokerage firms are required to ask customers for a trusted contact person, someone the firm can call if it suspects fraud or can't reach you; the person gets no access to the account. Only [42% of investors had named one](https://altswire.com/finra-proposes-changes-to-combat-fraud-and-protect-senior-investors/) in a 2024 FINRA Foundation survey. Many banks offer something similar. The firm can then contact that person if it suspects fraud.

**Set alerts and limits.** Text alerts on every withdrawal and transfer, and a lower daily limit on wires and transfers, buy time. Some banks will add a second person to approve large transfers.

**If money has already moved, act within hours.**

- Call your bank's fraud line, from the number on your card, and ask it to recall any wire or stop any transfer.
- File a report with the FBI at [IC3.gov](https://www.ic3.gov) and with the FTC at [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov).
- Call the Justice Department's [National Elder Fraud Hotline](https://justice.gov/usao-sdin/elder-fraud-0), 1-833-372-8311, which helps people 60 and older, and their families, make the reports and find local help.
- If gift cards were used, call the number on the back of each card.
- Ignore anyone who offers, for a fee, to recover what was lost. That's the second scam.

**Talk about it without shame.** People who are robbed this way are not careless. They were trying to protect their money from a convincing threat, which is exactly what the script is built to make them do. A family that treats a near miss as a good catch hears about the next one.

## The bigger picture

The arguments are mostly about where to put the speed bumps, and who decides when an adult's own money can be held.

**Crypto kiosks.** Since 2023, [35 states have passed laws on crypto kiosks](https://www.aarp.org/advocacy/states-crack-down-crypto-atm-2024), 18 of them in 2026, most with daily transaction limits, fraud warnings and licensing; some, such as [Arizona's](https://natlawreview.com/article/arizona-cryptocurrency-kiosk-law-takes-effect), require refunds to fraud victims who report quickly. In March 2026, [Indiana became the first state to ban the machines](https://www.aarp.org/advocacy/crypto-atm-fraud-protections/) outright, and some cities have banned them too. Supporters of limits and bans, AARP among them, say the machines have become the last step in a large share of scams against older people and that warnings shown to someone already being coached on the phone don't work. Operators and others in the industry [say](https://www.kioskmarketplace.com/articles/crypto-kiosk-chaos-can-the-bitcoin-atm-industry-survive/) the kiosks give people without bank accounts a legal way to buy cryptocurrency, that blanket bans hurt legitimate users, and that targeted rules and stronger compliance address the problem without a ban.

**Letting firms hold an older customer's money.** Since 2018, FINRA's rules have let a brokerage that reasonably believes a customer 65 or older (or an adult with an impairment) is being exploited place a temporary hold on a disbursement, and [since 2022 on a transaction](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/notices/22-05); with a report to a state agency or court, the hold can now last up to 55 business days. In 2026, FINRA asked the SEC to [extend that to as many as 145 business days](https://www.finra.org/compliance-tools/weekly-archive/08192026) and to let firms place a short hold on any customer's account when they suspect fraud. In June 2026 the House passed the [Financial Exploitation Prevention Act](https://www.psca.org/news/psca-news/2026/6/house-passes-bill-to-delay-suspicious-mutual-fund-redemptions/), 414 to 2, which would give mutual funds a similar power to delay a redemption for customers 65 and older or with an impairment; as of mid-November it had not passed the Senate. Supporters, including [the securities, fund and insurance industry groups that urged the Senate to pass it](https://www.sifma.org/news/press-releases/fsi-ici-iri-sifma-urge-senate-passage-of-financial-exploitation-prevention-act) and the CFP Board, say a delay breaks the urgency every scam depends on and gives family members and investigators time to step in. Critics include PIABA, a bar association of lawyers who represent investors, which [called a 145-day hold far too long](https://piaba.org/comment-letter-finra-26-02-rule-revisions-to-help-member-firms-protect-senior-investors-from-financial-exploitation-and-all-investors-from-fraud/) and an inversion of the principle that customers control their own accounts.

**Who should repay a scam victim.** Most of the money lost to these scripts was sent by the victim, which under federal law generally means no one has to give it back. Whether banks, payment companies and phone and social media companies should share that cost is the same fight described on the [payment apps page](payment-app-scams.html).

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of mid-November 2026.
