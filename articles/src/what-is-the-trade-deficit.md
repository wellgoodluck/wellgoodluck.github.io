## In one sentence

The trade deficit is the value of what Americans bought from other countries minus what they sold to them, in a month; the almanac's figure covers goods and services together and has been negative every month since 1992, the start of the series.

Goods and services behave differently. The United States runs a large deficit in goods (cars, electronics, clothing, oil until recently) and a surplus in services (software, finance, travel, education, royalties). The headline is the sum; both are on the chart as companions.

## The analogy

You run a trade deficit with your grocery store. You buy from it every week and it never buys anything from you, and nobody thinks you are losing. You run a surplus with your employer, who buys your labor and sells you nothing. The deficit with the store and the surplus with the employer are the same money, going around. A bilateral trade balance, the kind that gets quoted about China or Mexico, is the grocery-store number: real, and not a score.

The national balance is the sum of all of those, and it has a mirror image that the analogy makes visible. If you spend more than you earn, the difference comes from somewhere: savings drawn down, or borrowing. The same holds for a country. A trade deficit means foreigners end up holding dollars they didn't spend on American goods, and they put those dollars into American assets instead: Treasury bonds, stocks, factories, houses. The trade deficit and the inflow of foreign money are the same number seen from two sides. That is why the trade deficit widened in the 1980s and 2000s when the dollar was strong and foreigners were eager to invest here, and narrowed in recessions when Americans stopped buying.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**Prices: at the store.** Imports are the cheap end of most of what households buy, and the deficit is, from one side, a measure of how much of that cheapness the country took. Tariffs raise it directly: the 2025 tariffs showed up in goods prices within months and in the deficit within one, as importers rushed shipments in ahead of them (the March 2025 spike on the chart) and then cut back.

**Jobs: by industry, in both directions.** The deficit is concentrated in manufactured goods, and the regions that made those goods lost employment to imports, most sharply in the 2000s after China joined the World Trade Organization. The surplus is in services, and the regions that export those gained. The national job numbers barely see the trade balance; the local ones do.

**Interest rates: through the mirror image.** The foreign money that matches the deficit buys Treasuries, which holds [the 10-year yield](what-is-the-10-year-yield.html) lower than it would otherwise be. A smaller deficit means less of that, which is one reason the deficit and cheap mortgages have tended to come together.

**The dollar.** A strong dollar makes imports cheaper and exports dearer and widens the deficit with a lag of a year or two; the dollar index is on the chart as a companion.

## What moves it

Growth here against growth abroad, first: Americans buy more imports when the economy is strong, and foreigners buy more exports when theirs is. Then the dollar. Then oil, which swung the goods balance for decades until U.S. production made the country a net exporter of energy in 2019. Then tariffs and trade deals, which move particular goods and partners more than the total; the overall deficit has been roughly the same share of the economy under every trade regime since 2000, because the total is set by the saving-and-investment mirror image more than by trade policy.

## How to read this month's number

**Goods or the total.** The headline most outlets report is the goods deficit, which is larger. The almanac shows goods and services together, the figure that matches the mirror-image accounting.

The monthly figure swings $10–20 billion on the timing of shipments, so the three-month average is the signal.

**Watch the import line, not the balance, after a tariff.** Tariffs show up first as a surge of imports before they take effect, then a drop; the balance looks worse, then better, for reasons that have nothing to do with competitiveness.

## Where the analogy breaks

Your grocery store cannot retaliate, and a country's partners can. Your surplus with your employer is a wage; a country's surplus in services is spread over different people in different places from the ones who lost to the goods deficit, which is why the trade deficit is a regional and distributional issue even when it is not a national one. And the mirror image cuts both ways: the foreign money that matches the deficit is a loan or a sale, and a country that runs deficits for fifty years has sold a great deal of itself, which is the strongest form of the argument that it matters.

## What people argue about

**Whether the deficit is a loss.** One side: a deficit means jobs and production that happened abroad instead of here, and fifty years of it hollowed out manufacturing and the towns built on it. The other: the deficit is the mirror of foreign investment in a country people want to invest in, it funds cheaper borrowing and cheaper goods, and the countries with large surpluses (Germany, Japan, China) are not obviously better off for them. The data support both: the regional losses are documented, and so is the aggregate gain.

**Whether tariffs fix it.** The 2018 tariffs and the 2025 ones both reduced imports from the countries they targeted and did little to the total, as imports shifted to other countries and the dollar adjusted. One side reads that as proof tariffs don't work on the balance; the other says the balance was never the point, and the aim was particular industries. [You run a small business](you-run-a-small-business.html) has the version of this argument that lands on a firm.

**Bilateral balances.** The deficit with one country is the grocery-store number, and economists across the spectrum say it means little by itself; politicians across the spectrum cite it anyway, because it is concrete. The almanac shows the total and the two halves and leaves the country-by-country figures to the Census Bureau's tables, linked below.
