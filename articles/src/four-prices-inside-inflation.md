## In one sentence

Each of these is one line inside the consumer price index: the change, from a year earlier, in what the same care, coverage or car costs.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics prices them alongside everything else in the index and weights each by how much households spend on it out of pocket. In the index's December 2025 weights, medical care was 8.4% of the total, used cars and trucks 2.8%, motor vehicle insurance 2.8%, and day care and preschool 0.7%. Together that is about 15 cents of each CPI dollar.

## The analogy

Think of inflation as a band playing one song. The headline number is how loud the whole band is. These four are instruments that come in on their own count. Used cars react first, to anything that interrupts the supply of new ones. Car insurance follows a year or two behind, because insurers raise rates only after their claim costs have already gone up and, in many states, only after regulators approve the filing. Medical prices move slowly, on contracts and payment schedules set once a year. Child care tracks the pay of the people who do it.

A household that isn't buying one of these hears the band. A household that is can hear little else.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**Used cars: first in.** When the chip shortage cut new-car production, used-car prices rose 45.2% from a year earlier in June 2021, the fastest since the series begins in 1953. **Car insurance: the bill that came later.** Pricier cars and parts and costlier repairs raised what insurers paid out, and premiums followed. Car insurance prices rose 22.6% from a year earlier in April 2024, the most since 1976. By the middle of 2026 the catch-up had run its course, and premiums were falling from a year earlier. **Medical care: the biggest of the four, and the slowest.** Doctors, hospitals, drugs, equipment and health insurance, as households pay for them. They fell below zero, to −1.4%, in September 2023, the lowest on record, and that was a quirk of measurement, not cheaper care (see How to read, below).

**Child care: small in the index, large in a budget.** At 0.7% of the index, day care barely moves the headline. For a family paying for it, it can be one of the largest bills of the month. ## What moves them

**Used cars:** new-car supply first, then interest rates on car loans, then the number of cars coming off leases and rental fleets.

**Car insurance:** what claims cost, which follows repair bills, parts, medical costs after crashes and how much cars are worth when they're totaled. Rates change only through filings, so this line lags the others.

**Medical care:** payment rates negotiated between insurers and providers, Medicare's annual fee schedules, drug prices, and how health insurance is measured.

**Child care:** wages, mostly. It is labor heavy, and states set how many children each adult may look after, so a center can't take on more children per worker to cut costs.

## How to read this month's numbers

**Health insurance is measured indirectly.** The index doesn't price a policy. It tracks what insurers keep after paying claims, as a share of what they pay out, from industry data. When that measure fell through 2023, it pulled the whole medical index below zero even though no one's coverage got cheaper. The BLS smoothed the method that October, averaging over two years and updating every six months, to make those swings smaller.

**The index counts what households pay, not employers.** The share of a premium that an employer pays isn't in these weights. Neither is care paid for entirely by government programs.

**Car insurance and child care here are not seasonally adjusted.** The change from a year earlier compares the same month, so the season washes out, but a single month's move can be noisy.

**A change from a year ago is not a level.** A fall of a few percent after rises of more than 20% leaves car insurance well above where it was in 2021. The charts show the yearly change; the level is in the index itself.

## Where the analogy breaks

A band's instruments play the same song. These four don't always: in 2026 used cars and car insurance were both getting cheaper while child care kept rising. And the index weights them by what households spend on average, so it plays them at the average family's volume. A family with no car, no children and employer health coverage hears very little of any of them. A family with a teen driver and a toddler in day care hears them over everything else.

## What people argue about

**Whether the index captures health costs.** One side: it measures prices for the same care over time, which is what an inflation index should do, and households' out-of-pocket spending is the right weight. The other side: most of what a family pays for health care runs through premiums, much of it paid by employers out of what would otherwise be wages, so an index that leaves that out understates what health care costs workers. Both describe the method accurately; they disagree about what the number should be for.

**Whether car insurance has stopped climbing for good.** One side: premiums have caught up with the jump in repair and car prices of 2021–23, and as insurers compete for customers again, rates are coming down. The other side: repair costs, parts prices and the cost of the sensors and cameras in newer cars keep rising, so claims costs will pull premiums up again. The yearly change will show which, a year or two after the claims do.
