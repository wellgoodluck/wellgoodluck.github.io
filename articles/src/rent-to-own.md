## Short version

Rent-to-own lets you take home furniture, appliances or electronics today for a small weekly or monthly payment, with no credit check. Keep paying long enough and you own it. The catch is the total: [the Federal Trade Commission warns](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/public_statements/prepared-statement-federal-trade-commission-rent-own-transactions/110726renttoowntestimony.pdf) that owning an item this way can cost two to three times its regular retail price. The same model now shows up at online checkouts as "lease-to-own."

## The trap in one sentence

The weekly price is on the tag; the total price is something you have to calculate yourself.

## How it works

Rent-to-own is a lease, not a loan. You rent the item week by week or month by month. You can usually return it anytime and stop paying, but you don't get back what you've paid. If you make every payment through the end of the agreement, the item becomes yours.

Because it's legally a rental, it [usually isn't covered by the federal lending law](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RS21467.html) that requires an interest rate to be disclosed. So shoppers rarely see a figure they can compare with a credit card or a loan.

[In an FTC survey from the late 1990s](https://www.ftc.gov/sites/default/files/documents/public_statements/prepared-statement-federal-trade-commission-rent-own-transactions/110726renttoowntestimony.pdf), customers ended up buying about 70% of what they rented; studies using industry data have found lower rates. Stores typically offer an early-purchase option that cuts the total if you pay off sooner, but that price is still well above retail.

## A worked example

[An FTC consumer guide gave this example](https://www.self.inc/blog/rent-to-own-guide): a laptop with a retail price of $612, offered at $38.99 a week for 48 weeks.

## How to avoid it

**Always multiply.** Payment amount times number of payments. Compare that number with the retail price online before you sign anything.

**Look at used and refurbished.** A refurbished laptop or a used couch bought outright is usually far cheaper than renting a new one.

**Save for a few weeks instead.** Putting the weekly payment aside for 16 weeks would buy the $612 laptop outright, with about $12 to spare. Renting it costs 48 weeks.

**If you rent anyway, plan for the early buyout.** Ask exactly what it costs to own the item at 30, 60 and 90 days, and get it in writing.

**Watch for "lease-to-own" at online checkout.** It can look like a payment plan, but it works like rent-to-own. Check the total before you click.

## The bigger picture

The FTC has long recommended that rent-to-own stores [disclose the total cost of ownership while customers are shopping](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/RS21467.html), not just in the contract, so shoppers can compare. Some states have gone further and [limit how much stores can mark up merchandise](https://www.wtxl.com/syndication/renting-to-own-often-means-high-markups-rarely-savings/article_b9484f83-5e44-5956-a20b-31bb9bdfe64c.html); others leave pricing largely to the market.

{{illustration name=rto-price-tag caption="What a total-cost label would look like for the example laptop."}}

**Critics** argue rent-to-own charges the highest prices to the people with the least money and the fewest options, and that weekly pricing is designed to hide the total.

**The industry** argues it serves customers that banks and retailers won't, that the price includes delivery, repairs and the freedom to return items with no debt or credit damage, and that many customers rent short-term rather than buying out. One major chain has said [only a small share of customers pay the full cost to own](https://www.buzzfeednews.com/article/leticiamiranda/how-to-pay-2000-for-a-300-tv-rent-to-own).

Both points hold. The lease structure has real value if you might not keep the item; it's a very expensive way to buy something you plan to keep.
