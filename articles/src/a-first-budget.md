## Short version

A budget is a plan for your take-home pay, written down before the month starts. List the bills that are the same every month, add a monthly share of the ones that come once or twice a year, decide how much goes to savings, and divide what's left among groceries, gas and everything else. Many people find it easier after tracking one month of real spending first. Rules of thumb like 50/30/20 are a place to start, but a first budget with rent in it often won't fit them, and that's normal.

## The idea in one sentence

A budget gives every dollar of take-home pay a job before the month starts, so the month doesn't decide for you.

## How it works

**Start from take-home pay, not your pay rate.** The money you can budget is what actually lands in your account (see [your first paycheck](your-first-paycheck.html)). If your hours or tips vary, plan around a lean month and treat anything extra as a bonus.

**Three kinds of costs.**

- **Fixed:** about the same every month. Rent, utilities, phone, insurance, loan and card minimums, subscriptions.
- **Flexible:** necessary, but the amount is up to you. Groceries, gas, household supplies.
- **Irregular:** the ones that break budgets because they don't come monthly. Car registration and repairs, annual subscriptions, holidays and birthdays, a yearly insurance bill. Add up a year's worth, divide by 12, and set that much aside each month so the money is there when the bill comes.

**Savings as a bill.** Many planners suggest treating savings as a line in the budget, moved automatically on payday, rather than whatever's left at the end of the month. The first goal for most people is [a small cushion](emergency-cushion.html) for surprises.

**The 50/30/20 rule.** The best-known rule of thumb was popularized by [Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi's 2005 book, *All Your Worth*](https://www.acorns.com/learn/saving/50-30-20-budget-rule/): about half of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants, and 20% for savings and paying down debt. Supporters like it because it's simple: three buckets instead of dozens. Critics, including [Ramsey Solutions](https://www.ramseysolutions.com/budgeting/50-20-30-budget-rule), argue that most people's needs take more than half, and that putting savings last invites it to be skipped. Other approaches give every dollar a category (a "zero-based" budget) or put cash for each category in its own envelope. The method matters less than writing a plan down and checking it.

**What households actually spend.** In 2025 the average household [spent $81,905](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cesan_10292026.htm), according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The biggest shares were housing (33.9%), transportation (16.5%), food (13.0%), insurance and pensions, mostly Social Security and retirement contributions (12.4%), health care (7.9%) and entertainment (5.0%). Those are shares of spending, not of take-home pay, but they show where budgets go.

**Housing is usually the line that decides.** A common yardstick is that housing shouldn't take more than 30% of income; above that, researchers call a household "cost-burdened." By that measure, a record [22.7 million renter households, 49%, were cost-burdened in 2024](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/state-nations-housing-2026), Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies found.

## A worked example

You earn $18 an hour full time, about $37,440 a year. In a state with no income tax on wages, after federal income tax, Social Security and Medicare, that's about $2,689 a month take-home.

## What to do with it

**Track one month first.** Your bank and card statements already sort most of it. The surprise is usually small things that add up, and [subscriptions](subscription-creep.html).

**Write the plan before the month starts,** and move savings on payday, before anything else.

**Look at the biggest lines first.** Housing and a car payment decide more than coffee does.

**Leave a small line for things you'll forget.** A budget with no slack breaks in week two.

**Check it monthly and adjust.** A budget that's wrong in its first month is normal; one that's never revisited stops being a budget.

**When pay goes up, decide where the raise goes before it arrives.** Otherwise spending tends to rise to match it ([lifestyle creep](lifestyle-creep.html)).
