## The short version

Plug-in solar, also called balcony solar, is one or a few panels with a small inverter that plugs into a regular wall outlet. The power it makes is used by whatever else is running in the home, so the household buys less from the utility. Ten states have passed laws allowing it, Utah in 2025 and nine more in 2026, and all ten are in effect or will be by March 2027; the table under "Where it's legal" shows where each stands. The laws generally cap a kit at about what a hair dryer draws and require a safety lab's certification. Kits cost from a few hundred dollars to $1,500 or more. An 800-watt kit might save $13 to $20 a month at the national average electricity price, if all its power is used at home, and more where power costs more. The argument is about safety: home wiring was built for outlets that draw power, not ones that supply it, and the certification for these kits only began in January 2026. Supporters point to Germany, where more than 1.5 million are registered. And the laws mostly settle things with the utility, not the landlord: only a few of them limit what a lease or a homeowners' association can say.

## The analogy

A space heater in reverse. It plugs into the same kind of outlet, and at the usual legal limit it handles about the same amount of power, but it pushes electricity into the house instead of drawing it out. Whatever is running at the moment, the refrigerator, a window air conditioner, a laptop, uses the panel's power first and takes less from the grid.

Figures below are as of mid-November 2026.

## How it works

A kit is one to four panels, a microinverter that turns the panels' direct current into household alternating current, and a cord that plugs into an outlet. The panels hang on a balcony railing, lean against a wall or sit in a yard. The inverter is built to shut off within seconds if the grid goes down, so it can't send power onto lines that utility crews are working on ([Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/10/california-is-10th-state-to-legalize-balcony-solar/)). That also means a kit on its own isn't backup power during an outage. A kit with a battery can run appliances plugged straight into the battery ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/what-to-know-balcony-solar)), but its connection to the house wiring still has to cut off, so it can't push power back through the outlet and onto the grid. California's law, for one, requires a certified feature that isolates the kit from the building's wiring in an outage ([Public Utilities Code §8530, added by SB 868](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB868)).

Under the state laws, a system this small is exempt from the interconnection process that rooftop systems go through, the application and approval a utility requires before anything is connected to its grid. California's law, for example, bars utilities from requiring their approval or charging a fee. A utility may require the user to register through a simple online form giving the address and the kit's make, model and size, but registering can't be turned into an approval ([California Public Utilities Code §8531, added by SB 868](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB868)). Some states ask for notice first: Maryland requires the user to notify the utility before plugging in ([Maryland Public Service Commission](https://psc.maryland.gov/regulated-utilities/electricity/renewable-energy/balcony-solar/)), and Virginia gives the utility 15 days to check the notice for completeness, after which the customer may install ([Va. Code §56-596.8](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title56/chapter23/section56-596.8/)).

The power isn't credited if it flows out to the grid. Utah's law, for one, says these devices aren't subject to net metering ([Utah HB 340](https://le.utah.gov/Session/2025/bills/enrolled/HB0340.pdf)), and on some meters that can't track power flowing out, it can even be recorded as power used ([Maryland Public Service Commission](https://psc.maryland.gov/regulated-utilities/electricity/renewable-energy/balcony-solar/)). The savings come from power used in the home as it's made. As one nonprofit seller puts it, "you use it or you lose it" ([Bright Saver](https://www.brightsaver.org/post/plug-in-solar-complete-guide)).

## What it costs and what it saves

Kits range from several hundred dollars to more than $1,000, and larger ones cost more: Bright Saver listed a 1,600-watt kit at $2,348 ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/what-to-know-balcony-solar)). A typical 400- to 800-watt system costs $500 to $1,500, and an 800-watt system might make 70 to 110 kilowatt-hours a month depending on location and sun ([EnergySage](https://www.energysage.com/news/plug-in-balcony-solar-panels/)).

What that's worth depends almost entirely on the local electricity price and on using the power as it's made. The prices below are the U.S. Energy Information Administration's residential averages for January through August 2026 ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/electricity/monthly/epm_table_grapher.php?t=epmt_5_6_b)); the Bureau of Labor Statistics' city average, shown on the site's electricity chart, runs a little higher.

- **At the national average.** The average U.S. residential price was about 18.2 cents a kilowatt-hour. At that price, 70 to 110 kilowatt-hours a month is worth about $13 to $20 a month, or roughly $150 to $240 a year, if all of it replaces power the home would have bought.
- **Where power costs more.** California's average was about 32.6 cents, so the same 70 to 110 kilowatt-hours a month is worth about $275 to $430 a year. Bright Saver, which in April sold an 800-watt kit for $1,499 in Los Angeles and the Bay Area, only to homes that already had rooftop solar, said its systems can save California households nearly $500 a year ([Canary Media](https://www.canarymedia.com/articles/solar/what-to-know-balcony-solar)).
- **Where power is cheap.** Utah's average was about 13.2 cents. Bright Saver estimates a 180-watt panel there makes about 270 kilowatt-hours a year ([Bright Saver](https://brightsaver.org/is-plug-in-solar-legal-/utah)), worth about $36 a year at that price.

Small kits save little anywhere. A Baltimore man with one of Bright Saver's smaller kits told the Associated Press in 2025 that he expected to save about $40 a year and pay off the kit in about 10 years ([Associated Press, via NBC 6 South Florida](https://www.nbcmiami.com/news/national-international/solar-panel-balcony-deck-gain-traction-us/3678061/?amp=1)). A balcony that faces north, or is shaded, makes much less. And because only power used on the spot counts, a home that's empty all day, or a part-time residence that's empty for months, gets less from the same kit.

## The safety question

Home wiring was designed around outlets that draw power. A plug-in panel adds power on a circuit, and the safety concern is that a wire could end up carrying more current than its breaker is watching for, or that the kit could interfere with the ground-fault outlets that cut power in a shock. A third concern is the plug itself: once it's pulled from the wall, its prongs must not stay energized, and engineers at UL Solutions, the testing lab, write that unprotected prongs can stay live for up to two seconds ([UL Solutions engineers, in Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/02/balcony-plug-in-solar-panels-are-we-ready-for-this-new-era-of-accessible-energy/)).

UL Solutions launched a certification for these systems, UL 3700, in January 2026 ([UL Solutions](https://www.ul.com/news/ul-solutions-debuts-testing-and-certification-framework-safer-plug-solar-across-united-states)). The state laws require certification by UL or an equivalent lab (as listed under the table above), though Maine also accepts some alternatives ([35-A M.R.S. §3475](https://legislature.maine.gov/statutes/35-A/title35-Asec3475.html)). Certified products have been slow to arrive. In July 2026, the Electric Power Research Institute, an energy research nonprofit, told IEEE Spectrum that the kits on sale were either uncertified or carried certifications not written for plug-in solar; the magazine later noted that one just-announced microinverter met the new standard ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/plug-in-solar-power)). By the fall, Hoymiles, APsystems and Mango Power had announced UL 3700 certifications, Mango's for a complete kit ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/09/21/plug-in-solar-roundup-mango-power-gets-ul-3700-bright-saver-kicks-off-crowdfunding/); [Mango Power, via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/mango-power-s-earns-ul-3700-certification-for-interactive-plug-in-solar-applications-302882939.html)), but certified complete kits were still rare; in July, amNY reported that none had been certified yet ([amNY](https://www.amny.com/politics/balcony-solar-bill-nyc-hochul/)).

Germany is the large-scale test. About 1.55 million balcony systems are registered with its grid regulator, with about 1.7 gigawatts of panel capacity, through October 2026 ([Bundesnetzagentur](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/DE/Fachthemen/ElektrizitaetundGas/ErneuerbareEnergien/EE-Statistik/DL/EEStatistikMaStR.pdf?__blob=publicationFile)). Estimates that count unregistered systems run higher. A paper by the engineer Bill Powers, published by Boston University's Economics in Context Initiative, cites the trade group SolarPower Europe for four million and reports no safety incidents ([Boston University Economics in Context Initiative](https://www.bu.edu/eci/files/2026/07/Simple-Safe-Ready-to-Go-Plug-In-Solar-Configuration-26_7-25-V2-.pdf)).

This article explains how the kits and the state laws work. It isn't electrical or legal advice. Every home's wiring is different, and what a kit saves depends on the house, the sun and the local price of power. Before plugging one in, read the kit's instructions and check that it carries the certification your state requires. If you're unsure whether a circuit can handle it, ask a licensed electrician. Renters and condo owners should also check their lease or association rules.

## Renters, condos and homeowners' associations

The state laws mostly settle the relationship with the utility, not with a landlord or an association. California's says nothing about leases, condominium rules or building rules about what can hang on a railing ([SB 868](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260SB868)), and New York's bill has no protections for renters or association members either ([pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/06/01/new-york-legislature-passes-sunny-act-plug-in-solar-legislation/); [amNY](https://www.amny.com/politics/balcony-solar-bill-nyc-hochul/)).

Four states go further. New Jersey's protections are the most detailed, covering landlords, associations and towns: landlords can't ban kits used by tenants who pay their own electric bill, and associations can't ban them outright, though both can set reasonable rules on size and placement, and tenants must give 14 days' written notice ([New Jersey Assembly Budget Committee](https://pub.njleg.state.nj.us/Bills/2026/A5000/4836_S3.HTM)). From January 1, 2027, Colorado says no one may unreasonably prohibit a kit ([HB26-1007](https://leg.colorado.gov/bill_files/116074/download)), and Virginia bars landlords with more than four units from prohibiting one ([Va. Code §55.1-1212.1](https://law.lis.virginia.gov/vacode/title55.1/chapter12/section55.1-1212.1/)). Vermont bars covenants and deed restrictions that prohibit them ([Vermont Act 149](https://legislature.vermont.gov/Documents/2026/Docs/ACTS/ACT149/ACT149%20As%20Enacted.pdf)).

## Where the analogy breaks

A space heater's current all flows through the breaker that protects its wire, so the breaker trips before the wire overheats. A plug-in panel's current enters the circuit at the outlet, so the wire between the outlet and the other things on that circuit can carry the panel's power plus the grid's, and the breaker only sees the grid's share. That's why the laws cap a kit's size (Germany set its 800-watt limit with circuit capacity in mind) and why some guidance recommends a dedicated circuit, or one with little else on it ([UL Solutions engineers, in Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/02/balcony-plug-in-solar-panels-are-we-ready-for-this-new-era-of-accessible-energy/); [Boston University Economics in Context Initiative](https://www.bu.edu/eci/files/2026/07/Simple-Safe-Ready-to-Go-Plug-In-Solar-Configuration-26_7-25-V2-.pdf)).

## What people argue about

**Safety and oversight.** The Boston University paper argues that simple steps, such as checking the outlet's ground-fault protection and using an outlet at the end of a circuit, can make kits safe, pointing to Germany's record ([Boston University Economics in Context Initiative](https://www.bu.edu/eci/files/2026/07/Simple-Safe-Ready-to-Go-Plug-In-Solar-Configuration-26_7-25-V2-.pdf)), and the bills have drawn votes from both parties: Utah's passed unanimously, and New Jersey's passed both houses without a no vote ([National Caucus of Environmental Legislators](https://ncel.net/articles/the-rise-of-plug-in-solar-how-states-can-reduce-costs-and-streamline-clean-energy-adoption/); [New Jersey Legislature](https://www.njleg.state.nj.us/bill-search/2026/S2368)). A landlords' trade group in Southern California opposed California's law, saying it "removes important safety oversight" for devices that feed power into a building through an outlet ([Apartment Association of Greater Los Angeles](https://members.aagla.org/news/governor-newsom-signs-three-troubling-housing-bills)); the statewide California Apartment Association supported an earlier version of the bill, a committee analysis shows ([Assembly Committee on Utilities and Energy](https://autl.assembly.ca.gov/media/1505)). Engineers at UL Solutions write that U.S. electrical codes assumed power flows one way, to outlets ([UL Solutions engineers, in Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/02/balcony-plug-in-solar-panels-are-we-ready-for-this-new-era-of-accessible-energy/)). An engineering director at the testing lab Intertek, writing in IAEI Magazine, says user-installed kits raise questions inspectors can't yet answer consistently, such as how many can share a circuit ([IAEI Magazine](https://iaeimagazine.org/columns/certification-insights/balcony-pv-enters-the-code-conversation/)). And a solar consultant told a New Hampshire webinar that buyers of kits sold online with no known listing take on the risk themselves ([Valley News](https://vnews.com/2026/08/14/nh-balcony-solar-law-2027/)).

**The utilities.** Utilities have fought similar bills in some states, a Gothamist reporter told WAMC, though Con Edison, the utility serving New York City, issued a memo supporting the SUNNY Act ([WAMC](https://www.wamc.org/podcast/northeastnotebook/2026-11-16/some-say-new-york-is-lagging-behind-on-balcony-solar-panels)). In California, utilities sought and won the 2030 end date on the exemption from their approval ([Solar Power World](https://www.solarpowerworldonline.com/2026/10/california-is-10th-state-to-legalize-balcony-solar/); [pv magazine USA](https://pv-magazine-usa.com/2026/10/01/newsom-signs-california-plug-in-solar-bill-establishing-the-nations-largest-balcony-solar-market/)). Southern California Edison told IEEE Spectrum it supports customer access but wants customers to notify it, so it can check that equipment is certified and track what's on its lines ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/plug-in-solar-power)); the final law lets utilities require a simple registration form. Advocates such as the Natural Resources Council of Maine answer that kits must shut off when the grid does and that what they send onto the grid is minimal ([Natural Resources Council of Maine](https://www.nrcm.org/blog/what-to-know-maines-new-plug-in-solar-law/)).

**Whether it's worth it.** At the national average residential price, an 800-watt kit could save something like $13 to $20 a month ($150 to $240 a year) if all its power is used at home, less in cheap-power states, and about $275 to $430 a year at California's average price. That's small next to a rooftop system, which EnergySage estimates saves $1,480 to $6,160 a year. EnergySage's view is that the kits make the most sense where rooftop solar isn't an option, as for many renters ([EnergySage](https://www.energysage.com/news/plug-in-balcony-solar-panels/)).
