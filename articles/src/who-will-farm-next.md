## The short version

The average American farm producer was 58.1 years old in 2022, and 38% were 65 or older. Between 2017 and 2022 the number of producers 65 and over rose 12% while the number aged 35 to 64 fell 9%. Producers under 35 rose slightly, to just under 9% of the total. Farmers are older in the South than in the Midwest. The people who own farmland and rent it out are older still: they averaged 69.2 years old in 2024, and more than a third were 75 or older. Some economists say the alarm is overstated. In a 2013 analysis, farmers' average age ran about 15 years above the median age of U.S. workers, a gap that had changed little since 1980; a 2024 update found farmers had aged a bit more slowly than the population since 1960. Farming takes so much land and equipment that it takes years to build up enough to start. Both sides expect a great deal of land to change hands in the coming decades, and owners' own five-year plans show most of it moving through wills, trusts and family, with little offered for sale. Young farmers say finding affordable land is their biggest obstacle, and most of the income of beginning farm households comes from off the farm. A 2025 tax law raised the estate tax exemption to $15 million and lets some sellers of farmland to a working farmer pay the tax over four years. Larger federal farm loans are in both chambers' farm bills, neither of which has become law, and the 2018 farm bill lapsed on September 30.

## The analogy

A relay race. Each runner carries the baton for a stretch and hands it to the next one while both are still moving. In farming the baton is the land, the equipment, the herd and what one generation knows about a particular piece of ground. A clean handoff keeps the farm running. A dropped baton is a farm sold out of farming, split among heirs who don't farm, or kept by an owner who has no one to hand it to.

The worry about farmers' age is a worry about the handoffs: whether there's a runner waiting, and whether that runner can afford to take the baton.

Figures below are as of mid-November 2026.

## How old farmers are

The Census of Agriculture counted 3.37 million producers in 2022. Their average age was 58.1, up 0.6 years from 2017, a smaller rise than in earlier censuses. On average they had been farming 23.4 years. Producers are older, on average, in southern states and younger in the Midwest ([USDA NASS](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Highlights/2024/Census22_HL_FarmProducers_FINAL.pdf)).

The growth was at the older end. Producers 65 and over rose 12%, from 1.15 million to 1.29 million, and became 38% of all producers. Those aged 35 to 64 fell 9%. Those under 35 rose 3.9%, to 296,480, just under 9% (same source). The 221,233 farms with a young producer making decisions tend to be larger than average in both acres and sales ([USDA NASS](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/2024/02-13-2024.php)).

"New" doesn't mean young. Just over 1 million producers in 2022 had farmed 10 years or less. Their average age was 47.1; most of them, 69%, were 35 to 64, and 15% were 65 or older ([Katchova and Sun, Choices](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/347841/files/cmsarticle_934.pdf)).

## Is it a crisis?

**The case that it's overstated.** Carl Zulauf, an agricultural economist at Ohio State, compared farmers with the U.S. labor force in 2013. Farmers' average age in the 2007 census was about 15 years above the labor force's median age, and from 1980 on the gap barely changed: the two aged "in concert," he wrote, and if anything farmers aged a little more slowly. He attributed farmers' older age to how much capital farming takes; it takes years to build up enough, by inheritance, savings or both, to farm at American scale. The one notable drop in farmers' average age came in the mid-to-late 1970s, a boom, and he expected good years to draw younger people in again ([Zulauf, farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2013/10/putting-age-us-farmers-perspective.html)). In 2020 he noted that farmers 65 and older produced 23% of farm sales and argued that policy should focus more on helping older farmers stay active, since in his experience most intend to keep farming as long as they can ([Zulauf, farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2020/02/age-of-us-farmers-is-the-wrong-issue-being-addressed.html)). In 2024, with the 2022 census, he found that farmers' average age had risen 7.6 years since 1960 while the median age of all Americans rose 8.8, and concluded that the numbers don't suggest the U.S. has a farmer replacement problem ([Zulauf, farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2024/05/age-of-us-farmers-not-a-problem.html)).

Counting farms can also exaggerate the picture. In Illinois in 2012, principal operators 65 and older ran 31.6% of farms but worked 24.6% of the farmland and earned 21.5% of farm income. Most of the land and income was in the hands of farmers aged 45 to 64, about what you'd expect of most small businesses ([Kuethe, farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2015/02/we-may-not-have-an-aging-farmer-problem.html)).

**The case that it's real.** Older farmers are a larger share than older workers elsewhere: in the 2017 census, 34% of farmers were 65 or older, against 14% of self-employed people in businesses other than farming ([Zulauf, 2020](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2020/02/age-of-us-farmers-is-the-wrong-issue-being-addressed.html)). By 2022 the farmers' share was 38%. And the age of the people working the land is only half of it. The other half is the age of the people who own it.

## Who owns the land, and how it will change hands

About 348 million acres of farmland were rented out in 2024, roughly two of every five acres the department's landlord survey covered. The principal landlords were older than farmers generally: their average age was 69.2, up from 66.5 in 2014, more than 35% were 75 or older, and 11% were 85 or older. More than half had never farmed ([USDA NASS, 2024 TOTAL survey briefing](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/Executive_Briefings/2026/03-12-2026.pdf)). By American Farmland Trust's count, landlords 65 and older own about 24% of all U.S. farm and ranch land, up from 14% in 2014 ([American Farmland Trust](https://farmlandinfo.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/07/AFT_FIC_TOTAL-Fact-Sheet.pdf)).

Most farmland isn't headed for the open market. Asked about the next five years, all owners of farmland, farmers and landlords alike, expected to sell about 22.8 million acres to someone outside the family, about 2.6% of the 876 million acres the survey covered, and to sell 12.8 million acres to relatives and give away 7.4 million. Far more is going into estate plans: 135.2 million acres put or kept in a will, and 91.5 million in a trust ([USDA NASS](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/Executive_Briefings/2026/03-12-2026.pdf)).

Over a longer stretch, American Farmland Trust estimated from the 2014 landlord survey and the 2017 census that nearly 300 million acres, about a third of the land in farms in the lower 48 states, could change hands in the next 20 years, counting land owned by landlords 65 and older and by farms whose principal producers were all 65 or older ([American Farmland Trust](https://farmlandinfo.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2024/01/AFT_FIC_TOTAL_TalkingPoints_2023.pdf)). The trust hasn't published an updated 20-year figure from the 2024 survey. Inherited land doesn't have to leave farming: an heir who doesn't farm can rent it to someone who does, which is how much of the land owned by landlords who have never farmed is worked today.

## What stands in the way of starting

**Land.** Cropland averaged $6,020 an acre in 2026, up from $4,940 in 2022, while cash rent for cropland averaged $160 an acre ([Farm Policy News, on USDA's Land Values report](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/2026/08/us-cropland-tops-6000-per-acre-for-first-time-in-2026/)). In the National Young Farmers Coalition's 2022 survey, 59% of young farmers said finding affordable land to buy was very or extremely challenging, and 45% said the same of finding available land to buy. The coalition is an advocacy group, and its survey drew more than 10,000 responses, about 4,300 of them from farmers 40 and under, from people who chose to answer, not a random sample. Land topped its list of challenges in 2017 and 2022 and ranked second, behind lack of capital, in 2011 ([National Young Farmers Coalition, 2022 report](https://www.sdnewswatch.org/content/files/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/nationalsurveyreport22-1.pdf); [National Young Farmers Coalition, 2011](https://www.youngfarmers.org/?p=34852)).

**Income.** Beginning farms mostly start small, and their most common specializations are beef cattle and grain and oilseed crops ([Key and Lyons, USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/sites/default/files/_laserfiche/publications/95010/EB-29.pdf)). Most of their households' income comes from off the farm. From 2013 to 2017, off-farm income was 77% of total household income for beginning farms and 56% for established ones, counting farms that produced at least $10,000 a year. In 2017, 67% of beginning farms' principal operators worked off the farm, against 45% of established ones; from 2013 to 2017, a spouse worked off the farm in 55% of beginning farm households, against 41% ([USDA ERS, Amber Waves](https://www.ers.usda.gov/amber-waves/2020/february/beginning-farm-operators-and-spouses-more-likely-to-work-off-the-farm-than-established-counterparts)).

**Health insurance.** Most farmers are self-employed, so a family's health coverage often comes through an off-farm job. A study of married farm-operator households found that those with employer-sponsored coverage were about 14 percentage points more likely to work off the farm ([Miller, Mishra and Villacis, Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/316759/files/JARE316759.pdf?version=1)). In the coalition's 2022 survey, 40% of young farmers called personal or family health care costs very or extremely challenging ([National Young Farmers Coalition, 2022 report](https://www.sdnewswatch.org/content/files/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/nationalsurveyreport22-1.pdf)).

**Credit.** The Agriculture Department's Farm Service Agency lends directly to farmers who can't get enough credit elsewhere and guarantees loans that banks make. In fiscal 2025, beginning farmers received 15,552 of its loans, totaling $3.53 billion: about 56% of all its farm loans and 65% of its direct loans to buy farmland ([American Farm Bureau Federation](https://www.fb.org/intel/markets/financing-the-farm-a-look-at-usda-farm-loan-programs)). A direct loan to buy a farm is capped at $600,000, which buys about 100 acres of average cropland at 2026 prices. Operating loans carry a crop until it's sold, and [You run a farm](you-run-a-farm.html) covers what they cost.

## What has changed, and what's still in Congress

**Estate tax.** The 2025 tax and spending law set the federal estate tax exemption at $15 million a person, $30 million for a married couple, starting in 2026, and indexed to inflation after that ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill); [Texas A&M AgriLife](https://agrilife.org/texasaglaw/2026/02/16/2026-federal-estate-and-gift-tax-exemption-information/)). Without the law, the exemption would have fallen to roughly $7 million ([RFD-TV](https://www.rfdtv.com/estate-tax-changes-ease-burden-but-succession-looms)). Few farm estates owed the tax even before: with a $12.92 million exemption, the department estimated that 89 of the farm estates created in 2023, about 0.2%, would likely owe it ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/109393)). Rusty Rumley, a senior staff attorney at the National Agricultural Law Center, told RFD-TV that the higher exemption and the rules letting a surviving spouse use the unused part of a deceased spouse's exemption mean most farms will avoid immediate estate tax, and that planning for who takes over remains the larger concern (same RFD-TV source).

**Selling to a farmer.** The same law lets an owner who sells farmland to a working farmer pay the federal income tax on the gain in four equal yearly installments instead of all at once ([IRS, Instructions for Form 1062](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/i1062.pdf)). The land must have been farmed, by the owner or a farmer renting it, for substantially all of the 10 years before the sale, and it must carry a legally binding restriction to farm use for 10 years after. The Treasury and the IRS proposed rules for it in September 2026 ([Thomson Reuters](https://tax.thomsonreuters.com/news/proposed-rules-clarify-farmland-sale-tax-installment-election/); [Iowa State University Center for Agricultural Law and Taxation](https://www.calt.iastate.edu/post/deferring-tax-payments-sale-farmland)).

**The farm bill.** The 2018 farm bill, extended three times, lapsed on September 30, 2026 ([AgWeb](https://www.agweb.com/news/farm-bill-expires-congress-faces-tight-deadline-renewal)). The House passed its replacement, the Farm, Food, and National Security Act of 2026, on April 30, by 224 to 200 ([House Clerk](https://clerk.house.gov/evs/2026/roll154)). The Senate Agriculture Committee approved its version, the Agricultural Act of 2026, on September 16 by a party-line 12 to 11, and the full Senate hasn't voted on it ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-09-24_IF12047_f699a37e329990027e1e448d58dc74e09a88b1ba.html); [WJLA](https://wjla.com/news/nation-world/mcconnell-returns-to-back-senate-farm-bill-as-panel-advances-it-on-party-line-vote)). Both bills would raise the cap on a direct loan to buy a farm from $600,000 to $850,000 and on a direct operating loan from $400,000 to $750,000. They would raise the cap on a guaranteed farm ownership loan to $3.5 million, from a statutory base of $1.75 million that inflation adjustments have raised to about $2.56 million in fiscal 2027 ([Senate Agriculture Committee](https://www.agriculture.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/final_title_by_title_summaries.pdf); [National Sustainable Agriculture Coalition](https://sustainableagriculture.net/?p=61040); [USDA FSA](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/Internet/FSA_File/1flp-309.pdf)). In late October the Senate committee's chairman, John Boozman, said his goal was a five-year farm bill by the end of the year ([University of Arkansas Division of Agriculture](https://www.uaex.uada.edu/media-resources/news/2026/november/11-9-2026-ark-fryar-boozman.aspx)); Jonathan Coppess, a University of Illinois farm policy expert, expects Congress to extend the 2018 law again instead and leave the new bill to the next Congress ([farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/11/fdd110526.pdf)). {{due 2026-12-31 "Has a farm bill become law, or has the 2018 farm bill been extended again (e.g., in a continuing resolution)? ## Where the analogy breaks

A relay has a finish line and a fixed order of runners. A farm has neither. Many older farmers keep running alongside the next generation for years, sharing decisions rather than handing off at a mark. The baton can be split: the land to one heir, the equipment to another, the farming to a renter. And a runner can come from outside the family, though most land is handed within one.

## What people argue about

**Crisis or normal aging?** The National Young Farmers Coalition and American Farmland Trust say the coming transfer of land puts the next generation at risk unless land and credit become easier to get. In 2022 the coalition asked Congress for $2.5 billion for land access ([Farm and Dairy](https://www.farmanddairy.com/?p=735097)), and in August 2026 it urged senators to vote against the committee's farm bill, calling it a missed opportunity that ignores the need for investment in land access ([The Packer](https://www.thepacker.com/news/young-farmers-coalition-calls-revised-senate-farm-bill-missed-opportunity)). Zulauf and Kuethe say the age numbers look worse than they are once you compare farmers with other workers and weigh farms by acres and income ([Zulauf](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2024/05/age-of-us-farmers-not-a-problem.html); [Kuethe](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/2015/02/we-may-not-have-an-aging-farmer-problem.html)), and Zulauf argues that good farm years draw young people in. Iowa State's Mike Duffy, responding to Zulauf in 2013, agreed with his numbers but not his conclusion, saying the catch is helping young people with few assets and little access to land get a chance to farm ([MPR News, from the Associated Press](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2013/11/30/economist-challenges-idea-of-aging-farmer-crisis)).

**Bigger federal farm loans: who benefits?** Farm and lender groups, including the American Farm Bureau Federation, the National Farmers Union, the American Bankers Association and the Farm Credit Council, back higher loan caps, saying land values and input costs have outgrown them ([KFGO](https://kfgo.com/2025/03/12/hoeven-and-klobuchar-reintroduce-bill-to-improve-producer-access-to-credit/); [American Farm Bureau Federation](https://www.fb.org/intel/markets/financing-the-farm-a-look-at-usda-farm-loan-programs)). The National Sustainable Agriculture Coalition backs some increases but warns that raising the caps without raising the total available for loans could mean bigger loans to fewer farms and less money for smaller ones ([National Sustainable Agriculture Coalition](https://sustainableagriculture.net/?p=61040)).

**The estate tax.** Supporters of the higher exemption, including the American Farm Bureau Federation, say it keeps families from having to sell land or equipment to pay the tax when a farmer dies ([American Farm Bureau Federation](https://www.fb.org/news-release/estate-tax-cliff-threatens-family-farms)). Skeptics of that argument point to the department's estimates that only a fraction of 1% of farm estates were likely to owe the tax even under the earlier exemption ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/109393)).
