## In one sentence

The almanac's gas price is the Energy Information Administration's weekly average of what about a thousand stations across the country charge in cash for a gallon of regular at 8 a.m. on Monday, taxes included; your station can sit well above or below it, mostly on state taxes and local competition, but it moves with this line.

## The analogy

A road with four toll booths, one after another, and every gallon pays all four. The first booth charges for the crude oil, and its toll changes by the minute, set on a world market where a barrel in Houston and a barrel in Rotterdam trade against each other. The second charges for refining crude into gasoline; its toll jumps when a refinery breaks down or a hurricane shuts the Gulf Coast. The third is the trucks and the station, a fairly steady charge per gallon. The fourth is government, mostly a fixed number of cents per gallon rather than a percentage, so it barely rises with the price. The Energy Information Administration splits the price into those four parts every month. In 2025, when regular averaged $3.10, crude was about $1.59 of it, refining 44 cents, distribution and the station 55 cents, and taxes about 52 cents. In May 2026, at $4.48, crude was $2.32 and refining 97 cents; taxes were still about 52 cents.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**Your tank: within days.** At $4.27 a gallon, filling a 15-gallon tank costs about $64. A year ago it cost about $46. Of all the almanac's prices, this one reaches you fastest: stations reprice daily, and you buy it every week or two.

**Everything that moves by truck or plane: within weeks.** Diesel and jet fuel are made from the same crude. Airline fares were up 22% from a year earlier in September as jet fuel fed through, and freight costs reach store shelves over the following months. [Why everything costs 30% more](why-everything-costs-30-percent-more.html) follows the chain.

**Inflation, and the Fed: the same month.** Energy is the loudest part of the consumer price index. Gasoline accounted for more than half of September's 0.6% rise in prices, which is why headline inflation (3.7%) ran so far above core (2.6%), the measure that leaves food and energy out. The Fed mostly looks through an oil shock unless it starts showing up in everything else; [supply vs. demand](supply-vs-demand.html) is the argument about whether 2026 is that case.

## What moves it

Crude oil, mostly. A barrel holds 42 gallons, so every $1 change in the price of a barrel is worth about 2.4 cents a gallon once it works through. Over the past decade crude has averaged about half the pump price. Then the refiners' margin, which widens when refineries are down for repairs, in hurricane season, and in spring, when stations switch to summer gasoline that costs more to make. The station's own margin moves opposite to wholesale in the short run, squeezed when wholesale prices spike and rebuilt when they fall, which is part of why prices rise fast and fall slowly. Taxes barely move. The federal tax was set at 18.4 cents a gallon in 1993, and state taxes averaged about 33 cents in January 2026, so together they come to roughly 52 cents whether gas costs $3 or $5.

In 2026 the first two booths did nearly all of it: from the 2025 average to May, crude added about 73 cents a gallon and refining about 53. After the U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran on February 28 and the closing of the Strait of Hormuz to most shipping, the national average went from $2.94 a gallon the week before to $3.50 within ten days and a 2026 high of $4.50 in May. About a fifth of the world's oil normally passes through the strait, some 20 million barrels a day in 2024. Brent crude, the world benchmark, averaged $71 a barrel in February, $103 in March and $117 in April. It eased to $85 in June and climbed back to $114 in September, and the pump followed it down and back up a few weeks behind: from $4.50 in May to $3.78 in early July, then to $4.48 in late September.

## How to read this week's number

**Against crude.** If the pump price moved and crude didn't, look at refineries and the season. If crude moved and the pump hasn't caught up, it will: the pass-through takes a few weeks, and it is faster on the way up.

**Against the season.** The series is not seasonally adjusted. Prices usually rise in spring, when stations switch from winter gasoline to the summer blend, which costs more to make, and the driving season begins, so part of any spring rise is the calendar.

**Against history, adjusted for inflation.** The almanac's range for gas is in today's dollars, because a raw price only ever trends up. At $4.27, gas is higher than about 80% of weeks since 1990 by that measure, but below its June 2022 peak of $5.01, which would be about $5.70 today, and well below June 2008, about $6.33 in today's dollars.

## Where the analogy breaks

Tolls are posted; these are bid. The crude toll is set in futures markets on what traders expect a barrel to cost next month, so a threat to the Strait of Hormuz can raise it before a single tanker is stopped. The booths also react at different speeds, and not independently: when crude jumps, the station's toll shrinks for a week or two as it absorbs the wholesale increase, then rebuilds. And the road is not the same everywhere. California requires its own costlier blend, taxes gasoline at about 90 cents a gallon all told, and is cut off by mountains from most other refining centers, so its pump price often runs more than a dollar above the national average. The national line is a poor guide to a California pump.

## What people argue about

**Whether a president can bring gas prices down.** One side: there are real levers, releasing oil from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve, waiving summer fuel rules or shipping restrictions, permitting and leasing that shape production over years, and diplomacy and sanctions that move supply abroad. In 2022 the Treasury estimated that the reserve's largest-ever sale, with allies' releases, held gasoline down by up to about 40 cents a gallon. The other: the price of a barrel is set on a world market, and the United States became the world's largest oil producer without escaping it. In 2026 the administration pulled most of the levers within weeks: it offered to lend 172 million barrels from the reserve as its share of a 400-million-barrel international release, waived summer fuel rules and the Jones Act, which reserves shipping between U.S. ports for American ships, and temporarily lifted sanctions on Iranian and Russian oil already at sea. With domestic output near its record, the pump price still rose more than 50% between February and May. The one lever only Congress holds, suspending the federal tax, has never been used; a bill to do it stalled in the House in September. Both sides agree the reserve can soften a spike but cannot end one.

**Whether rising fast and falling slowly is gouging.** One side: when crude falls, stations and refiners are slow to pass it on, and that lag is money taken from drivers. The other: economists have documented the pattern, called rockets and feathers, since the 1990s, and the leading explanations are about how stations compete and how slowly drivers shop around when prices are falling, not about collusion. When the Federal Trade Commission investigated the price spike after Hurricane Katrina, it found no evidence that refiners manipulated prices, and only limited, short-lived gouging by some individual sellers. Later research put most of the lag between the wholesale market and the pump: one study found retail prices rise three to four times as fast as they fall, more so where a few companies own most of the stations.

**Drill more, or use less.** One side: more domestic production and pipelines lower costs and protect the country from foreign shocks. The other: oil is priced worldwide, so only using less of it, through efficiency and electric vehicles, reduces a household's exposure to the next Gulf war. Each side's evidence is the other side's 2026: near-record U.S. output, and a price spike anyway.
