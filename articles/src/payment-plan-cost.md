## How it's worked out

The total is each payment times the number of payments. The extra is the total less the price, or less the cash you get.

The yearly rate is the annual percentage rate (APR) a loan with the same payments would carry, counting everything above the price as the cost of borrowing. It's the rate per period that makes the payments, discounted back to the day you get the item or the cash, add up to its price; that rate times the number of periods in a year is the APR. This is close to the method in the federal disclosure rules, which also set the period of a single-payment loan by its length in days (a two-week loan has about 26.07 periods a year). If the first payment is due when you get the item, it counts as a down payment, and the rate applies to the rest.

Rent-to-own agreements and some paycheck-advance apps usually aren't treated as loans under federal law, so they don't have to show an APR. The figure here is what a loan with the same payments would carry. It leaves out sales tax and optional fees, such as damage waivers, which raise the cost further.
