## What reaches you, in order

1. **Inflation**, at every checkout, against a wage that changes once a year.
2. **The unemployment rate**, which sets how much bargaining power you have when the raise is discussed, and how fast you'd find the next job.
3. **Jobless claims**, within a week, if the layoff is yours.
4. **Jobs added**, as the number your employer's hiring plans and the Fed's decisions both read, including the version that gets revised.
5. **The Fed**, which is deliberately aiming at the second number in order to slow the first.

Mortgage rates, housing starts and the 10-year reach you as a renter or an owner, on those pages. This one is about the paycheck.

## Pay against prices

Typical hourly pay for production and nonsupervisory workers, about four-fifths of private employment, is up about 38% since 2019. Prices are up about 31%. That is a real gain of about 5% over seven years, less than 1% a year, and it did not arrive evenly: real pay fell for two years from mid-2021, rose from 2023 to early 2026, and has been falling again since spring as energy prices rose. The year-over-year version is on the inflation chart as "real wage growth," and it has been below zero since May.

What that average hides is the spread. Through 2023 the lowest-paid quarter of workers saw the largest real raises in forty years, while the top quarter lost a little, because a tight job market bids hardest for the people easiest to replace. The gains were real and they were concentrated at the bottom, which is why the same years read as a raise to some and a cut to others.

## The unemployment rate is your leverage

When the rate is low, employers compete for workers; when it is high, workers compete for jobs. The clearest evidence is what happens to people who switch: in 2022, with unemployment at 3.5%, job-switchers got raises about two points larger than people who stayed, and the share of workers quitting hit a record. By 2025–26, with the rate near 4.2% and hiring at its slowest pace outside a recession in more than a decade, the switcher premium had mostly closed. Nothing about the rate itself changed much. What changed was how easy it was to leave.

The rate also sets the cost of a layoff: at 3.5% the next job is weeks away; at 6% it is months. [The unemployment rate](what-is-the-unemployment-rate.html) explains who is counted, and the prime-age employment ratio on its chart is the better gauge of how full the room is.

## Claims, if it's you

Unemployment insurance replaces about half of prior pay up to a state cap, for 26 weeks in most states and as few as 12 in some, and only for layoffs. Claims nationally have sat near 200,000 a week, a low-layoff economy; continuing claims, which measure how long people stay on the rolls, rose through 2025 and have eased, which says the laid-off were taking longer to land. [Jobless claims](what-are-jobless-claims.html) has what the number can and can't see, which matters for anyone on contract or gig work, who aren't in it at all.

## Jobs added, and the first-print trap

The monthly payroll number is the one your employer's planners and the Fed read, and it is revised: since 2023 the first print has been revised down by about 65,000 a month on average. A worker reading "jobs beat expectations" on a Friday and planning around it is reading a number that will, on recent form, be smaller by the time it's final. The three-month average is the honest version, and [the jobs report](what-is-the-jobs-report.html) page has the revision record. The same release carries the wage line this page opened with.

## The Fed is aiming at your job market, on purpose

When inflation runs above target, the Fed raises rates to slow spending, and the channel through which that works is employment: fewer hires, slower raises, and, if it goes too far, layoffs. This is not a side effect. In 2022 the Fed chair said plainly that bringing inflation down would likely require "some pain" in the labor market. In the end unemployment rose less than a point and inflation fell anyway, which is the softest landing on record and the subject of the first argument below. In 2026 the Fed raised rates into a job market that was already soft, which is the second.

## Where you come out ahead

Wages are the one income that renegotiates with prices, imperfectly and late, but it does. A retiree's pension doesn't. A worker in a tight market has the leverage no other household on this site has, and the 2021–23 compression showed what that leverage does at the bottom of the pay scale. Against that: the raise comes once a year and prices move every month; the people with the least leverage are the first to feel a slowdown; and the first-print trap means the headlines about the job market are, on average, better than the job market.

## What people argue about

**Whether the Fed had to cool the job market.** One side: inflation fell because supply chains healed and energy prices reversed, and the rate hikes mostly raised mortgage rates and slowed building; the job market would have cooled on its own. The other: the hikes kept wage growth from feeding a second round, which is what the 1970s lacked, and the soft landing is proof the dose was right. The data show the outcome. They can't show what would have happened without the hikes, because that economy didn't run.

**Whether wages cause inflation.** The textbook spiral is wages chasing prices chasing wages. In 2021–23 prices moved first and wages lagged for two years, which one side reads as workers catching up rather than causing anything, and points to profit margins widening before wages did. The other side says that once wages caught up, services inflation (which is mostly labor) became the sticky part, and that is why the last two points of inflation took two years. Both can be seen on the chart above; the order of the lines is the argument.

**The minimum wage.** The federal minimum has been $7.25 since 2009, the longest stretch without an increase since it was created; most states and many cities set higher ones, and more than half of workers are in a state above the federal floor. One side says raising it lifts the bottom with little job loss, citing the state-by-state record; the other says it prices out the least experienced and that the 2021–23 market raised the bottom faster than any law. The almanac marks each federal increase on the jobs charts, which is the record both sides read.
