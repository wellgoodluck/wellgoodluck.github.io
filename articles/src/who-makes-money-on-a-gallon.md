## The question

Regular gasoline averaged $4.15 a gallon in the week of November 2, $1.13 more than a year earlier. In the same weeks the oil companies reported their third quarter, and the profits were large: by a New York Times count, more than 50 big U.S. oil and gas companies earned over $130 billion from April through September, almost three times as much as in the same months of 2025. So the question in every argument about the pump is a fair one: who is getting the extra dollar?

The companies' own reports give a clearer answer than most of those arguments. The extra profit of 2026 is mostly in refining, the business of turning crude oil into fuel, and much less in pumping the crude itself. This page follows the dollar, then sets out the two readings of it. It is dated: the figures are as of early November 2026.

## Where the extra dollar went

The Energy Information Administration splits the pump price every month into four parts: the crude oil, refining, distribution and the station, and taxes. The [gas page](what-sets-the-price-of-gas.html) explains the four. Its latest month is August.

{{figure kind=pairs title="What a gallon of regular paid for" series="August 2025;August 2026" rows="Crude oil=1.58,2.04;Refining=0.55,0.99;Distribution and the station=0.49,0.51;Taxes=0.51,0.52" fmt="${v}" caption="Dollars per gallon, from EIA's percentage split of the average retail price: $3.13 in August 2025, $4.06 in August 2026." source="EIA, Gasoline and Diesel Fuel Update"}}

Of the 93-cent rise from August to August, crude accounted for about 46 cents and refining for about 44. Distribution and the station added about 2 cents and taxes about 1. Refining went from 18% of the price to 24%, and in July it reached 26%, while crude's share barely moved. The station's share shrank: it collected about the same cents on a costlier gallon, and EIA describes retail margins in 2026 as depressed.

One caution about the refining slice. EIA measures it as the gap between the wholesale price of gasoline and the price of crude, so it covers the refiners' costs (labor, energy, maintenance, environmental credits) as well as their profit. A wider gap is not all profit. But the profit reports show that this year much of it was.

## The oil came back. The fuel didn't.

After the U.S. and Israeli strikes on Iran on February 28, Brent crude went from $72.48 a barrel to well above $100. Seven months later, crude had largely found a way around the Strait of Hormuz. JPMorgan estimated at the end of September that crude flows from the Middle East were back to about 98% of pre-war levels, and the ship-tracking firm Kpler counted 14 days in September when they topped the pre-war average. Much of it now avoids the strait: Kpler found that about 40% of the region's crude exports, Iran's aside, left from the Gulf of Oman coast or through the Red Sea in September, against 17% before the war.

Refined fuel did not come back the same way. The same JPMorgan note put the region's exports of products such as diesel, jet fuel and gasoline at about 58% of pre-war; the International Energy Agency's count for September was lower, 1.9 million barrels a day against 4.7 million. Russia, the other big exporter of diesel, lost much of its refining to Ukrainian drone strikes: by the IEA's figures its refineries ran about 30% below a year earlier in June, the lowest in more than 20 years, and Russia banned gasoline exports in April and diesel exports in July. Its refineries recovered from mid-October as the strikes slowed, though Ukraine struck Russian refineries again in early November. In September, the IEA reported, the Middle East and Russia together had net exports of diesel less than 40% as large as a year earlier.

The world has been short of finished fuel more than of crude, and when fuel is scarce, the price of fuel rises faster than the price of the oil it is made from. That gap is the refiners' margin. On the Gulf Coast, Valero's measure of the gasoline margin averaged about $24 a barrel in the third quarter, against about $8 a year earlier, and the diesel margin about $72, against $21. EIA reported that the gasoline margin more than doubled from a year earlier and the diesel and jet fuel margins almost tripled. That is why diesel, at $6.29 a gallon in the week of November 2, has risen more than gasoline.

U.S. refiners ran nearly flat out to catch it. Refineries used an average of more than 95% of their capacity from May through September, the highest for those months since 1998, and shifted their output toward diesel and jet fuel. Net exports of diesel and other distillates averaged 1.61 million barrels a day in the third quarter, against 1.17 million a year earlier, and net exports of gasoline rose to about 0.53 million barrels a day, from 0.18 million.

## What the profit reports say

The third-quarter reports, filed from late October into the first days of November, show the same pattern company by company.

The refiners show it most plainly: Valero earned more than six times what it did a year earlier, and Marathon more than five times. ExxonMobil's own estimate, filed before its results, put the gain in refining margins from the spring at about $4.5 billion and the loss from lower oil and gas liquids prices at about $1.3 billion. Its oil and gas production business earned less in the summer than in the spring; its refining business more than doubled, before one-time items and timing effects. The Times total, for its part, included analysts' estimates for the third quarter when it was published.

## A market of its own: California

California shows what thin supply does to a margin. Two of its refineries stopped making fuel in the past year: Phillips 66 in Los Angeles (about 139,000 barrels a day) stopped processing crude in October 2025, and Valero's Benicia refinery (145,000) wound down over the first three months of 2026 and ended fuel production in April. Together they were about 17% of the state's refining capacity. No pipeline brings gasoline into California from across the Rockies, so the shortfall comes by tanker: the state imported about 165,000 barrels a day of gasoline and blending components in 2025, and the state's energy commission expects more in 2026.

The refiners' own reports to the California Energy Commission show the result. Their gross margin on gasoline averaged about 75 cents a gallon from March 2025 through February 2026; in March and April 2026 it averaged $1.13, peaked at $1.29 in May, and was $1.09 in August. The commission notes the figures are self-reported and that a gross margin is not profit. In the week of November 2, California regular averaged $5.75, $1.60 above the national average. A 2023 state law let the commission cap refiners' margins and fine companies that exceed the cap; in August 2025 it voted to put off setting one for at least five years.

Why the two refineries closed is its own argument. Valero's chief executive called California's regulatory and enforcement environment the most stringent and difficult in North America, and Phillips 66 pointed to high costs and low earnings at its Los Angeles plant, Snopes reported. Severin Borenstein of the Energy Institute at Haas, at the University of California, Berkeley, has pointed to falling demand for gasoline in the state, along with regulation and local opposition. A widely shared claim that the state had closed four refineries was rated mostly false by Snopes in September: two closed in this period; the other two had stopped processing crude in 2020 and 2024.

## What drivers paid

Three trackers put a number on what Americans have paid for the war at the pump, each by a different method:

- Brown University's Watson Institute, a university research lab: about $153 billion in extra spending on gasoline and diesel through November 5, about $1,165 a household. It compares actual prices with an estimate of what prices would have been without the war.
- The Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, a progressive tax-policy research group: about $130 billion, about $970 a household, through November 5.
- The Centre for Research on Energy and Clean Air, a research group that advocates moving away from fossil fuels: about $160 billion from March 1 to mid-October, against prices in January and February.

Mark Zandi of Moody's Analytics put the war's cost at nearly $2,000 a household: about $1,100 in higher energy prices of every kind, $540 in higher interest rates and $360 in military costs.

The drivers' bill and the companies' profits are not the two ends of one pipe. Some of what drivers paid went to foreign producers and refiners and to the costs of running refineries harder; some of the profit came from customers abroad. But they are the same order of size, which is why the comparison keeps coming up.

## What people argue about

**Gouging, or what a shortage costs.** One side: margins have run far ahead of costs, and refiners are profiting from a shortage at drivers' expense. With fewer refineries, especially in California, the ones left face less competition; Borenstein has called imports a critical check on the market power of the refiners left in the state. The energy commission itself told legislators in May that of California's price rise since the war began, higher crude costs accounted for about $1.13 a gallon and wider refining and distribution margins for about 32 cents, which it attributed to a premium the market was charging for volatility. Consumer Watchdog, a California advocacy group, estimated that a $1-a-gallon margin cap would have returned about $610 million to the state's drivers from March through May. Asked in August about Exxon's and Chevron's earnings, President Trump said, "They're making too much money based on a shortage. I don't like it." In June he said he had told the Justice Department to look into prices, and in July the department and the Federal Trade Commission urged state attorneys general to investigate; as of early November neither had publicly announced an investigation of a named company. The other side: refiners ran nearly flat out, so the high margins did not come from holding fuel back; when the world is short of diesel and gasoline, the price of the fuel that exists rises until demand fits supply, and high U.S. prices are what pull tanker cargoes to places like California. The nearest precedent is a different kind of shock: when the FTC investigated the price spike after Hurricane Katrina in 2005, it found no evidence of illegal manipulation; it found 15 companies whose prices fit the law's definition of evidence of gouging, and in nearly all of those cases regional or local market trends appeared to explain the prices.

**Why California lost its refineries.** One side: the state's rules, permitting and enforcement made refining there more stringent and difficult than anywhere in North America, and the closures, and the price spike, are the result. The other: California's gasoline use has started to trend down as cars get more efficient and more of them are electric, and refiners were closing plants that no longer paid in a shrinking market. Borenstein's own account includes both.

## How long it lasts

ExxonMobil's chief executive, Darren Woods, told analysts on October 30 that his guess was it would take well through next year, possibly longer, before fuel markets get closer to normal. His reasons: Middle East refiners that can't get their output to world markets, Russian refining capacity still offline, and how hard China chooses to run its spare refineries for export. Brent fell below $100 on November 4 and settled at $98.29 on November 5.
