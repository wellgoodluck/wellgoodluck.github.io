## In one sentence

An owner who moves can sell the house and give up its mortgage, or keep both and rent the house out, and renting it turns a home into a small business in the eyes of everyone who sends a bill.

## The analogy

Think of a parking space leased for 30 years at 2021's price, in a city where a space now costs twice as much. The lease can't move with you. When you leave town you can hand it back, or keep paying it and let someone else park there.

Figures below are as of November 2026. This page describes what changes. It doesn't say which to do, and it isn't tax or legal advice.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

In the second quarter of 2026, 49.1% of outstanding mortgages carried a rate below 4% and 65.6% a rate below 5%, by Realtor.com's reading of the federal mortgage database ([Realtor.com](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/mortgage-rate-lock-in-still-dominates-302915980.html)). Take an owner who borrowed $320,000 at 3% in 2021. The payment of principal and interest is $1,349 a month, and about $284,500 is still owed. Borrowing that $284,500 again at 7.24%, the average in late October 2026, would cost about $1,939 a month: $590 more for the same debt, by our arithmetic.

Economists call the result lock-in. Researchers at the Federal Housing Finance Agency found that each percentage point by which the market rate exceeds an owner's own rate lowers the chance of a sale by 18.1%, and estimated that lock-in prevented 1.33 million sales from spring 2022 through the end of 2023 ([FHFA](https://www.fhfa.gov/research/papers/wp2403)).

Some owners who do move keep the house. Zillow counts homes that were listed for sale, went unsold and turned up as rentals within three months. They were 2.3% of the rentals listed on its site in October 2025, just under the record of 2.4% set in November 2022. Among single-family houses the share was 3.4%. It ran from 4.9% in Denver to 0.6% in Boston and Providence ([Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/research/accidental-landlords/)).

## What changes when the house becomes a rental

**The mortgage.** The standard Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac mortgage form, for a loan made on a primary home, has the borrower promise to move in within 60 days and to live in the house for at least a year, unless the lender agrees in writing or circumstances beyond the borrower's control intervene ([Form 3008, a copy](https://www.tenaco.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/09/Fannie-Mae-Form-3008-Delaware-Mortgage-Revised-08-22.pdf)). FHA loans use the same promise and add a hardship exception ([HUD](https://www.hud.gov/sites/dfiles/SFH/documents/modelfwd_mtg_2023.pdf)). The promise runs for one year from the day the owner moves in. A separate clause covers what the borrower told the lender about living there when applying.

**The next mortgage.** An owner buying another home has to qualify while still owing on the first. Under Fannie Mae's rules, as restated in September 2026, the lender sets the old house's rent from an appraisal or from at least three comparable rentals, and may not use a lease. It counts 75% of that rent against the old house's full monthly payment. The rent can cancel that payment but cannot be added to the owner's income; a shortfall counts as debt. An owner with less than a year's experience as a landlord needs six months of the old house's payments in reserve ([Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.8-05/rental-income-non-subject-property-departing-residence)). If the market rent is $2,400, the lender counts $1,800.

**Insurance.** A standard homeowner's policy may not cover losses while the house is rented out. Insurers sell a separate landlord policy, which the Insurance Information Institute says generally costs about 25% more ([Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/coverage-for-renting-out-your-home)). [What homeowner's insurance costs](what-homeowners-insurance-costs.html) covers the premium itself.

**Property tax.** Some tax breaks belong to owners who live in the house. California's $7,000 homeowners' exemption requires that the owner occupy the home as a principal residence on January 1 ([California Board of Equalization](https://aws.boe.ca.gov/pdf/pub800-6.pdf)). Florida treats renting out all or nearly all of a homestead as abandoning it. A rental that begins after January 1 does not cost that year's exemption unless the home is rented more than 30 days a year two years running, and service members and some federal employees posted elsewhere are exempt ([Florida Statutes, s. 196.061](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0100-0199/0196/Sections/0196.061.html)).

**Income tax while it is rented.** Rent is income, reported on Schedule E. Mortgage interest, property tax, insurance and repairs come off it, and so does depreciation: the cost of the building, not the land, spread over 27.5 years. For a former home the starting figure is the lower of what the owner has in the house and what it was worth on the day it became a rental. If the result is a loss, up to $25,000 of it can offset other income for an owner who takes an active part, an allowance that shrinks once income passes $100,000 and is gone at $150,000 ([IRS Publication 527](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p527)).

**Tax when it is finally sold.** A seller can keep up to $250,000 of gain on a main home free of federal tax, or $500,000 on a joint return, after owning and living in it for 24 months of the five years before the sale ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc701)). An owner who lived in the house for at least two years before moving out therefore has about three years to sell and still pass that test, by our arithmetic. The depreciation is the exception: gain equal to the depreciation allowed after May 6, 1997, cannot be excluded ([IRS Publication 523](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p523)) and is taxed at up to 25% ([IRS](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409)). On a building worth $300,000, three full years of depreciation is about $32,700, and the tax on it up to about $8,200.

## How to read the numbers

**"2.3%" is a share of rental listings on one website.** It is not 2.3% of homeowners. Zillow's count covers homes listed on Zillow, usually peaks in November, and catches only owners who tried to sell first.

**Two lock-in estimates measure different things.** The FHFA's 1.33 million is sales that didn't happen. Federal Reserve economists estimate that lock-in explains 44% of the drop in moves by mortgage holders from 2021 to 2022, mostly moves within the same area ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/feds/files/2024088r1pap.pdf)). So a little over half of that drop is not explained by lock-in.

**"The rent covers the mortgage" is not profit.** The mortgage payment leaves out vacancy, repairs, the landlord's insurance and any lost tax break. Fannie Mae's own rule counts three-quarters of the rent.

## Where the analogy breaks

A parking space doesn't need a roof, and nobody calls its owner at midnight. The lease in the story has one fixed price, where a house's taxes and insurance keep moving after the loan is fixed. And a space is worth what it was. A house can gain or lose value while someone else lives in it.

## What people argue about

**Whether owners who rent instead of selling help the market.** One side: each one adds a house to the rental supply, and Zillow says these owners are rarely being forced to sell ([Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/research/accidental-landlords/)). It reported rents up 1.9% in February 2026 from a year earlier as those houses and new apartments arrived ([Mortgage Professional America](https://www.mpamag.com/us/mortgage-industry/market-updates/unable-or-unwilling-to-sell-their-home-how-homeowners-are-becoming-accidental-landlords/569185)). The other: each one is a house not for sale. No study cited here measures that group alone. The nearest estimates cover lock-in as a whole: the FHFA researchers put its effect on prices at 5.7%, and the Federal Reserve economists at 8%, an effect they say came entirely from a market that was already tight; in a balanced one, as in 2019, the same shock would have done little ([Federal Reserve, summary](https://fedinprint.org/item/fedgfe/99222/100097)).

**Whether a mortgage should move with its owner.** One side: Bill Pulte, the director of the Federal Housing Finance Agency, said in November 2025, "We are actively evaluating portable mortgages," loans an owner could carry, rate and all, to the next house. People in the industry who favor the idea say the borrower keeps the original terms and that such loans are already common in Canada, Australia and Britain ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/fhfa-assumable-portable-mortgages/)). The other: Jake Krimmel, a senior economist at Realtor.com and a co-author of the Federal Reserve paper above, argues that only owners who already hold cheap loans would gain, and that investors in mortgage bonds would demand more for loans that last longer, raising rates ([Fox Business](https://www.foxbusiness.com/economy/portable-mortgages-explained-what-how-work)). He also argues that an owner carrying a 3% loan would be bidding with cheap money against buyers paying today's rate, which would push prices up ([The National News Desk](https://cnycentral.com/news/nation-world/trump-administration-tries-to-break-through-lock-in-effect-with-portable-mortgages-housing-market-affordability-tariffs)).

**Whether the tax on a sale keeps homes off the market.** One side: the $250,000 and $500,000 limits have not changed since 1997, and a bill before Congress, the More Homes on the Market Act, would double them and tie them to inflation; supporters say the tax discourages long-time owners from selling ([Third Way](https://www.thirdway.org/memo/how-to-get-more-homes-on-the-market)). The other: John Ricco of the Budget Lab at Yale calls relief on capital gains almost certain to favor the well-off, and puts the cost of doubling the limits at $76 billion over 10 years ([National Mortgage News](https://www.nationalmortgagenews.com/articles/richest-to-profit-in-trumps-capital-gain-tax-ideas)).
