## What reaches you, in order

1. **The Fed funds rate**, the same day, through the prime rate every line of credit and most small-business loans are priced from.
2. **Jobless claims and the unemployment rate**, as the price and availability of the next hire.
3. **Inflation**, from both directions: what your suppliers charge you and what your customers will pay.
4. **GDP and the jobs report**, as the demand side, a quarter or two before you feel it.
5. **The Fed's cycle**, when a term loan comes up for renewal.

The 10-year and mortgage rates reach you if you own your building or your customers are buying houses. Housing starts reach you if you're in the trades. The rest is this page.

## The Fed, the same day

The prime rate is the Fed funds target plus three points, and banks move it the afternoon the Fed does. Lines of credit, SBA 7(a) loans, equipment loans and most business credit cards are priced as prime plus a margin, so a quarter-point Fed move is a quarter-point move in your interest cost within a statement. Prime went from 3.25% in early 2022 to 8.5% by mid-2023, the fastest rise since 1980, and it stands near 7% in late 2026.

This is the sharpest difference between you and the large companies you compete with. They borrowed in the bond market in 2020–21 at 2–3% for five or ten years and are still paying it; you float on prime. The Fed's hikes reached small firms in months and large firms, in many cases, not yet. [The Fed funds rate](what-is-the-fed-funds-rate.html) has the full order of arrival; you are at the front of it.

## Claims and unemployment: the next hire

Low jobless claims mean nobody is being laid off, which means nobody is on the market. In 2022, with claims near 200,000 and unemployment at 3.5%, small firms reported their hardest hiring conditions on record and raised starting pay to compete with the large employers who could absorb it. By 2025–26 the market had loosened from the hiring side without claims rising: fewer openings, more applicants per posting, starting-pay pressure easing. [Jobless claims](what-are-jobless-claims.html) is the weekly read on that, and the unemployment rate is the monthly one. When claims start rising, hiring gets easier and your customers' confidence gets worse, in that order.

## Inflation, from both sides

Your costs arrive first. Materials, freight, insurance, rent and wages all rose in 2021–23, and they rose before you could reprice, which is where small-business margins went in 2022. Then the question of how much you can pass through, which depends on whether your customers' pay kept up: it did, with a lag, through 2025, and it has fallen behind prices again in 2026 as energy rose. The rent line and the wage line on [the inflation chart](../chart.html?id=cpi&range=5y&ov=cpi_rent,wages,real_wages) are your two biggest costs and your customers' budget, on the same axis.

Tariffs land here too, as a cost on imported inputs. The broad tariffs of 2025 were struck down in February 2026 and partly replaced under other authorities, so the tariff on a given import has changed several times in two years, and the arguments below take that up.

## GDP and jobs: the demand side

Consumer spending is two-thirds of GDP, and most of it goes through businesses your size. A quarter of 2% growth means your customers, in aggregate, are spending a little more than a year ago; a quarter near zero means they aren't, and it shows up in your receipts a quarter or two before the GDP release confirms it. The jobs report is the other side: steady hiring means paychecks to spend and competition for your staff, and the number gets revised, so [the jobs report](what-is-the-jobs-report.html) page's three-month average is the better read.

## The cycle, when the loan renews

A five-year term loan on a building or major equipment taken in 2021 at 4% came up for renewal in 2026 at 8% or more, and commercial real estate loans have been renewing on those terms since 2023. The Fed cycle lane on the chart shows where in the sequence a renewal falls: the first moves of a cutting cycle lower prime immediately but reach long-term loan pricing through the 10-year, which can lag or go the other way, as in late 2024. A renewal that lands in a hiking cycle is a different loan from one that lands in a cutting cycle, whatever the business did in between.

## Where you come out ahead

You can reprice. A worker waits a year for a raise and a retiree waits for January; a business changes the sign on the door. In 2021–23 that is how small firms survived a 20% rise in costs. You can also hire faster than a large employer when the market loosens, and you are the first to feel a cutting cycle through prime. Against that: you float on prime while your biggest competitors locked in 2021 rates, your margins absorb cost increases before prices can catch up, and a loan renewal can land on the wrong side of a Fed cycle through no fault of the business.

## What people argue about

**Whether rates or demand hurt small firms more.** One side: the 2022–23 hikes hit small business hardest because small firms borrow short and floating, and the gap between small and large firms' borrowing costs is the widest in decades. The other: surveys through 2023–26 put inflation, labor quality and taxes above interest rates as the top problem, and firms that don't borrow were untouched by the hikes. Both read the same surveys; the difference is which firms they count.

**Tariffs.** For a domestic maker competing with imports, a tariff is a price floor and a reason to hire. For an importer, a retailer or a manufacturer using imported parts, it is a cost increase with no supplier to negotiate with, and the 2025–26 changes added uncertainty to the cost. Both kinds of firm exist in every town. The chart marks the events; which side of them a business is on decides what the event meant.

**Labor costs and the minimum wage.** Rising state minimums and the 2021–23 wage compression raised pay fastest at the bottom, where most small-business jobs are. One side says that priced out hiring and shortened hours; the other says higher pay cut turnover, which is the largest hidden cost in a small firm, and that the market raised the floor before any law did. The evidence from state increases is mixed enough to support both, within limits that both sides also argue about.
