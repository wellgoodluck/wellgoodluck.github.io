## Short version

When you co-sign a loan, a credit card or a lease, you promise to pay the whole debt if the other person doesn't. The lender can come straight to you, a co-signed loan or card shows up on your credit report as your debt, and a single missed payment by the borrower can hurt your credit score. People co-sign out of love or friendship, often for a first car, a first apartment or a private student loan. It can help someone get started, but it's one of the few money decisions that can cost you a debt you never used, and a relationship too.

## The trap in one sentence

Co-signing feels like a favor, but the lender treats it as your loan.

## How it works

**You're not a backup; you're a borrower.** Federal rules require auto dealers, finance companies and other nonbank lenders to hand co-signers a separate notice before they sign, and most banks and credit unions use the same notice. (It isn't required for home-purchase loans or apartment leases.) It says, in part: "You are being asked to guarantee this debt. Think carefully before you do." It warns that you may have to pay the full amount, plus late fees and collection costs, and that "the creditor can collect this debt from you without first trying to collect from the borrower."

**It's on your credit report.** The loan appears on your report as if you'd taken it out. Late payments by the borrower show up as your late payments. The monthly payment usually counts as your debt when you apply for your own car loan, apartment or mortgage. Some mortgage lenders will leave it out if you can prove the borrower has made the last 12 payments on time themselves. A lease guarantee usually shows up on your report only if unpaid rent is sent to collections.

**You may not hear about trouble.** The lender usually has no duty to tell you when the borrower misses a payment. Some co-signers find out from a collection call or a drop in their score.

**Getting off is hard.** Some loans, especially private student loans, advertise "co-signer release" after a set number of on-time payments. In 2015, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, using data from large private student lenders, found that about 90% of borrowers who applied for co-signer release were turned down.

**Where it comes up:**

- **Car loans** for a friend, partner or family member with thin or damaged credit.
- **Apartments.** Landlords often ask young renters for a guarantor, usually a parent, who owes the rent if the tenant doesn't pay.
- **Private student loans.** Nearly all private loans to undergraduates have a co-signer. With new federal limits that took effect July 1, 2026 (Grad PLUS loans closed to new borrowers, and Parent PLUS loans capped at $20,000 a year and $65,000 per student), more families may turn to private loans (see [borrowing for school](student-loans.html)).
- **Credit cards**, though most large card issuers no longer allow co-signers; adding someone as an authorized user is different and doesn't make them liable.

## A worked example

You co-sign a friend's $20,000 car loan at 9% for five years. The payment is $415 a month.

## How to avoid it

**Only co-sign for an amount you could afford to pay yourself.** That's the honest test. If you couldn't cover the payments for months, you can't really afford to co-sign.

**Get online access to the account.** Ask the borrower to let you see the account, or ask the lender for copies of statements, so you know right away if a payment is missed.

**Get it in writing.** Keep a copy of the loan or lease and the co-signer notice. Know the total amount, the length and the late fees.

**Ask about release before you sign.** If the lender offers co-signer release, ask for the exact requirements in writing, and know they're often hard to meet.

**If you need a co-signer, look at the alternatives first.** A smaller loan, a cheaper car, a secured credit card that builds credit with your own deposit, or a roommate to share rent can all reduce the need. For an apartment, some landlords accept a larger deposit (where state law allows; California caps most deposits at one month's rent), or a guarantor company that charges a fee, often close to a month's rent, that you don't get back.

**If someone co-signs for you, treat it as their money on the line.** Pay on time, tell them early if you're going to have trouble, and refinance in your own name as soon as your credit allows.

## The bigger picture

A 2016 survey for CreditCards.com of about 400 co-signers found that nearly four in ten had to pay some or all of the debt, 28% saw their credit score drop, and about one in four said the relationship was damaged.

Some protections have been added over the years. Since 1985, the FTC's Credit Practices Rule has required the co-signer notice. A 2018 law, for private student loans signed since late November 2018, bars lenders from declaring the loan in default or demanding full payment just because a co-signer dies or goes bankrupt, a practice the CFPB had criticized, and requires them to release the co-signer if the student borrower dies.

The current argument is mostly about private student loans and co-signer release, as new federal borrowing limits push more students toward private lenders.

**Lenders and their supporters** argue that co-signers make credit possible for students and young adults who have no credit history, at lower rates than they could get alone; that strict underwriting with a co-signer keeps default rates low; and that release requires the borrower to qualify on their own, the same test as a new loan, which keeps the loans affordable for everyone.

**Consumer advocates** argue that co-signers, often parents and grandparents near retirement, take on risks that are poorly explained; that co-signer release is advertised more than it's granted; and that a 2026 report from Protect Borrowers (formerly the Student Borrower Protection Center) and The Century Foundation estimated that more than 40% of Americans fall below the credit and income cutoffs most traditional private student lenders use, so many families have no one who could qualify, even as a co-signer.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 9, 2026.
