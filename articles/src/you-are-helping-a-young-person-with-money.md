## What reaches them, in order

Money trouble rarely starts with one big mistake. It starts with a default setting, a small fee or an easy payment, usually the first time a young person handles something alone. The traps arrive in a fairly predictable order, and each has a short page in the [Money Traps](money-traps.html) series.

1. **A first bank account**, often in the teens. The traps: [overdraft fees](overdraft-fees.html), which can turn a $6 purchase into a $35 charge; [payment app scams](payment-app-scams.html), where money sent by Venmo or Zelle is usually gone; and [subscriptions](subscription-creep.html) that renew on their own.
2. **A first paycheck.** From a regular job, taxes come out automatically. From delivery, rideshare or freelance apps, [nothing is withheld](gig-work-taxes.html), and the bill arrives in April.
3. **A first credit card**, at 18 (before 21, only with their own income) or earlier as an authorized user on yours. The traps: [what goes into a credit score](how-credit-scores-work.html), [paying only the minimum](credit-card-minimum-payments.html), and [buy now, pay later](buy-now-pay-later.html) plans that stack up.
4. **A first car.** A [seven-year loan](long-car-loans.html) lowers the payment and raises the total.
5. **A first apartment**, with the costs of furnishing it and the temptation of [rent-to-own](rent-to-own.html) or a [payday loan](payday-loans-and-paycheck-apps.html) when money runs short. This is also when someone may ask you to [co-sign](co-signing.html) a lease or a loan.
6. **College**, and the [student loan choices](student-loans.html) that follow them for decades.
7. **The feed, all along:** [investing tips from strangers](finfluencers-and-investment-scams.html), [ads that sell the worry](ads-that-sell-the-worry.html), and [sports betting and prediction apps](sports-betting-and-prediction-apps.html), some of which, including the largest, Kalshi, accept users at 18 even in states where the sportsbook age is 21.

## Three things you can set up early

**Freeze a child's credit.** Identity thieves use children's Social Security numbers because no one checks a child's credit for years. A parent or guardian can freeze the credit file of a child under 16 for free at each of the three credit bureaus, and it stays frozen until you lift it. At 16 and 17, the young person can manage the freeze themselves.

**Consider adding them as an authorized user.** If you have a card you pay on time, adding a young person as an authorized user can start a credit history in their name before they could get a card of their own. They aren't responsible for the debt; you are. Your late payments can show up on their credit reports too, though Experian leaves them off. Issuers set their own age rules, and not every issuer reports authorized users to all three bureaus. You don't have to hand them the card for it to count.

**If they have a job, a retirement account can start now.** There's no minimum age for an IRA, only a requirement to have earned income (the IRS calls it taxable compensation). A teenager who earns $2,000 from a summer job can put up to $2,000 into a Roth IRA, usually opened by a parent as custodian. Decades of growth is the one advantage young people have that no one can buy later (see [the employer match](employer-retirement-match.html)).

Four free tools on this site run the same math as the Money Traps pages on your own numbers. Nothing typed into them is saved or sent anywhere.

- [How Long Will It Take to Pay Off a Card?](/tools/card-payoff.html): pull up a real statement and compare paying the minimum with paying a fixed amount.

## For grandparents, and anyone being asked for help

The traps reach the other end of life too, often through the young people in it. In the "grandparent scam," a caller who sounds like a grandchild in trouble, or a "lawyer" or "officer" calling for one, asks for money right away, in cash (sometimes collected at your door by a "courier"), gift cards, a wire transfer, crypto or a payment app, and asks you to keep it secret. A related scam impersonates your bank and tells you to move your money "to keep it safe." The FTC reported in 2025 that among adults 60 and older, losses of more than $100,000 to scammers posing as businesses or government agencies rose eight-fold in four years, from $55 million in 2020 to $445 million in 2024.

Two habits make these calls much harder to pull off:

- **Hang up and call back** on a number you already know: the grandchild's own phone, a parent's, or the number on the back of your bank card.
- **Agree on a family code word** that a real caller in trouble would know and a stranger wouldn't.

When a grandchild asks you to co-sign a loan or a lease, read [what co-signing really means](co-signing.html) first. The lender can come to you for the whole debt.

## What people argue about

**Whether personal finance classes work.** As of April 2026, 30 states had adopted a requirement that high school students take a standalone personal finance course before graduating; once all take effect, by the class of 2031, about three in four public high school students will be covered, according to Next Gen Personal Finance, which tracks the laws and campaigns for them.

**Supporters** point to studies of state requirements. In Georgia, Idaho and Texas, young adults who graduated after personal finance courses became required had higher credit scores and fewer serious late payments than similar young adults elsewhere. A New York Fed study found that financial literacy and math requirements each modestly reduced late payments and collections, though economics requirements did not. Supporters also cite a 2022 review of 76 randomized studies that found financial education improves both knowledge and behavior, with effects comparable to other kinds of education.

**Skeptics** point to a 2014 review of 201 studies that found education explained about 0.1% of the differences in financial behavior, with effects that faded to almost nothing within about 20 months, and argue that lessons work best at the moment of a decision, like signing a car loan, rather than years before it. Some, such as the law professor Lauren Willis, argue that the stronger protection is rules on the products themselves, such as fee limits and clearer disclosures, rather than expecting a 17-year-old to outsmart them, and that a required course takes time from other subjects.

Whatever the answer on classes, the research on timing points to the kind of help a family can give: talking through a real decision, with real numbers, when it comes up.
