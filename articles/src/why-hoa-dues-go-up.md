## In one sentence

Your dues are your share of what the association expects to spend this year to run, insure and maintain what the owners hold in common, plus a deposit toward replacing those things when they wear out.

## The analogy

A small private town. It has an elected council (the board), a yearly budget, a tax bill (the dues), and things to keep up: streets, gates, a pool, a roof. Like any town, it can put money aside for the roof it knows it will need, or wait and send everyone a bill when the roof gives out. What it can't do is make the roof cost less by not saving for it.

Figures are as of November 2026.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

About 23.2 million of the country's 87.5 million owner households paid a condo or homeowners' association fee in 2025, and the median fee was $137 a month. Households with a mortgage paid a median of $121; households without one paid $186 ([Census Bureau, table B25143](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2025.B25143)). For 2024 the Census Bureau found the same pattern, or no measurable difference, in every state ([Census Bureau, America Counts](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2025/09/condo-hoa-fees.html)), and the site's comparison of the 2025 state figures, using their margins of error, finds the same.

Where you live matters. Nevada (55%) and Arizona (49%) had the highest shares of owners paying a fee, followed by Florida (47%) and Delaware (45%), whose difference is within the survey's margin of error. About 5.8 million households paid less than $50 a month, and about 3.4 million paid $500 or more ([Census Bureau, table B25142](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2025.B25142)).

Listings show the trend. Realtor.com found that 43.6% of homes listed for sale in 2025 carried an association fee, up from 34.3% in 2019, including 84.8% of condos and townhomes and 33.4% of single-family houses. The median listed fee was $135 in 2025, against $125 in 2024 and $108 in 2019 ([Realtor.com, January 27, 2026](https://finviz.com/news/287939/nearly-44-of-us-homes-for-sale-now-carry-hoa-fees-as-dues-continue-to-climb-realtorcom-finds); [The MortgagePoint](https://themortgagepoint.com/2026/01/27/homeowners-associations-continue-to-increase-in-numbers-price-in-2025/)). By Realtor.com's figures as the Wall Street Journal reported them, the median condo fee reached $420 in 2025, up 29% from 2019, and the median single-family association fee $63, up 26% ([PYMNTS, on the Journal's report](https://www.pymnts.com/?p=3641498)).

Consumer prices rose about 26% over the same years ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/regions/mid-atlantic/data/consumerpriceindexhistorical_us_table.htm)). In 2025 dollars, 2019's $108 is about $136, so after inflation the typical listed fee was flat, the single-family median too, and the condo median rose about 2%. On listings, the typical fee has kept pace with prices rather than run ahead of them. But the typical fee is an average of very different places: a new subdivision with a gate and a pond, and a 40-year-old tower with elevators and a roof coming due. And a median monthly fee leaves out special assessments altogether.

## How to read the numbers

**"Up 25%" and "up 50%" can both be right.** Realtor.com measures the fee posted on homes for sale, a mix that changes every year. Vantaca, a company that sells software to associations, measured the budgets of more than 3,000 associations that use it and found assessments up 50.5% from 2021 to 2025: condo assessments up 96%, single-family associations up 49% ([ConsumerAffairs, on Vantaca's 2026 report](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/heres-why-hoa-fees-are-going-up-so-much-110426.html)). Different years, different samples, and neither is a survey of every association.

**Take out inflation and the budget figures shrink, but most stay well ahead of prices.** Consumer prices rose about 19% from 2021 to 2025 ([BLS](https://www.bls.gov/regions/mid-atlantic/data/consumerpriceindexhistorical_us_table.htm)). Here are Vantaca's figures with that taken out:

**Special assessments sit outside the monthly figure.** Vantaca put the 2025 average at $1,450 per condo and $514 per single-family home (same report).

## What pushes dues up

**Insurance.** An association insures what the owners share, and in a condo that is the building itself. In an April 2025 survey by the Foundation for Community Association Research, the research foundation of the Community Associations Institute (whose members are association managers, board members and the businesses that serve them), 93% of associations said their premium rose at their last renewal and 47% had faced a cancellation or nonrenewal. To pay for it, 71% raised regular dues, 50% drew on operating funds, 31% levied special assessments and 21% dipped into reserves ([Foundation for Community Association Research, 2026 report](https://foundation.caionline.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/Hanke2026FinalReportClimateRiskResilience.pdf)). In Vantaca's data, association insurance spending rose 75% from 2021 to 2025, and the average master-policy premium for condo buildings went from $53 to $105 per unit ([ConsumerAffairs](https://www.consumeraffairs.com/news/heres-why-hoa-fees-are-going-up-so-much-110426.html); [CNBC Select](https://www.cnbc.com/select/hoa-fees-are-rising-heres-how-to-save/)). The forces behind it are the same ones driving up premiums on single houses: more damage, dearer rebuilding and dearer reinsurance.

**Utilities.** Water for common landscaping, power for pool pumps, gates and lights. Up 63% from 2021 to 2025 in Vantaca's data.

**Labor and upkeep.** Landscaping, pool service, security and management are mostly wages. Vantaca put maintenance spending up 26% over the same years.

**Savings for repairs.** The reserve fund is the savings account for roofs, paving and pool resurfacing. Vantaca found reserve contributions up 44%. Lenders are pushing that higher. Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the government-sponsored companies that buy mortgages from lenders, are raising the minimum share of a condo association's budget that must go into replacement reserves from 10% to 15%, required for loan applications from January 4, 2027 ([Newrez, comparison chart](https://www.newrezwholesale.com/media/y45co2kf/new_guidelines_comparison_chart.pdf); [Orrick](https://infobytes.orrick.com/2026-03-27/fannie-mae-and-freddie-mac-revise-homeowners-insurance-and-condominium-requirements/)). An association that budgets less can still qualify with a reserve study done within the past three years by an independent specialist, if its budget includes the highest reserve contribution the study recommends ([Fannie Mae, Selling Guide B4-2.2-02](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-2.2-02/full-review-process); [Fannie Mae, condo FAQs](https://singlefamily.fanniemae.com/media/document/pdf/faqs-condo-and-property-insurance)). Fannie Mae says the majority of projects already meet the new requirement, and estimates that going from 10% to 15% adds $13 to $14 per unit a month on average (same FAQs). Since August 3, 2026, all but the smallest condo projects get a full review of their budgets, reserves and delinquencies ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/ny-condo-financing-overhaul/); Orrick). A project that fails the standards becomes "non-warrantable": Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac won't buy mortgages on its units, which rules out most conventional loans and shrinks the pool of buyers for every owner in the building ([Chicago Cooperator](https://chicago.cooperatornews.com/article/fannie-mae-raises-the-bar-for-reserve-funding); [National Mortgage News](https://www.nationalmortgagenews.com/news/non-qm-lending-grows-in-condo-space)). Buyers can still look for a lender that keeps the loan itself or sells it to private investors, usually at a higher rate and with a bigger down payment ([Hudson Gateway Association of Realtors, September 3, 2026](https://hgar.com/new-fannie-mae-freddie-mac-project-standards-require-full-review-what-this-means-for-new-york-condominium-and-cooperative-transactions); National Mortgage News). The same March announcement lets condo master policies insure roofs at actual cash value, a depreciated figure, rather than full replacement cost (Orrick).

**Old buildings and new safety laws.** After the Champlain Towers South collapse in Surfside in 2021 killed 98 people, Florida required condo buildings three or more stories tall to have milestone inspections by the end of the year a building turns 30, and every 10 years after; to have engineers' structural reserve studies; and to fully fund reserves for the roof, structure, plumbing, electrical systems and foundation, with no more votes to waive them. A 72-unit building from 1981 in Panama City Beach faced $1.5 million in repairs, about $20,000 a unit. In a Lake Worth complex, one owner's mother, counting on her unit for retirement, saw her monthly payments roughly triple and couldn't sell, with more than 70 units already listed there ([Newsweek, March 19, 2025](https://www.newsweek.com/end-south-florida-dream-2039443)). A 2025 law, in effect from July 1, 2025, gave owners some room, including a narrow, temporary exception to the no-waiver rule. The rules now cover buildings three or more habitable stories tall, and the deadline for the reserve studies moved to the end of 2025. For budgets adopted through 2028, an association that has had its milestone inspection in the past two years can, with the approval of a majority of all the owners' votes (not just those cast), pause or reduce reserve contributions for up to two consecutive budget years to pay for the repairs the inspection found; it must get a structural reserve study before contributions resume. Owners can also approve, by the same majority of all votes, paying for the required reserves with a special assessment, a loan or a line of credit ([Florida Senate, 2025 summary of HB 913](https://flsenate.gov/Committees/BillSummaries/2025/html/913); [Florida Statutes § 718.112](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0700-0799/0718/Sections/0718.112.html)).

California followed this fall, on a slower clock. AB 2050, signed September 29, 2026, takes effect January 1, 2027, and its funding rules start January 1, 2032. From then on, an association must put in at least the yearly amount its reserve study says will keep the reserve balance above zero for 30 years. If the projection shows a shortfall, it must move at least 15% of its gross annual budget into reserves each year until it doesn't. If dues can't cover it, the association levies a reserve special assessment, at most once every nine years; the first 5% of the budget needs no vote, and anything more needs the owners' approval. Until now, California required associations to study their reserves but not to fund them ([Senate Housing Committee analysis](https://billtexts.s3.amazonaws.com/ca/ca-analysishttps-leginfo-legislature-ca-gov-faces-billAnalysisClient-xhtml-bill-id-202520260AB2050-ca-analysis-399809.pdf); [ManageCasa](https://managecasa.com/articles/california-ab-2050-what-the-new-hoa-reserve-law-means-for-boards)).

## What the reserve money earns

Between roofs, the reserve fund sits in an account, and what it earns stays in the fund. For years that was close to nothing. The three-month Treasury bill, the usual benchmark for the safest short-term money, paid an average of 0.04% in 2021. As the Federal Reserve [raised rates](what-is-the-fed-funds-rate.html) it climbed to 5.07% in 2023, and it was 4.05% in October 2026 ([FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TB3MS)). California's new funding rules will make reserve balances larger from 2032, so what they earn will matter more.

Take a hypothetical association of 100 units with $1 million in reserves. At 2021's bill rate it would have earned about $400 for the year. At October 2026's, about $40,500: roughly $405 per unit, or $34 a month that owners don't have to put in. Interest is taxable income for an association, so it keeps somewhat less ([Davis-Stirling.com](https://www.davis-stirling.com/HOME/H/HOA-Tax-Returns)).

{{figure kind=bars title="Three-month Treasury bill rate, yearly average" rows="2021=0.04;2022=2.02;2023=5.07;2024=4.97;2025=4.07;2026 (Jan–Oct)=3.71" fmt="{v}%" caption="Monthly rates averaged by year. Discount basis." source="[FRED, TB3MS](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/TB3MS)"}}

Where the money sits matters as much as the rate. Bank deposit rates haven't followed Treasury rates closely: the FDIC's national average for a 12-month CD under $100,000 was 1.81% in November 2026, less than half the bill rate ([FRED, FDIC national rates](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/NDR12MCD)). That average spans banks that pay much more and much less, so it shows how wide the gap can be, not what any association earns. California limits the choices. Money a management company handles for an association has to sit in insured accounts that protect the principal, and it can't go into stocks or other high-risk investments ([Civil Code § 5380](https://california.public.law/codes/civil_code_section_5380)). Money the board places itself falls under a general duty of prudent fiscal management ([Davis-Stirling.com, citing § 5515](https://www.davis-stirling.com/HOME/I/Investing-Reserve-Funds)).

Interest has limits, too. It's earned only on the cash in the account, while inflation raises the cost of every roof and road the fund will eventually pay for. Association Reserves, a large reserve-study firm (it sells the studies), calls the belief that interest can offset inflation a persistent misunderstanding among boards ([Association Reserves](https://www.reservestudy.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Inflation-and-Reserves-Planning-Adjusting-to-Rising-Costs-RMN.pdf)). And the rate is a guess built into the funding plan. A plan that assumes the reserves earn 4% asks owners for less than one that assumes 2%, so if rates fall and stay down, contributions have to rise to stay on schedule. California owners can see the guess: the Assessment and Reserve Funding Disclosure Summary that comes with every budget report states the assumed long-term interest rate on reserves and the assumed inflation rate ([Civil Code § 5570](https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/civil-code/civ-sect-5570/)).

## Who decides, and how much say owners have

The board adopts the budget, the owners elect the board, and state law and the association's own documents set the limits. The rules vary by state. In 2025 the Community Associations Institute counted 31 states plus the District of Columbia with laws on collecting assessments, raising them, or both ([CAI](https://www.caionline.org/getmedia/9020fbf0-e30f-4f67-adc9-5894e12b89e0/AssessmentStatutes2025.pdf)). Two examples:

- **California.** Without a vote of the owners, a board may raise regular dues by up to 20% over the year before and levy special assessments totaling up to 5% of the year's budgeted expenses. Beyond either line, it needs approval from a majority of a quorum, and a quorum means more than half of all members ([Senate Judiciary Committee analysis](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/media/2146); [Bay Legal](https://baylegal.com/?p=34929)). The budget report must go out 30 to 90 days before the fiscal year ends, and notice of an increase 30 to 60 days before it's due. A board that misses the budget report can't raise regular dues that year without a vote ([Davis-Stirling.com](https://www.davis-stirling.com/HOME/A/Annual-Budgets)). There are exceptions for emergencies, such as a court order or a threat to health or safety ([Bay Legal](https://baylegal.com/?p=22699)).
- **Arizona.** A planned community can't raise its regular assessment more than 20% over the year before without approval from a majority of the members, unless its own documents set a lower limit ([A.R.S. § 33-1803](https://www.hopb.co/arizona-planned-communities-act-ars-section-33-1801)).

## Where the analogy breaks

A town spreads a big repair across thousands of taxpayers. A 40-unit association spreads it across 40. And unlike a town's residents, an association's members also pay the real town's taxes.

## What people argue about

**Cap the increases, or let boards keep up with costs.** California's SB 1007 began in February as a cap at the rate of inflation and was amended in May to require owners' approval for any regular increase over 8%. It would also have required budgets to show what the management company is paid ([findHOAlaw](https://findhoalaw.com/sb-1007-assessments/); [CAI](https://www.caionline.org/getattachment/bfddc098-94fe-4768-a93b-16ddb6c79263/key-campaign-CA-SB-1007-overview-1pager.pdf)). One side: its supporters said the 20% ceiling isn't tied to any measure of costs and can push out owners whose incomes don't keep up, and that a lower cap would make dues predictable ([Senate floor analysis](https://billtexts.s3.amazonaws.com/ca/ca-analysishttps-leginfo-legislature-ca-gov-faces-billAnalysisClient-xhtml-bill-id-202520260SB1007-ca-analysis-399327.pdf)). The other side: insurance, roofs and elevators don't follow the price of groceries. The president of a Long Beach condo association wrote that her board had passed "two consecutive 20% assessment increases" to keep up with insurance and inflation, increases an inflation cap would have barred ([Capitol Weekly, May 19, 2026](https://capitolweekly.net/protect-homeowners-from-special-assessments-or-guarantee-them/)). The Community Associations Institute's California committee campaigned against the bill, and it was pulled before its Assembly committee hearing in June (CAI). It didn't pass.

**Save now or pay later.** The case for laws like AB 2050 and the lenders' 15% rule: steady saving avoids the large, sudden special assessments Florida owners are facing, and keeps condos eligible for ordinary mortgages. The Community Associations Institute's California committee sponsored the bill, and the California Association of Community Managers supported it. The case against: in the Senate Housing Committee's June analysis, the California Association of Realtors, the Center for California Homeowner Association Law, the Consumer Federation of California and Housing and Economic Rights Advocates warned that the catch-up assessments could be very large and strain owners' finances, questioned what required funding would do to affordability, and doubted that keeping a balance above zero is enough to prevent deferred maintenance ([Senate Housing Committee analysis](https://billtexts.s3.amazonaws.com/ca/ca-analysishttps-leginfo-legislature-ca-gov-faces-billAnalysisClient-xhtml-bill-id-202520260AB2050-ca-analysis-399809.pdf)).

**Paying twice.** Association members pay property taxes that maintain public streets, and dues that maintain their own. New Jersey's Municipal Services Act, passed in 1989, requires towns either to provide or to reimburse snow and ice removal, the electricity for street lights, and trash, recycling and leaf collection on the roads of qualifying private communities ([HOPB, statute text](https://www.hopb.co/new-jersey-municipal-services-act-40-67-23-2)). A state appeals court described its purpose as relieving owners of "the burden of paying twice" ([Stonehill Property Owners Association v. Township of Vernon, 1998](https://law.justia.com/cases/new-jersey/appellate-division-published/1998/a5590-96-opn.html)). The other side: private streets come with the development the buyers chose, and they may not be built to a town's standards or open to the public. A town that takes over a private street usually requires it to meet the town's street standards first ([Borough of Longport ordinance, for example](https://ecode360.com/10307433)).
