## Short version

Streaming, music, cloud storage, apps, meal kits, gym memberships: each subscription is small, and each renews automatically. Together they can quietly cost over $1,000 a year, including services you've stopped using. Free trials that turn into paid plans and cancellation processes built to wear you down make it worse. A federal "click to cancel" rule was struck down in 2025, so the main federal protection is an older, narrower law, plus state rules.

## The trap in one sentence

Signing up takes one tap; noticing you're still paying can take a year.

## How it works

Subscriptions are designed around inertia. A few habits work against you:

- **Free trials that convert.** You give a card number for a free week. If you don't cancel, the paid plan starts automatically.
- **Small, regular charges.** A $10.99 charge doesn't stand out on a statement the way a $130 one would, even though they add up to the same thing over a year.
- **Price increases.** Services raise prices on existing subscribers, often with little more than an email.
- **Hard cancellation.** Some companies make you call, click through several "are you sure?" screens, or turn down offers before you can leave.

## How to avoid it

**Do a subscription audit.** Go through two or three months of bank and card statements and list every recurring charge. Most people find at least one surprise.

**Set a reminder the day you start a free trial.** Put the end date on your calendar a couple of days early, or cancel right away; many services let you keep the trial even after canceling.

**Rotate instead of stacking.** Keep one streaming service at a time and switch when you've watched what you wanted.

**Check app-store subscriptions separately.** Subscriptions bought through your phone's app store are managed in your phone's settings, not inside the app.

**Cancel in writing and keep proof.** Screenshot the confirmation. If charges continue after you've canceled, dispute them with your card company.

## The bigger picture

[In October 2024, the Federal Trade Commission finalized](https://www.gibsondunn.com/ftc-restarts-negative-option-rulemaking-after-eighth-circuit-vacatur-enforcement-under-rosca-continues/) a "click to cancel" rule requiring that canceling a subscription be as easy as signing up, with clear disclosures and real consent before charging. In July 2025, a federal appeals court threw out the rule entirely, ruling that the FTC skipped a required step in the rulemaking process. The court didn't rule on whether the idea itself was good. [In March 2026, the FTC started the process over](https://www.cooley.com/news/insight/2026/2026-03-19-ftc-issues-new-advance-notice-of-proposed-rulemaking-on-negative-option-marketing); as of November it had not proposed a new rule.

Meanwhile, [an older federal law still requires online sellers](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/15/8403) to disclose terms clearly, get consent before charging, and offer a simple way to stop recurring charges, and the FTC continues to bring cases under it. [Many states also have their own automatic-renewal laws](https://arnoldporter.com/en/perspectives/advisories/2026/02/ftc-and-state-ags-continue-to-scrutinize-subscription-practices), some with requirements similar to the vacated rule.

**Supporters of a federal rule** argue that one national standard is clearer for everyone, and that companies shouldn't profit from making it hard to leave.

**Opponents** argue that a broad rule would impose costs on every subscription business to stop a minority of bad actors, that existing law already covers deceptive practices, and that retention offers can be legitimately useful to customers.

These rules may change. This page reflects federal law as of November 8, 2026.
