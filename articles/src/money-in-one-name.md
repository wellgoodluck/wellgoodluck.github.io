## Short version

When one partner manages all the money, the other can end up financially invisible: no credit history of their own, no idea which accounts exist, no way to log in. That's rarely anyone's plan; it just happens as couples divide up chores. But money in one person's name can be hard for the other to reach after a death or a breakup, and a person with no credit file can struggle to rent an apartment, finance a car or even get utilities without a deposit. The fixes are simple and work in any relationship: each partner keeps at least one account and one line of credit in their own name, both know where everything is, and both can get to it.

## The trap in one sentence

Sharing a life doesn't mean sharing a credit history, and the partner who doesn't handle the money can find that out at the worst possible moment.

## How it works

**Credit history follows the name on the account.** Your credit file is built from accounts reported in your name. If every card and loan in the household is in one partner's name, the other partner may have a thin file or none at all. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau [estimated](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/data-research/research-reports/technical-correction-and-update-to-the-cfpbs-credit-invisibles-estimate/) that 2.7% of adults had no credit record in 2020, and another 9.8% had a record too thin or too old to produce a score.

**Joint account, authorized user, or neither.** On a joint account, both people are responsible for the debt, and it usually shows up on both credit reports. An authorized user can use the card but isn't responsible for paying, and many issuers report the account on the user's report too, which can help build a history. For married couples, since 1977 [a federal rule](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1002/10/) has required lenders that report to credit bureaus to report accounts that both spouses use or owe in both names. If the account holder dies or closes the card, the authorized user's card stops working.

**What happens to accounts after a death.** A joint account with right of survivorship generally passes straight to the surviving owner. An account in only one person's name usually goes through the estate, unless it names a beneficiary (often called "payable on death" or "transfer on death"). Estates can take months, and the bills don't wait.

**Retirement accounts have their own rules.** Federal law (the Retirement Equity Act of 1984) gives a spouse strong rights to a 401(k) or other workplace plan: the spouse is the beneficiary unless they sign a written waiver in front of a notary or a plan official. That protects the spouse when the worker dies, but most 401(k) plans don't need the spouse's consent for the worker to withdraw money, borrow it or roll it out. The rights also don't follow the money into an IRA. Once a 401(k) is rolled into an IRA, whoever is named on the IRA's beneficiary form generally gets it, with or without a spouse's signature, except where state community property law gives a spouse a share. And unmarried partners have no automatic rights to either; they inherit only if they're named.

**Marriage and state law.** In nine community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin), most income and property acquired during a marriage belong to both spouses, whoever's name is on the account. Elsewhere, the name on the title matters more. Either way, "it's legally half mine" doesn't help much when you can't log in to pay the mortgage.

**How it used to be.** Until the Equal Credit Opportunity Act took effect in 1975, lenders could refuse a married woman credit in her own name or demand her husband's signature, and the history built on a couple's accounts often went to the husband alone. The law now bans discrimination by sex or marital status, but the old pattern, in which one partner holds the credit and the other doesn't, still happens by habit.

## A worked example

Sam and Alex have been together 15 years. Sam handles the money. Here's what their accounts look like, and what Alex can reach the week after Sam dies unexpectedly.

The household's bills, about $4,500 a month, keep coming while most of the money is out of reach. The same accounts set up differently, a payable-on-death beneficiary on the savings and a card in Alex's own name, would have left Alex with $21,200 to work with and a credit history of their own.

## How to avoid it

**Each partner keeps at least one account and one credit card in their own name.** Use the card for something small and pay it off every month. It builds a credit history that belongs to you (see [how credit scores work](how-credit-scores-work.html)).

**Make a one-page list.** Every account, where it is, roughly what's in it, and how to get in. Keep it somewhere safe that both of you know about, or in a password manager you both can open.

**Name beneficiaries.** Bank accounts, brokerage accounts, IRAs, life insurance and retirement plans all have beneficiary forms. Check them every few years and after any marriage, divorce or birth. An old form naming an ex or a parent usually wins over a will.

**Both know the monthly routine.** Which bills come when, which are automatic, and what the household spends. A yearly "money date" to walk through it together is enough.

**Pull both credit reports.** Free every week at [AnnualCreditReport.com](https://www.annualcreditreport.com/). Check that joint accounts appear on both reports and that nothing is there you don't recognize.

**If you're the one handling the money, make yourself replaceable.** The kindest thing you can do for a partner is make sure they won't be lost if you're not there.

## The bigger picture

Two arguments touch on this trap.

**Should a spouse have to sign before retirement money leaves the plan?** Federal protections end once money is withdrawn or rolled into an IRA, and IRAs held [$19.9 trillion in mid-2026](https://www.ici.org/statistical-report/ret_26_q2), against [$10.8 trillion in 401(k)s](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/retirement-assets-hit-512-trillion-as-savers-keep-contributing/268272), much of it rolled over from workplace plans. [A March 2026 report by the Government Accountability Office](https://files.gao.gov/reports/GAO-26-107536/index.html) laid out the arguments. **Retiree and women's groups and legal experts**, along with AARP and the National Women's Law Center in their own policy papers, argue that requiring a spouse's consent would protect spouses, especially in a divorce, and make sure they know what's in the account; the American Academy of Actuaries warned in 2025 that one spouse acting alone can endanger the other's retirement. **Retirement industry groups**, speaking for employers and the companies that run the plans, told GAO that consent rules would add paperwork, staff and costs that could be passed on to workers, hit small employers hardest, and delay people's access to their own money. Some suggested middle paths: notifying a spouse rather than requiring a signature, requiring consent only above a set amount, and exceptions for cases like domestic violence. GAO made no recommendation. Some IRA custodians already ask for a spouse's consent on their own.

**Debts one partner runs up in the other's name.** Sometimes the trap is worse than not knowing: one partner opens credit in the other's name, or pressures them into signing, without real consent. Advocates call this coerced debt. As of mid-2026, [nine states](https://www.nclc.org/vermont-governor-signs-bill-to-address-coerced-debt/) (California, Connecticut, Illinois, Maine, Minnesota, Nevada, New York, Texas and Vermont) had passed laws letting victims contest such debts; Vermont's, the newest, is based largely on a 2024 model law from the National Consumer Law Center. **Supporters** argue that existing identity-theft rules don't cover debts a partner forced someone to sign, and that the damage to a victim's credit can last for years. **Opponents** of [California's 2022 law](https://sjud.senate.ca.gov/sites/sjud.senate.ca.gov/files/sb_975_min_sjud_analysis.pdf) included the California Bankers Association and a business coalition led by the Civil Justice Association of California. They argued that a lender has no way to see what happened inside a relationship, so a claim of coercion is nearly impossible to disprove; that the definition of coerced debt was vague; and that the standard of proof was too easy to meet. Real estate and title groups objected; the bill was amended to exempt debts secured by real property. Vermont's financial regulator [reported in January 2026](https://legislature.vermont.gov/assets/Legislative-Reports/DFR-Act-23-of-2025-Section-18-Report.pdf) that its stakeholders agreed the problem was serious but couldn't agree on a specific law; the legislature passed one anyway that spring, taking effect in July 2028.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 11, 2026.
