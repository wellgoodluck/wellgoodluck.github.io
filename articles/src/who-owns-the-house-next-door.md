## The short version

Most rental houses in America belong to small landlords. Companies that own more than 1,000 houses each did not exist before 2012; by 2022 about 32 of them owned nearly 450,000, which is about 3% of single-family rentals and less than one house in 200 overall. They are not spread evenly. The Government Accountability Office estimated that such investors held 25% of the single-family rentals in metro Atlanta, 21% in Jacksonville and 18% in Charlotte, against 2% in Columbus, Ohio. They began by buying foreclosed houses in bulk after the 2008 crisis, moved on to buying one house at a time, and now mostly build new houses to rent. Their home buying has fallen nearly 70% since 2021, and in the first half of 2026 the largest investors sold more houses than they bought. A federal law enacted in July 2026 bars any investor that controls 350 or more houses from buying more, starting January 7, 2027, with exceptions for houses built to rent and houses bought to renovate. It does not make anyone sell. The research the GAO reviewed found that these investors may have pushed up prices, and in some studies rents, where they bought the most; their effect on homeownership and on tenants is less clear. The largest of them paid $48 million in 2024 to settle federal charges over fees, deposits and evictions, without admitting wrongdoing.

## The analogy

A pinch of salt. Stirred through the whole pot, you can't taste it. Dropped into one bowl, it's all you taste.

Both descriptions of the same pinch are true, and most arguments about investor-owned houses are between someone looking at the pot and someone holding the bowl.

Figures below are as of November 2026.

## The pot: how much they own

About 16% of the country's one-family houses are rentals, and most of those belong to small investors ([Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/will-regulating-large-institutional-investors-actually-make-housing-more-affordable)). In one tabulation of 2018 property records, owners with one or two units held two-thirds of the country's small rental properties, and investors with 1,000 or more held 2% ([Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies](https://jchs.harvard.edu/blog/8-facts-about-investor-activity-single-family-rental-market)).

The large owners are new. As of late 2011 no investor owned 1,000 single-family rentals. By 2015 institutional investors owned an estimated 170,000 to 300,000. By June 2022, 32 investors with more than 1,000 houses each held nearly 450,000, about 3% of single-family rentals, and by the end of that year the five largest held about 300,000 ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)). That is less than half of 1% of all single-family houses ([Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/will-regulating-large-institutional-investors-actually-make-housing-more-affordable)).

These are estimates. The GAO says few public data exist on who owns rental houses; for its own count it had to match owners' names and mailing addresses in property records ([GAO, 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108675)). The most recent national counts by researchers are from 2022.

In metro Atlanta that was 71,832 houses. Within a metro area the houses cluster again, in particular neighborhoods ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)).

A second GAO report, in March 2026, followed six metro areas through 2024 with a stricter definition, owners of 5,000 or more houses. Their share of single-family rentals ranged from 4% in Seattle to 22% in Jacksonville. Measured against all single-family houses, rented or not, it ranged from under 1% to 3% ([GAO, 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108675)).

## Who they are

Two of the largest are publicly traded: Invitation Homes, which the Federal Trade Commission called the nation's largest single-family landlord ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/86362)), and American Homes 4 Rent, which trades as AMH. The others belong to investment firms: Progress Residential (Pretium Partners), FirstKey Homes (founded by Cerberus Capital), the Amherst Group, and Tricon Residential and Home Partners of America (both bought by Blackstone). One count from company filings and property records put the six largest at about 422,000 houses in 2025 ([Wolf Street](https://wolfstreet.com/2025/09/15/the-biggest-single-family-rental-landlords-and-multifamily-landlords-in-the-us-big-shifts-underway/)).

[Where the money goes](where-the-money-goes.html) covers who puts up the money behind firms like these.

## How they got here

**Foreclosures, in bulk.** After the 2007 to 2009 crisis, foreclosed houses were sold at local auctions, many at a time. Most buyers at those auctions paid cash, which favored firms that could raise it. In 2017 Fannie Mae backed a $1 billion loan to Invitation Homes ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)).

**One house at a time.** As foreclosures ran out, the firms bought ordinary listings, using software that priced each house to meet a target return ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)). In the six metro areas the GAO followed, their holdings grew fastest from 2021 to 2023. In Nashville, 35% of the houses they held in 2024 had been bought from people who lived in them, and 15% were newly built ([GAO, 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108675)).

**Building to rent.** Builders started about 63,000 single-family houses meant to be rented in the year through June 2026, just under 7% of single-family starts. The average from 1992 to 2012 was 2.7%. Builders sold perhaps another 3% to 5% of new houses to investors to rent out ([National Association of Home Builders](https://eyeonhousing.org/2026/08/second-quarter-declines-for-single-family-built-to-rent/)).

## What is happening now

The largest investors have cut their buying by more than two-thirds. In the first half of 2026, by Realtor.com's count, investors that have bought more than 350 houses bought about 12,700, which was 0.6% of all home purchases and nearly 70% fewer than in the first half of 2021. Large investors sold more than they bought. Small investors, those with fewer than 10 purchases, made 66% of investor purchases, their highest share in at least 15 years ([National Mortgage Professional](https://nationalmortgageprofessional.com/news/investor-homebuying-slows-small-buyers-claim-biggest-share-15-years); [HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/small-investors-make-gains-as-institutional-homebuyers-retreat/)).

How big "investors" are in total depends on who is counted. Realtor.com puts all investors at 10.7% of purchases in that half year. Cotality, which counts any buyer who owns three or more properties, puts them at 27% of single-family purchases from March through June ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/cotality-investors-single-family/)). By either count, most are small.

## What the 2026 law does

On January 20, 2026, an executive order made it federal policy that large institutional investors should not buy single-family houses that families could otherwise buy, and asked Congress to write that into law ([Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/congress-weighs-sweeping-ban-institutional-investor-ownership-single-family)). Congress did, inside a larger housing bill, the 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, which passed the Senate 85 to 5 and the House 358 to 32. It became law on July 11, 2026, without the president's signature ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49354.html)); he had said he would sign nothing until Congress passed a separate voter identification bill ([CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/03/29/affordable-housing-trump-ban-investor-home-buying-real-estate.html)).

The section is titled "Homes Are for People, Not Corporations." It works like this:

- **Who is covered.** A for-profit company in the business of owning or renting single-family houses that controls 350 or more of them, alone or together with related companies. The president had sought a limit of 100 ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/1058332434/senate-backs-bill-to-limit-institutional-investors-in-buying-houses)).
- **What is barred.** Buying another single-family house, from January 7, 2027, for 15 years ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49354.html)).
- **What is still allowed.** Among other exceptions: houses built to be rented; run-down houses bought and renovated to rent; houses in programs that help renters become owners; houses bought from another large investor; houses taken to settle a debt; and, until January 7, 2029, houses bought from investors too small to be covered ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/23/2026-21675/21st-century-road-to-housing-act-prohibition-on-large-institutional-investor-purchases-of); [Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/21st-century-road-to-housing-act)).
- **What is not required.** Selling. The law has no requirement that investors give up houses they own ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/21st-century-road-to-housing-act)). An earlier Senate version would have made them sell built-to-rent houses after seven years; the final law dropped that ([KMPH](https://kmph.com/news/connect-to-congress/limits-on-build-for-rent-homes-in-housing-bill-draws-industry-pushback-21st-century-road-to-housing-act-institutional-investors-housing-supply-home-affordability); [CNN](https://www.cnn.com/2026/05/20/economy/congress-passes-housing-affordability-investor-ban-bill)).
- **The penalty.** Up to the greater of $1 million or three times the purchase price, for each violation ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/23/2026-21675/21st-century-road-to-housing-act-prohibition-on-large-institutional-investor-purchases-of)).

The Treasury proposed rules on October 23, 2026, and is taking comments until November 23. Under the proposal a built-to-rent house is a new one, never lived in by an owner, bought within 18 months of completion. A renovated one must have failed the building code on its structure or core systems when bought, and the investor must spend at least 15% of the purchase price on improvements ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/23/2026-21675/21st-century-road-to-housing-act-prohibition-on-large-institutional-investor-purchases-of)). The law also tells the housing department to set up a phone line and website where tenants of large investors can report disputes ([Congressional Research Service](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R49354.html)).

## What regulators have done

In September 2024 Invitation Homes agreed to pay $48 million to settle a Federal Trade Commission complaint. The commission alleged that the company advertised rents that left out mandatory fees of more than $1,700 a year, kept security deposits for normal wear and tear, and started eviction proceedings against tenants who had already moved out ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/86362)). By the commission's figures the company returned 39.2% of tenants' deposit money from 2020 to 2022, against a national average of 63.9% ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/node/86362)). The company said the settlement contained no admission of wrongdoing and defended its disclosures and practices ([Associated Press](https://www.news4jax.com/business/2024/09/24/invitation-homes-agrees-to-pay-48-million-to-settle-claims-it-saddled-tenants-with-hidden-fees/)).

## What the research says

The GAO reviewed 74 studies in 2024 ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)).

**Prices.** Investor buying may have raised prices where it was heaviest, and may have helped steady neighborhoods after the crisis. One study found that houses within a quarter mile of bulk-sale properties rose 1.4% more than houses a little farther away.

**Rents.** Mixed. One study found rents rose in neighborhoods where large landlords merged between 2015 and 2017; others found no significant link between investor buying and rents.

**Homeownership.** Several studies tied concentrated investor buying to lower homeownership nearby. The GAO called the overall effect unclear, because so much else moves homeownership.

**Tenants.** Thin evidence. A Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta paper found that in Fulton County, Georgia, in 2015, companies owning more than 15 rental houses were 8% more likely to file eviction notices than small landlords, after allowing for the house, its location and its history ([Atlanta Fed](https://www.atlantafed.org/community-development/publications/partners-update/2017/01/170216-housing-instability-single-family-evictions-in-one-atlanta-metro-county)). The GAO notes that eviction data are limited.

**Repairs.** Two investors reported spending $15,000 to $39,000 renovating each house they bought in 2020, against about $6,300 for a typical homeowner's first year.

## Where the analogy breaks

Salt doesn't change the soup around it. A large buyer in a small market can: the research suggests prices rose more where these firms bought most. And the salt is no longer being added the way it was. The firms have cut back sharply on buying existing houses and now mostly build their own, which they say adds houses to a market short of them.

## What people argue about

**Small share or big presence.** One side: 3% of rentals and less than half of 1% of houses cannot be what made houses expensive, and the country's shortage of homes is the cause ([Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/will-regulating-large-institutional-investors-actually-make-housing-more-affordable)). The other: a national average hides the places where one house in four for rent has the same few owners, and those are the places where the studies found effects ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)). Each is describing the same numbers.

**Whether the ban will help buyers.** One side: the executive order's stated policy is that large investors should not buy houses that families could otherwise buy ([Davis Polk](https://www.davispolk.com/insights/client-update/congress-weighs-sweeping-ban-institutional-investor-ownership-single-family)), and the law's sponsors, Senators Tim Scott and Elizabeth Warren, wrote the section to promote homeownership for families, not corporations ([Mortgage Professional America](https://www.mpamag.com/us/mortgage-industry/industry-trends/senate-passes-sweeping-housing-bill-as-mba-and-chla-urge-major-fixes/568409)). Supporters can point to the GAO's review, in which several studies tied investor buying to higher prices and lower homeownership where it was concentrated, and to Nashville, where 35% of the houses these investors held had been bought from people who lived in them. The other: Laurie Goodman of the Urban Institute argues that it adds no houses, and that many houses these firms buy need repairs that a first-time buyer can't finance; lenders denied 44% of renovation loan applications in 2024 ([Urban Institute](https://www.urban.org/urban-wire/will-regulating-large-institutional-investors-actually-make-housing-more-affordable)). Cotality said in June, before the law passed, that its data lend credibility to the idea that smaller investors would replace most of the large ones' purchases ([Cotality](https://www.cotality.com/press-releases/investor-report-q1-2026)). The home builders' association backed the housing bill but opposed the limit, saying it would curtail supply ([CoStar](https://www.costar.com/article/1058332434/senate-backs-bill-to-limit-institutional-investors-in-buying-houses)); the final law exempts houses built to rent.

**Better or worse landlords.** One side: the FTC's complaint describes undisclosed fees, withheld deposits and unfair evictions at the largest of these companies. The other: the trade group for real estate investment trusts says single-family rental companies add to the housing supply and let families live in neighborhoods they could not otherwise reach ([Nareit](https://reit.com/news/articles/single-family-rental-reits-playing-key-role-in-boosting-housing-supply-and-allowing-families-to-live-in-neighborhoods-otherwise-not-accessible)). Amherst, one of the firms, reported in 2021 that 85% of its renters lacked the credit or income to qualify for a mortgage ([GAO, 2024](https://gao.gov/assets/870/869193.pdf)). Independent evidence on how tenants fare is, by the GAO's account, limited to a few companies and places.
