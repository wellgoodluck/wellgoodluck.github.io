## In one sentence

A house has a sale price only on the day it sells; every other number attached to it is somebody's estimate, made on a different date, by a different method, for a different purpose.

## The analogy

Think of a used car. The state has a value for it on the registration bill. The insurer has a value for what it would pay if the car were totaled. A website will give a price in seconds from the make, model and mileage. And then there is what a buyer hands over. Nobody expects the four to match, because each was worked out for its own job.

A house has the same four: the assessed value on the tax notice, the appraisal the lender orders, the estimate on a real estate website, and the sale price. The house on this page is worth about $400,000. Figures are as of November 2026.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**The assessed value: for dividing a tax bill.** A county assessor values every property at once, by formula, so that the cost of local government can be split among owners in proportion. The number on the notice may not be meant to equal market value at all. Georgia assesses property at 40% of fair market value, so a $400,000 house there carries an assessed value of $160,000 ([Georgia Department of Revenue](https://dor.georgia.gov/property-tax-valuation)). California sets the value at the purchase price and then generally raises it by no more than 2% a year until the house changes hands; an addition, or the restoring of a value that was cut in a downturn, can raise it more ([California Board of Equalization](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/pdf/pub29.pdf); [on declines in value](https://www.boe.ca.gov/proptaxes/decline-in-value/)). A house bought for $250,000 in 2012, with neither, would be assessed at no more than about $330,000 in 2026, whatever it would sell for. And the value is as of a fixed date, January 1 in Georgia, that is months old by the time the bill arrives ([Georgia Department of Revenue](https://dor.georgia.gov/property-tax-real-and-personal-property-faq)).

**The appraisal: for protecting a lender.** When a buyer borrows, the lender wants to know the house is worth the loan, and orders an appraisal. The appraiser estimates market value, which Fannie Mae defines as the most probable price in a competitive, open market ([Fannie Mae](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b4-1.1-01/definition-market-value)), by comparing the house with similar ones sold recently nearby. It is one trained person's opinion of one house on one date.

**The online estimate: for anyone, instantly.** Zillow's Zestimate and its competitors are produced by computer models for millions of homes at once. Zillow says its model draws on county and tax assessor records, listing services, the home's size and features, and prior sales, and it says the result is not an appraisal ([Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/zestimate)). So this number borrows from two of the others: the assessor's records and past sale prices.

**The sale price: the only fact.** It is what one buyer and one seller agreed to on one day. It is also the number the other three are trying to predict, and the number each is later graded against.

## What moves them

**Assessed value** moves when the assessor revalues, which in some places is every year and in others every several years, and when the law caps it. [How a property tax rate is set](how-a-property-tax-rate-is-set.html) covers what happens when only part of a town is revalued, and [how states limit property taxes](property-tax-limits-by-state.html) covers the caps.

**Appraisals** move with nearby sales, and with the contract. The appraiser on a purchase knows the agreed price.

**Online estimates** move whenever the model gets new data: a nearby sale, a new listing, a new tax record. They can move when the house itself is listed, because the model then takes in the asking price.

**Sale prices** move with [mortgage rates](what-is-the-mortgage-rate.html), the number of homes for sale and the buyers in the market that month.

## How to read each number

**An appraisal rarely disagrees with the price.** Fannie Mae reported in 2016 that for at least two decades more than 90% of the appraisals it received on purchases had valued the home at or above the purchase price, and nearly a third had come in at exactly the price ([Fannie Mae](https://fanniemae.com/portal/research-insights/perspectives/102816-palim-rosenblatt.html)). Economists at the Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia, working with similar data, found fewer than 10% below the contract price, where an unbiased estimate would land below about half the time ([Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2017/wp17-23.pdf)). When an appraisal does come in low, it matters: in one Fannie Mae study, 8% of buyers renegotiated a lower price, against 51% when the appraisal came in 2% or more below the contract ([Fannie Mae](https://www.fanniemae.com/research-and-insights/publications/housing-market-effects-appraising-below-contract)).

**Many loans now skip the appraisal.** In March 2026, 19.7% of the purchase loans Freddie Mac bought and 12.1% of Fannie Mae's had an appraisal waiver, meaning the companies' own models accepted the value ([AEI Housing Center](https://www.aei.org/research-products/report/prevalence-of-gse-appraisal-waivers/)).

**An online estimate is much better once the house is listed.** Zillow reports its own accuracy. For homes on the market, its median error is 1.80%; for homes not on the market, 7.21% ([Zillow](https://www.zillow.com/zestimate), updated November 2, 2026). On a $400,000 house, 7.21% is about $28,800 in either direction. Median means half of the estimates were closer than that and half were farther off.

**An assessment is graded on a curve.** The assessors' own professional standard asks that the typical ratio of assessed value to sale price fall between 90% and 110% of the legal target, and that homes not scatter too far from that typical ratio. Washington State, which publishes a yearly report card, had a statewide ratio of 0.94 for 2024; 27 of its counties met the standard for uniformity among homes and 12 did not ([Washington Department of Revenue](https://dor.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2025-01/2024RatioReport.pdf)).

**Even "the sale price" comes in versions.** The asking price is not the sale price: in September 2026, by Redfin's count, 60.5% of homes sold for less than their original asking price ([Redfin](https://www.redfin.com/news/pending-home-sales-sink-rates/)). And two careful sources can report different medians for the same month. The National Association of Realtors put the median price of an existing home sold in September 2026 at $417,500 ([NAR](https://www.nar.realtor/newsroom/existing-home-sales-increased-1-5-in-september-according-to-nar-report)); Redfin put the median sale price at $393,895 (same Redfin report). They count different sets of sales. The almanac's [home prices](../chart.html?id=home_prices) tile avoids the problem by using an index that follows the same houses from one sale to the next.

## Where the analogy breaks

A used car has thousands of near-identical twins, so a price guide can be very close. No two houses are the same, and most go years between sales, so every estimate leans on a handful of neighbors. And a car's registration value doesn't change what your neighbor pays. An assessed value does: the tax bill is shared, so if one house is assessed too low, the others make up the difference.

## What people argue about

**Whether appraisals tell lenders anything.** One side: an estimate that lands at or above the price nine times in ten is confirming the price, not testing it, and the Philadelphia Fed economists concluded that appraisals carry less information about risk than computer models do ([Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/working-papers/2017/wp17-23.pdf)). The other side: the Appraisal Foundation, which sets the profession's standards, warned that widening the waivers puts consumers and the market at risk ([Inman](https://inman.com/2024/10/29/fannie-and-freddie-will-let-more-buyers-off-the-hook-for-an-appraisal/amp)), and the Fannie Mae research itself shows what a person adds: the rare low appraisal is the one that gets a buyer a lower price.

**Whether assessments are fair to cheaper homes.** One side: Christopher Berry of the University of Chicago compared assessments with millions of sales and found that, within the same jurisdiction, homes in the lowest tenth by price were assessed at twice the share of their value that homes in the highest tenth were, on average, so owners of cheaper homes pay a higher effective rate ([Berry](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3800536)). The other side: a study in the assessors' journal looked at Suffolk County, Massachusetts, with the state's own data. It found the same pattern to a smaller extent, said his method appeared to mismatch the year of the sales with the year of the assessments, and said it left out homestead exemptions, which lower the bills on cheaper homes ([Journal of Property Tax Assessment & Administration](https://researchexchange.iaao.org/jptaa/vol20/iss1/2)). Both find the pattern in assessments; they differ on its size and on what it does to the final bill.
