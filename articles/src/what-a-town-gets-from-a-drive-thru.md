## In one sentence

A busy new restaurant typically pays its city somewhere between nothing and a few hundred thousand dollars a year in sales tax, depending almost entirely on the state's rules, and the city's real gain is smaller than that, because some of those sales would have happened somewhere in town anyway.

## The analogy

Think of a city's share of the sales tax as a commission: a few cents on each dollar sold inside its borders. A busy new restaurant is like a big new account for a salesperson on commission. It pays, but the commission is a small slice of a big number, and if the account's customers were already buying from a colleague down the hall, the office as a whole is no richer.

Tax rates below are as of July 1, 2026; other figures carry their own dates.

## Where a city's tax money comes from

Across the country, local governments (cities, counties, school districts and special districts together) raised about 72% of their tax money from property taxes in 2021 and about 13% from general sales taxes, by the Tax Foundation's count of Census data. Income taxes were about 5%, excise taxes on things like fuel, utilities and hotel rooms about 4%, and other taxes such as licenses about 6% ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/state/local-tax-revenue-local-sales-taxes-local-income-taxes/)).

{{figure kind=stack title="Where local governments' tax money comes from" rows="Property taxes=72;General sales taxes=13;Income taxes=5;Excise taxes=4;Other taxes=6" fmt="{v}%" caption="All U.S. local governments, 2021, share of tax revenue. Excise taxes include fuel, utility and hotel taxes; other taxes include licenses." source="Tax Foundation, from Census data"}}

That national average hides enormous differences, because states write the rules. Five states have no statewide sales tax: Alaska, Delaware, Montana, New Hampshire and Oregon. Thirty-eight states let localities add a sales tax of their own, and Alaska, alone among the five, allows local sales taxes without a state one ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/2026-sales-tax-rates/)). In Louisiana and Arkansas, local governments collect about half their tax money from sales taxes; in a state with no local sales tax, they collect none that way ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/state/local-tax-revenue-local-sales-taxes-local-income-taxes/)). {{due 2027-07-15 "Tax Foundation's 2027 midyear sales tax rates: update the state counts and averages"}}

Some states' rules lean cities toward the sales tax. Oklahoma cities can use property taxes only to repay bonds, not to run the city, so day-to-day city operations are paid for mainly from sales tax ([Oklahoma Policy Institute](https://okpolicy.org/senators-request-interim-study-to-rethink-how-oklahoma-cities-are-funded-capitol-update/)). In California, Proposition 13 caps the property tax at 1% of a property's value, sets that value at the purchase price and lets it rise by 2% a year or the rate of inflation, whichever is lower, until the property sells again; cities receive on average about a fifth of what is collected within their borders ([Legislative Analyst's Office](https://lao.ca.gov/publications/report/3497)). Meanwhile the state returns a 1% local sales tax to the city or county where each sale happened ([Public Policy Institute of California](https://www.ppic.org/press-release/pursuit-of-sales-tax-revenues-driving-development-decisions-in-california-cities/)).

## What one busy restaurant pays

Take a high-volume fast-food restaurant. The top-grossing chains average $6 million or more a year per location, by the trade magazine QSR's estimates for 2025: Chick-fil-A about $7.7 million, Raising Cane's about $6.6 million and In-N-Out, the highest-grossing burger chain, about $6.0 million ([QSR](https://www.qsrmagazine.com/story/these-20-fast-food-chains-earn-the-most-per-restaurant/)). Most fast-food restaurants sell far less, so $6 million is close to the top of the range, not a typical store. {{due 2027-08-15 "QSR 50 for 2027: update the per-restaurant sales figures"}}

What the city keeps from $6 million a year depends only on how many cents of each dollar's tax go to the city:

The three rates are illustrations, not particular cities. In Alabama, Colorado and Oklahoma, local sales taxes average between 4.5 and 5.5 cents on the dollar, county and city combined ([Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/2026-sales-tax-rates/)).

Property tax adds something, and here too the state decides how much. Under California's rules, a restaurant property bought or built for $4 million would owe about $40,000 a year in general property tax, and a city with the average one-fifth share would get about $8,000 of it. Property tax rates, assessment rules and the city's cut differ from state to state, so the same building can pay its city much more or much less elsewhere.

## Gross is not net

Those figures are what the restaurant pays. What the city gains is less, for two reasons.

The first is where the customers come from. Some meals at the new place would otherwise have been eaten at another restaurant in the same city, which already pays the city its share. Others are bought by people who used to drive to the next town, and that spending, and its tax, comes home. The city's net gain is the second group, not the whole register.

The research on big stores points the same way. When Walmart opened in a county, retail employment there rose by about 100 jobs in the first year, and about half of that faded within five years as competing stores shrank or closed ([Basker, Review of Economics and Statistics, 2005](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:tpr:restat:v:87:y:2005:i:1:p:174-183)). A study of Florida found that higher local sales taxes went with more big-box and anchor-store employment but not with more retail employment overall ([Burnes, Neumark and White, NBER, 2011](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16932/revisions/w16932.rev0.pdf)). In California, researchers found that total city sales tax per resident had grown only marginally, after inflation, over two decades, and argued that cities in a region compete for a relatively fixed pool of shopping ([Public Policy Institute of California, 1999](https://www.ppic.org/press-release/pursuit-of-sales-tax-revenues-driving-development-decisions-in-california-cities/)). Some of one city's gain can be its neighbor's loss.

The second reason is cost. A busy drive-thru adds traffic, calls for service and wear on streets, and the gross figure leaves all of that out.

Neither point makes the money imaginary. A city that recaptures sales its residents were making elsewhere gains real revenue, and a busy business can bring customers to its neighbors, whose sales are taxed too. That spillover is the hardest part to measure, and there is no simple rule of thumb for it.

## Why some cities chase stores

Where cities depend on sales tax, a store pays the city every year, while new homes bring residents who need services and, where the city's share of the property tax is small, may pay less than those services cost. In a survey published in 1999 of 330 California cities, about three-quarters of those with vacant land ranked retail above every other use, and city officials named sales tax revenue as their top reason for development, ahead of jobs and affordable housing ([Public Policy Institute of California](https://www.ppic.org/press-release/pursuit-of-sales-tax-revenues-driving-development-decisions-in-california-cities/)). Planners call this the fiscalization of land use.

The competition can turn into bidding. Since 1999, California has barred a city or county from giving financial help (subsidies, tax rebates, cheap land or waived fees) to a car dealer, or a big-box store of more than 75,000 square feet, that relocates from another city or county in the same market area, which is capped at 40 road miles for dealers and 25 for big-box stores. The ban covers relocations only, not a business opening a new store ([California Government Code § 53084](https://california.public.law/codes/government_code_section_53084)).

## Where the analogy breaks

A commission rate is the same everywhere in a company. A city's share of the sales tax is set by its state and its voters and runs from nothing to several cents on the dollar, so the same restaurant is a small account in one city and a large one in the next. And a salesperson's account costs the office little to serve; a busy restaurant uses streets, police and fire service, which the commission never counts.

## What people argue about

**Do cities really zone for sales tax?** One side: the incentive is plain, officials say so in surveys, and the PPIC's 1999 study found retail ranked first in most California cities with land to develop. The other: when California's Legislative Analyst's Office compared similar cities in 2016, it found that fiscal incentives did not appear to have a major influence on land use. In almost two-thirds of matched pairs, the city with the smaller property-tax share permitted more housing, and cities more reliant on sales tax were slightly more likely to rezone for retail but did not consistently permit more of it ([Legislative Analyst's Office](https://lao.ca.gov/publications/report/3497)). The LAO noted that cities may still respond in other ways, such as tax breaks, publicly financed property improvements or cheap land for retailers.

**Should cities rely on sales tax at all?** One side: sales tax lets a city collect from visitors and commuters, not only residents, and pay for the services they use. The other: it rewards cities for winning shopping from their neighbors rather than for adding anything new, and gives cities a reason to favor stores over homes; the PPIC's authors proposed shifting a much larger share of the property tax to cities and counties, possibly in exchange for returning some narrower revenue sources to the state ([Public Policy Institute of California](https://www.ppic.org/press-release/pursuit-of-sales-tax-revenues-driving-development-decisions-in-california-cities/)).
