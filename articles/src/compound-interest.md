## Short version

When interest is added to a balance, the next round of interest is figured on the bigger balance. That's compounding: interest earning interest. Over a year or two it barely shows. Over decades it does most of the work, which is why money put away early can end up worth more than larger amounts put away later. The same math runs the other way on debt: an unpaid credit card balance grows on its own interest, at a rate several times what savings earn.

## The idea in one sentence

Compounding means each period's interest is figured on the previous periods' interest too, so growth speeds up the longer it runs, for savings and for debt alike.

## How it works

**Simple vs. compound.** Put $1,000 somewhere that pays 6% a year. With simple interest, you'd earn $60 every year, forever. With compound interest, you earn $60 the first year, then 6% of $1,060 ($63.60) the second, then 6% of $1,123.60 the third, and so on. The difference is small at first and then isn't.

**Time does most of the work.** At 6% a year, that $1,000 becomes:

<table><thead><tr><th>After</th><th>Balance</th><th>Growth in that decade</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td>10 years</td><td>$1,791</td><td>$791</td></tr><tr><td>20 years</td><td>$3,207</td><td>$1,416</td></tr><tr><td>30 years</td><td>$5,743</td><td>$2,536</td></tr><tr><td>40 years</td><td>$10,286</td><td>$4,542</td></tr></tbody></table>

Nothing was added after the first $1,000. Each decade simply grows more than the one before.

**The rule of 72.** A quick way to estimate how long money takes to double: divide 72 by the yearly rate. At 6%, about 12 years. At 4%, about 18. At the national average savings rate of 0.38%, nearly 190 years (the rule gets rough at very low rates; the exact answer is about 183). At a credit card's 22%, a little over three.

**The debt side.** Most credit cards charge interest daily on the balance you carry, and the interest is added to what you owe. The average rate on card accounts being charged interest was [22.36% in the third quarter of 2026](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g19/current/), a preliminary figure. At that rate, a balance grows nearly four times as fast as savings earning 6%, and more than fifty times as fast as savings earning 0.38%. That's why [paying only the minimum](credit-card-minimum-payments.html) can stretch a modest balance across a decade.

**Prices compound too.** Inflation is compounding working against you: 3.7% a year, as consumer prices [rose over the year to September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm), roughly doubles prices in about 20 years. Interest only adds buying power when it beats inflation (see [real vs. nominal](real-vs-nominal.html)).

**The quote.** You'll often see Albert Einstein credited with calling compound interest the eighth wonder of the world, or mankind's greatest invention. Quote Investigator found [no real evidence he said the first](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/09/09/interest/) and [none for the second](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2011/10/31/compound-interest/), and Princeton's *The Ultimate Quotable Einstein* lists the line under "Probably Not By Einstein." The math holds up anyway.

## A worked example

**Starting at 25 or at 35.** Two people each put $100 a month into an account earning 6% a year, an assumption for illustration and not a promise any investment makes, and stop at 65.

Ten more years of deposits, $12,000 more put in, and nearly twice the result.

**The same $2,000, both ways.** One person has $2,000 in savings at 4%. Another has a $2,000 card balance at 22.36% and, to show what compounding does, never pays it down or adds to it.

In real life a card requires minimum payments, and missed ones bring late fees and damage your credit, so no balance actually sits untouched. But every dollar of the balance you don't pay off behaves like the right-hand column.

## What to do with it

**Start early, even small.** At the same 6%, someone who starts at 45 with $200 a month puts in the same $48,000 as the person who starts at 25 with $100, and ends with about $90,700, less than half as much.

**Know both of your rates:** what your savings earn (the APY) and what your debts cost (the APR). The gap between them is what compounding is doing to you, one way or the other.

**Pay card statements in full when you can.** When you can't, pay as much more than the minimum as you can, and see the difference with [the card payoff tool](/tools/card-payoff.html).

**Let savings sit.** Withdrawing the interest, or the money, shrinks what's left to compound.

**Treat 6% as an illustration, not a forecast.** Savings accounts pay less, investments that can earn more can also lose money, and nobody can promise any rate.
