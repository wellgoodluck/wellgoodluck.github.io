## Short version

Social media is now where many young people learn about investing, and some of it is genuinely helpful. But feeds also reward confidence over accuracy, hide who's paying for a post, and are where the most expensive scams start. Investment fraud was the largest category of losses the FBI tracked last year, [at about $8.65 billion](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf). A few warning signs catch most of it.

## The trap in one sentence

The people with the most exciting results on your feed are the ones you see; the ones who lost money aren't posting.

## How it works

A FINRA Investor Education Foundation study found that [60% of investors aged 18 to 34](https://finrafoundation.org/sites/finrafoundation/files/2026-03/FINRA_Foundation_Research_Brief_Social_Media_Finfluencers.pdf) use social media for investment ideas, and 61% said they had made investment decisions based on a finfluencer's recommendation.

Three things make feeds risky:

- **Selection.** You see winning trades, not losing ones. People who made money post screenshots; people who lost money go quiet. That makes risky strategies look safer than they are.
- **Hidden incentives.** Some creators are paid to promote a stock, a coin, an app or a trading course. Paid endorsements are supposed to be disclosed, but the disclosure can be easy to miss or missing entirely. Others profit when followers buy something they already own.
- **Outright fraud.** Many investment scams begin with a friendly contact on social media or a messaging app, move to a private "investment club" or group chat, and end with the victim sending money, often in cryptocurrency, to a fake trading platform. The FBI recorded [about $7.2 billion in losses to cryptocurrency investment fraud](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf) alone in 2025.

That's nearly 38 times your money in a year. No legitimate investment reliably does anything close; if someone could, they wouldn't need your money or your follow. The smaller and more "reasonable" a daily return sounds, the more likely it's a setup.

## How to spot the warning signs

**Guaranteed or steady high returns.** Real investments go up and down. Anyone promising they won't is either mistaken or lying.

{{illustration name=finfluencer-post caption="A made-up post from an invented account, with four warning signs numbered."}}

**Urgency.** "Only a few spots left," "buy before it moons," "the window closes tonight." Pressure is there to keep you from thinking.

**Moving to a private chat or a new app.** Being invited into a group chat, a "VIP" channel or an unfamiliar trading app is one of the most common scam patterns.

**Requests for crypto, gift cards or wire transfers.** Legitimate brokerages don't ask you to pay this way.

**No license you can check.** Anyone selling investments or charging for personalized advice should be registered. You can look up brokers and advisers for free on FINRA's BrokerCheck and the SEC's adviser search.

**"Withdrawal fees" or "taxes" before you can take money out.** That's a hallmark of fake platforms. The balance on the screen isn't real.

## How to use your feed well

Social media can be a fine place to learn concepts: what an index fund is, how compound interest works, why fees matter. Treat it like a starting point, not a source of trades. Before acting on anything, ask who benefits if you buy, and check the claim somewhere that isn't selling you anything.

If something goes wrong, report it at ic3.gov, and tell your bank or exchange right away; the sooner a transfer is flagged, the better the chance of stopping it.

## The bigger picture

Regulators are still working out how to handle investing advice on social media. In December 2025, FINRA, the industry's self-regulator, [published a report on social media-influenced investing](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2025-12/2025-social-media-influenced-investing.pdf) and asked for public comment on it; comments closed in May 2026. [In July 2026 it proposed updating its advertising rules](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2026-07/Regulatory-Notice-26-14.pdf), including how they apply to paid endorsers such as finfluencers. [The SEC warned investors in February 2026](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/social-media-stock-scams) against making investment decisions based solely on social media.

**Those who want tighter rules** argue that paid promotion should be impossible to miss, that platforms should do more to stop scams, and that creators giving specific buy-and-sell advice should face the same standards as licensed advisers.

**Those wary of tighter rules** argue that social media has opened investing to people the industry long ignored, that many creators explain jargon better than traditional firms do, and that broad rules could chill free discussion of markets. FINRA's own report says social media holds the potential to improve overall financial literacy.
