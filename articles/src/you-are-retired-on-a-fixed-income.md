## What reaches you, in order

1. **Inflation**, continuously. A fixed income is a fixed number of dollars, and inflation decides what each one buys. Only Social Security adjusts, and it adjusts once a year, for last year.
2. **The Fed funds rate**, within days, through what your cash and short-term savings earn.
3. **The 10-year Treasury yield**, through CDs, bond funds and annuity quotes, and in 2022 through the worst year bonds ever had.
4. **Medicare premiums**, every January, out of the same check the COLA goes into.
5. **Mortgage rates and home prices**, if the house is part of the plan.

Jobs, claims and GDP matter to you mostly through the people you support and the taxes that fund your benefits. This page is about the five above.

## Inflation, and the raise that answers it

Social Security's cost-of-living adjustment is computed from the CPI for urban wage earners, CPI-W, averaged over July through September, compared with the same three months a year earlier. It is announced in October and paid starting in January. For 2027 it is 3.6%, so a $2,000 monthly benefit rises by $72.

The lag runs the same direction every year. Prices that rose in the spring of 2026 are not compensated until January 2027; if they rise again next spring, the gap opens again until January 2028. In a year of steady 2–3% inflation that is a nuisance. In 2021–22 it was the difference between a 1.3% adjustment and prices rising 7%, a loss that the 8.7% adjustment of 2023 made up only in part and only afterward.

Two other things about the formula. The index is for wage earners, who spend less on health care and more on gasoline than retirees do; an experimental index for older households, CPI-E, has run slightly higher over the decades, which is one of the arguments below. And a pension without a cost-of-living clause, which describes most private pensions, has no formula at all. A $3,000 pension fixed in 2019 buys what $2,290 did then.

## The Fed, through your cash

Savings accounts, money market funds and short-term Treasury bills follow the Fed funds rate within days of a decision, in both directions. From 2009 to 2015 and again in 2020–21, cash earned nothing while prices rose; from 2023 on it has earned a real return for the first time since 2007. The 2026 hikes raised it again. That makes the Fed's decisions the most immediate number on this page for anyone holding cash, with one caveat: the large traditional banks barely passed the increases on, and the gap between an account paying 0.01% and one paying near the Fed funds rate is a choice of bank, not a market condition. [The Fed funds rate](what-is-the-fed-funds-rate.html) has the order in which everything else follows.

## The 10-year, through bonds

CDs, bond funds and annuity payouts are priced off Treasury yields of matching length, and a bond's price moves opposite to its yield. In 2022 the 10-year yield went from 1.5% to 4% and a typical intermediate bond fund lost about 13%, the worst year on record for an asset class sold as the safe part of a portfolio. The same mechanism works in reverse: at 5% the 10-year now pays more than at any time since 2007, which is why annuity quotes and CD rates are the highest many retirees have seen since they started saving. [The 10-year yield](what-is-the-10-year-yield.html) explains what sets it; it is not the Fed, and it rose through the Fed's 2024 cuts.

## Medicare, every January

The standard Part B premium is deducted from the Social Security check, and it has risen faster than the COLA in most years: for 2026 it rose about 10%, to $202.90 a month, against a 2.8% benefit increase. A "hold harmless" rule prevents the premium increase from exceeding the dollar COLA for most beneficiaries, so the check can't fall, but the premium regularly eats a quarter or more of the raise. Higher-income retirees pay surcharges that step up in brackets, set on income from two years earlier.

## Mortgage rates and home prices

A retiree who owns outright is insulated from mortgage rates except through the buyer pool for the house, and from inflation except through insurance and property tax; [you own with a fixed mortgage](you-own-with-a-fixed-mortgage.html) covers both. The lock-in effect cuts the other way for anyone planning to downsize: the smaller house carries a 7% mortgage or a cash price set by other buyers who couldn't get one either.

## Where you come out ahead

The benefit is indexed, which almost no private income is. Cash and bonds finally pay a real return, after fifteen years in which they mostly didn't. And the biggest cost in most retirements, housing, is fixed or paid off for the two-thirds of retirees who own. Against that: no raise to negotiate when prices move, a formula that answers inflation a year late, health-care costs that rise faster than the index measuring them, and a bond market that proved in 2022 it could lose a decade of coupons in a year.

## What people argue about

**Whether the COLA is the right index.** CPI-E, the experimental index for people over 62, weights health care and housing more heavily and has run about two-tenths of a point above CPI-W per year on average, which compounds. Supporters say that is the honest measure of what retirees buy; opponents say the gap is small, inconsistent, and that switching would add to a program already short of money. A chained CPI, which runs lower, has been proposed from the other direction for the same reason.

**Whether higher rates are good for retirees.** Savers finally earn something, which one side counts as the first fair deal for cash since 2007. The other side points out that the same rates cut bond prices, raised the mortgage on the downsize, and that inflation, which the rates are fighting, is what hurt fixed incomes in the first place. Both are true at once, and which one dominates depends on how much of a retirement is in cash, bonds or a house.

**The trust fund date.** Under current law the Social Security trust fund is projected to be exhausted in the early 2030s, after which incoming taxes cover roughly four-fifths of scheduled benefits. One side reads that as a cut coming unless taxes rise; the other as a cut coming unless benefits are trimmed; nobody reads it as nothing. The trustees' report is linked below. The site shows the date and the arithmetic; it does not pick the fix.
