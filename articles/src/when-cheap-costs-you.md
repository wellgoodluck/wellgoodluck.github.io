## Short version

Saving money isn't the trap. Store brands, a used couch and a cheaper phone plan are usually the same thing for less. The trap is a narrower kind of bargain: the price is lower because the seller skips what makes the other option cost more, like commercial insurance, a license, a safety test or a signature. You save a little, for certain. In return you take on a rare but large risk that the higher price would have covered. The warning signs repeat: no license number, no proof of insurance, cash only, nothing in writing. A small local business that has all of those isn't the problem. One that has none of them is.

## The trap in one sentence

You save a small amount for certain and take on a large risk you can't see, one the higher price would have paid for.

## How it works

**What's missing is what you weren't paying for.** A licensed car service pays for commercial insurance. A licensed contractor answers to a state board you can complain to. A paid tax preparer has to sign the return. The cash option skips some or all of that, and that's part of why it's cheaper. When nothing goes wrong, the savings were real.

**Your own coverage often has a hole right where theirs should be.** Personal auto policies almost always exclude driving people for pay. California's Department of Insurance has said [virtually all of them do](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0400-news/0100-press-releases/archives/release009-15.cfm), except for a carpool where riders share the cost of the trip. So if someone drives you for a set fare in their own car and crashes, [their insurer can refuse the claim](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/0250-insurers/0300-insurers/0200-bulletins/bulletin-notices-commiss-opinion/TransNetwkDrvrs.cfm). Splitting gas with a friend is a carpool. Paying a fare is a ride for hire.

**Sometimes the law hands the risk to you.** In California, if you hire an unlicensed person for work that needed a contractor's license, [the state treats that person as your employee](https://dir.ca.gov/dosh/dosh_publications/Domestic-Svc-for-Homeowners-fs.pdf), and [you could be asked to pay](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Resources/GuidesAndPublications/OwnerBuilders.pdf) if they're hurt on the job. On a tax return, [you're legally responsible for what's filed](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/dirty-dozen-tax-scams-for-2026-irs-reminds-taxpayers-to-watch-out-for-dangerous-threats), even if someone else prepared it and wouldn't put their name on it.

**Paying in cash removes the last safety net.** A credit card charge can be disputed. Cash can't, and money sent by payment app to a stranger is usually gone (see [payment app scams](payment-app-scams.html)). That's why demands for cash or a big deposit up front show up on official warning lists, such as [the federal government's list for movers](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/sites/fmcsa.dot.gov/files/docs/Protect_Your_Move_Brochure.pdf).

**Where it turns up most:**

- **Rides.** A "car service" found on a flyer or in a group chat, paid in cash. In California, a car service has to hold a state permit and, for a car with up to eight seats including the driver's, carry [at least $750,000 in liability insurance](https://cpuc.ca.gov/-/media/cpuc-website/files/uploadedfiles/cpucwebsite/content/licensing/go-115-g.pdf). The state requires ride apps to carry [$1 million from the moment a driver accepts your ride until you get out](https://www.cpuc.ca.gov/tncinsurance).
- **Home repairs.** The handyman who quotes well under licensed contractors. In California, a license is required for [any job of $1,000 or more in labor and materials](https://www.cslb.ca.gov/Resources/IndustryBulletins/2024/AB%202622%20Implementation.FINAL.pdf), and for smaller jobs too if the work needs a permit or the worker [hires anyone to help](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_bus_and_prof_code_section_7048).
- **Moving.** The federal agency that oversees interstate movers warns about ["rogue movers"](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/sites/fmcsa.dot.gov/files/docs/Protect_Your_Move_Brochure.pdf) who give a low estimate without ever seeing what you own. In 2023 it [sent investigators across the country](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/newsroom/fmcsa-launches-operation-protect-your-move-nationwide-crackdown-moving-scams) after a rise in complaints about movers holding belongings hostage on the truck until customers paid far more.
- **Tax returns.** The IRS's [2026 "Dirty Dozen" list of tax scams](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/dirty-dozen-tax-scams-for-2026-irs-reminds-taxpayers-to-watch-out-for-dangerous-threats) includes ghost preparers: people paid to prepare a return who won't sign it or include their preparer ID number. They often [promise big refunds and may disappear after filing](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/be-informed-not-fooled-by-ghost-preparers-and-tax-credit-scams), leaving the taxpayer to deal with the penalties, interest or audit.
- **Chargers and batteries.** Off-brand chargers and replacement batteries for e-bikes and scooters that were never tested by an independent lab. The Consumer Product Safety Commission says [many lithium-ion battery fires and deaths happen at night](https://cpsc.gov/Safety-Education/Safety-Education-Centers/Micromobility-Information-Center), while batteries are charging.

## A worked example

Alex needs a ride to the airport and has three choices (the prices are made up for the example):

- A licensed car service: $95
- A ride app: $60
- A neighbor's cousin who "does rides," cash: $40

Alex is self-employed and doesn't own a car. Alex takes the $40 ride. On the way, the driver runs a red light. Alex ends up with a broken wrist, a concussion, $40,000 in medical bills and four weeks off work at $1,000 a week, with no paid leave or disability pay.

On a marketplace health plan at [the 2026 limit](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/out-of-pocket-maximum-limit/), Alex could owe up to $10,600 toward the medical bills. Add the $4,000 in lost pay and the total is as much as **$14,600**, to save $20 over the app or $55 over the car service. That's about 730 times the $20 saved, even with health insurance. ## How to avoid it

**Ask four questions before you hire anyone.**

1. *What's your license or permit number?* Then look it up yourself, on the agency's own website, not a link they send you. Interstate movers must have a USDOT number you can [check with the Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/protect-your-move). California car services are required to show a "TCP" permit number [on the car](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/Published/G000/M236/K851/236851000.PDF) and [in their ads](https://california.public.law/codes/ca_pub_util_code_section_5386).
2. *Can I see your insurance?* Ask for a certificate of insurance and, for a contractor with employees, proof of workers' compensation.
3. *Can I pay by card?* Pay deposits with a credit card, not cash, a wire or a payment app. Be wary of anyone who wants [a large deposit up front](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/senior_lawyers/publications/voice_of_experience/2022/september-2022/moving-fraud-how-to-prevent-it/).
4. *Will you put it in writing and sign it?* A written estimate (for movers, a binding one), a written contract, and a tax return with the preparer's signature and ID number on it. Never sign a blank or incomplete return.

**Small isn't the same as unlicensed.** A local car service or a one-person contracting business with a license and insurance can be every bit as safe as a big one. The questions are the same for both.

**For anything that plugs in, look for a testing lab's mark.** UL, ETL (from Intertek) and CSA are among [the labs the federal government recognizes](https://www.osha.gov/nationally-recognized-testing-laboratory-program/current-list-of-nrtls) to test product safety. For e-bikes and scooters, use the charger that came with it or one the maker recommends, never a "universal" one, and don't charge it overnight or while you're out.

**Save where cheaper means the same thing for less.** Store brands, used furniture, a smaller apartment, a plan sized to what you actually use. Be wary where cheaper means the same thing minus the protection.

**Keep a small cushion.** Sometimes people take the risky option because the safe one costs more than they have that day. [A few hundred dollars set aside](emergency-cushion.html) gives you room to choose.

## The bigger picture

Whether there's a license to check at all depends on the state, and states differ widely. About one in five employed Americans ([21.6%](https://www.bls.gov/cps/cpsaat53.htm)) held an active government-issued professional license in 2025, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, up from [fewer than 5% with a state license in the early 1950s](https://obamawhitehouse.archives.gov/sites/default/files/docs/licensing_report_final_nonembargo.pdf). A 2015 White House report found that states license very different lists of jobs: funeral attendants were licensed in nine states, florists in one. Meanwhile no federal license is required to prepare tax returns for pay: enrolled agents, CPAs and attorneys hold credentials, but [anyone with an IRS preparer ID number](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108723) can do the work. The IRS tried to require [testing and continuing education](https://www.journalofaccountancy.com/issues/2013/mar/20137232/) in 2011, and in 2014 a federal appeals court ruled ([Loving v. IRS](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/13-5061/13-5061-2014-02-11.html)) that Congress had never given it that power. Only [a few states, including California, New York, Oregon and Maryland](https://dontmesswithtaxes.com/7-states-that-regulate-paid-tax-preparers/), set their own requirements. The Government Accountability Office has recommended since 2014 that Congress give the IRS that authority, and [repeated it in February 2026](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/884479.pdf). In October 2026 it [reported](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-27-108176) that the IRS doesn't know how many ghost preparers it has identified and dealt with, because the systems it uses to track them aren't connected. {{due 2027-04-15 "After the IRS's 2027 Dirty Dozen list and the 2027 State Occupational Licensing Index: recheck the ghost-preparer paragraph (GAO's recommendations, any federal preparer bill), the states that regulate preparers, and the universal-recognition count"}}

The argument is over whether more licensing would protect people like Alex or push more of them toward the cash option.

**Supporters of licensing and stronger enforcement** argue that consumers can't check a stranger's training, insurance or honesty from an ad, and that licensing is how insurance and bonding requirements get enforced at all. California's ride apps carry $1 million in coverage because [state regulators required it in 2013](https://docs.cpuc.ca.gov/PublishedDocs/Published/G000/M166/K685/166685470.PDF) and a 2014 state law wrote it into statute; in between, the state's insurance department [warned drivers that their personal policies likely wouldn't pay](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/west/2014/11/13/347035.htm). Even the 2015 White House report, which found real costs in licensing, said that when it's designed and carried out carefully, licensing can offer important health and safety protections. On tax preparers, they point to [GAO's undercover visits](https://www.gao.gov/assets/890/884479.pdf) to commercial preparers in 2006 and 2014, where most of the preparers visited got the refund wrong, and to [a tax professionals' group's complaint](https://www.cpapracticeadvisor.com/?p=14443) that in most states it takes more to become a barber than a paid tax preparer.

**Skeptics** argue that licensing raises prices and limits who's allowed to do the work, which is part of why the licensed option costs more and some people skip it. The same 2015 report found that licensed workers earn 10 to 15% more than unlicensed workers with similar education, training and experience, and the economist Morris Kleiner, a leading researcher on licensing, [has written](https://www.brookings.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/reform_occupational_licensing_policies_kleiner_v4.pdf) that studies find far more cases where licensing raised prices and reduced employment than where it improved quality or safety. After the IRS rules were struck down, the lawyer for the preparers who sued [told a Senate committee](https://www.finance.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Dan%20Alban%20Testimony.pdf) that existing laws already punished preparer fraud and that licensing would reduce competition and drive up prices.

Between the two positions, the 2015 report suggested that lighter options, such as state certification, may strike a better balance in some fields. Another reform lets licensed workers keep working when they move to another state: [28 states](https://www.archbridgeinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/2025-State-Occupational-Licensing-Index.pdf) had adopted some form of "universal" license recognition by 2025, though the 2026 edition of the same index [found no new states had joined](https://glsolutions.com/?p=231826).

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 14, 2026.
