## At a glance

- **Who:** grades 9–12 economics or personal finance; adult education.
- **Time:** 45 minutes (hook 5, chart 10, sorting activity 20, discussion 10).
- **Standard:** Council for Economic Education, [National Content Standards in K–12 Economics, 3rd ed. (2025)](https://www.councilforeconed.org/wp-content/uploads/National-Content-Standards-in-K–12-Economics-3rd-Edition.pdf), Standard 12: Unemployment.
- **Pages used:** the [Unemployment rate chart](../chart.html?id=unemployment) and [What the Unemployment Rate Is, and Who It Doesn't Count](what-is-the-unemployment-rate.html).
- **Materials:** a projector, and the [student handout](lesson-unemployment-handout.html), printed (it holds the sorting cards).
- **Students leave able to:** sort people into employed, unemployed and not in the labor force; work out a rate from a headcount; explain how the rate can fall when nobody finds work; say what U-6 adds.

## Opening hook (5 minutes)

Read three people aloud and ask, by show of hands, "Is this person unemployed?"

1. A high school senior with no job who isn't looking for one.
2. A laid-off factory worker who has sent out applications every week for two months.
3. A laid-off factory worker who gave up looking six weeks ago because nothing came of it.

Most classes say yes to the third. The official answer is no: only the second counts. Bridge: "Today we find out who the unemployment rate counts, and what that means when you read the headline."

## Reading the chart together (10 minutes)

Project the [Unemployment rate chart](../chart.html?id=unemployment).

1. **What is it?** "Share of the labor force looking for work." The rate was {{v unemployment}} in {{v unemployment period}}, against {{v unemployment.previous}} the month before.
2. **Where does it come from?** A monthly survey of about 60,000 households by the Census Bureau for the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It's a sample, so a change of a tenth of a point can be noise ([What the Unemployment Rate Is](what-is-the-unemployment-rate.html) explains why).
3. **Is it high?** Read the bar and the line under it. The 2015–19 average was {{v unemployment.baseline}}.
4. **The long view.** Click **All**. Students find the highest month, {{v unemployment.max}} in {{v unemployment.max period}}, and the lowest, {{v unemployment.min}} in {{v unemployment.min period}}. Point out the shaded recessions: the rate rises in every one.
5. **The wider count.** Under Companion series, tick **U-6**. It adds people working part time who want full time, and people who want a job but stopped looking recently. U-6 was {{v u6}} in {{v u6 period}}. Ask why it always sits above the headline rate.

## Activity: Sort the town (20 minutes)

The handout has twelve cards. In pairs, students sort each person into **Employed**, **Unemployed** or **Not in the labor force**, using the three rules on the handout. Then they work out the town's unemployment rate.

**Twist (last 5 minutes):** It's next month's survey. Jordan has stopped looking; the last application was five weeks ago. Everyone else is unchanged. Re-sort Jordan and work the rate out again. Ask: "Did anyone find a job? What happened to the rate?"

## Discussion questions (10 minutes)

1. *Reading it.* Why does the survey ask whether someone looked for work, not just whether they have a job?
2. In the twist, the rate fell. Was that good news for the town?
3. *Placing it.* Why might a reporter quote U-6 instead of the headline rate? Why might another reporter not?
4. The rate comes from a survey of households, not from unemployment-benefit filings. What does that let it see that benefit filings miss?
5. *Judging it.* Some people say the official rate "hides" people who gave up. Others say counting them would mix in people who don't want a job. Which count would you put in the headline, and why?
6. Is an unemployment rate of zero possible? Is it desirable?

## Answer key

**Sorting (rules: employed = did any paid work in the survey week, or was away from a job they have; unemployed = no job, looked in the past four weeks and could start, or is on temporary layoff waiting to be called back; not in the labor force = everyone else):**

Count: 4 employed, 3 unemployed, 5 not in the labor force. Labor force = 7. Rate = 3 ÷ 7, about 43% (a town in deep trouble, on purpose, so the arithmetic shows). After the twist: 2 unemployed, labor force 6, rate 2 ÷ 6, about 33%. Nobody found work; the rate fell because Jordan left the count.

1. A person with no job who isn't looking, like a retiree, isn't short of work. Looking is what separates "can't find a job" from "doesn't want one now."
2. No. The rate fell because someone gave up, which is why economists also watch the share of people with jobs (the almanac's prime-age employment tile).
3. U-6 catches part-timers who want more hours and people on the edge of the labor force, so it shows slack the headline misses. The case against: it mixes in people with jobs, and its definitions are looser.
4. A survey reaches people who never filed for benefits: new graduates, people who quit, people whose benefits ran out, and people who don't qualify.
5. Either view holds up; students should name the trade-off. BLS publishes six measures, U-1 to U-6, so readers can choose ([BLS table A-15](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.t15.htm)).
6. Not possible: people are always between jobs, moving or newly looking. Most economists treat some unemployment as normal; the Congressional Budget Office's estimate of the "natural rate" is on the chart: under Companion series, tick **CBO estimate of the natural rate of unemployment**.
