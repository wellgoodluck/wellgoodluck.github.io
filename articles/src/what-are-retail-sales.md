## In one sentence

Retail sales are the dollars that stores, car dealers, gas stations, online sellers, bars and restaurants took in during a month, measured by a Census Bureau survey; the almanac shows them after inflation, as the change from a year earlier.

The first estimate comes out about ten business days after the month ends, from a survey of about 4,800 companies, and is revised the next month from a larger survey of about 13,000. The Census Bureau publishes the figures in dollars. The St. Louis Fed takes out inflation by dividing by the consumer price index, and that adjusted line is the one on the almanac.

## The analogy

Picture the receipt tape from every cash register in the country, spliced into one roll and totaled at the end of the month. That is retail sales. It catches the groceries, the gas, the new couch, the car, the online order and the dinner out. It never sees the rent check, the doctor's bill, the insurance premium, the phone plan or the streaming subscription, because none of those pass through a register.

## How it reaches your kitchen table

**Jobs: stores hire to meet it.** Retail and restaurants employ millions, many in first jobs and part-time jobs. When sales slow, hours get cut before anyone is laid off.

**Prices: through the Fed.** Strong spending is one reason the Fed holds rates up; weak spending is one reason it cuts. Retail sales are the first spending number of each month, so markets move on them.

**The economy's growth: a preview.** Consumer spending is about two-thirds of GDP, and retail sales arrive weeks before the quarter's GDP estimate. Forecasters use a narrower piece of it, sales outside car dealers, gas stations, building-material stores and restaurants, to estimate the goods part of consumer spending.

## What moves it

Jobs and paychecks, first. Then prices: a jump in gas prices raises sales at gas stations without anyone buying more gas. Then the calendar: holidays, the number of weekends in a month, and the weather, which the seasonal adjustment tries to smooth. Then one-time pushes, such as relief checks, or people buying ahead of an expected price increase.

Car sales matter more than their share suggests. They swing a lot from month to month, so a headline can move on cars alone.

## How to read this month's number

**It is about two-fifths of consumer spending, by dollars.** In September 2026, retail and food services sales ran at about $745 billion for the month, roughly $8.9 trillion a year, while all consumer spending ran at about $22.5 trillion a year. The two don't line up exactly: some store sales go to businesses, and most services never pass through a store. A month of flat retail sales can come with rising total spending if the rise is in services.

**The inflation adjustment is rough.** Dividing by the whole consumer price index takes out the price of everything households buy, including rent and medical care, which retail sales don't include. When goods prices and service prices move apart, the adjusted line can make store sales look stronger or weaker than they were.

**The first estimate is a first estimate.** It comes from a smaller sample and is revised a month later, and again each spring. A change of a few tenths of a percent in one month can disappear.

**The yearly change hides the base.** After inflation, retail sales were 19.8% below a year earlier in April 2020, when stores were closed, and 45.5% above it in April 2021, because April 2020 was the comparison. ## Where the analogy breaks

A receipt tape records what was sold, not who bought it or how they paid. A store sale paid for with a credit card counts the same as one paid in cash, so retail sales can rise while households are borrowing to spend. A sale to a small business counts as retail too. And the tape doesn't know whether a higher total meant more things or higher prices; that is what the inflation adjustment is for, and it's approximate.

## What people argue about

**Whether spending is healthy.** One side: after inflation, store and restaurant sales kept growing through 2026, alongside steady jobs and a record median income in 2025, and spending that grows with income is what a healthy economy looks like. The other side: the saving rate in 2026 was well below its 2015–19 average, so spending was growing faster than income, partly by saving less, which can't continue indefinitely. The two readings use the same numbers; they differ on how long the gap can last (see [Four numbers on how households are holding up](what-the-household-numbers-measure.html)).

**Whether retail sales still matter as much.** One side: they are the fastest hard number on spending, from actual sales rather than surveys of intentions, and markets still move on them. The other side: as more household budgets go to services, health care and subscriptions, a measure built around stores covers less of the picture each decade, and the monthly personal spending report, two weeks later, covers it all.
