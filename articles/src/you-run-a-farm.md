## What reaches you, in order

1. **Crop and cattle prices**, every trading day, set on world markets and by the few buyers described in [The Name on the Label](who-owns-the-farm.html).
2. **Fertilizer and fuel**, before planting and all season: [nitrogen fertilizer](../chart.html?id=fertilizer) and [diesel](../chart.html?id=diesel), and behind fertilizer, [natural gas](../chart.html?id=natural_gas).
3. **The [Fed funds rate](what-is-the-fed-funds-rate.html)**, through the operating loan that carries a crop from planting to sale, and through land and equipment loans when they renew.
4. **The dollar and trade policy**, through exports, a season or two out.
5. **The jobs report and pay**, because most farm households live partly on off-farm income.

The almanac's farm numbers are national benchmarks: [corn](../chart.html?id=corn) at Gulf of Mexico export ports, [soybeans](../chart.html?id=soybeans) on Chicago futures, [wheat](../chart.html?id=wheat) at Kansas City, [cotton](../chart.html?id=cotton) on the world market, and [cattle](../chart.html?id=cattle) as what packers pay for fed steers and heifers. What your elevator, gin or sale barn pays differs by place, season and freight, but it moves with them.

Figures below are as of early November 2026.

## What you sell

In September 2026 corn averaged $6.02 a bushel at the Gulf, up from $4.98 a year earlier; soybeans $13.11 on Chicago futures, up from $10.03; wheat $6.72 at Kansas City, up from $4.22; and cotton 96¢ a pound on the world market, up from 78¢. Adjusted for inflation, corn, soybeans and wheat sit near the middle of where they've been since 1992, and cotton near the low end, below about four in five months ([IMF, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PMAIZMTUSDM)). {{due 2027-01-15 "Update the farm prices in What you sell to the latest month, with year-ago figures and ranks from the tiles"}}

Cattle went the other way this year: what packers paid for fed steers and heifers was 7.0% lower in September than a year earlier, after rising 32.2% the year before ([BLS, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WPU013101)). The herd is the smallest since 1951 ([USDA NASS](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Newsroom/printable/2026/01-30-2026.pdf)), and cattle producers' share of the retail value of Choice beef was 49.3% in September, up from 36.8% in 2021 ([USDA ERS, Meat Price Spreads](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/meat-price-spreads)). How cattle are sold, and who buys them, is in [The Name on the Label](who-owns-the-farm.html).

The department forecasts crop sales up 6.1% in 2026, to $253.0 billion, and sales of animals and animal products down 5.4%, to $287.3 billion. It forecasts direct government payments at $47.4 billion, up from $27.9 billion in 2025, and net farm income down 5.5% after inflation ([USDA ERS, farm income forecast](https://ers.usda.gov/topics/farm-economy/farm-sector-income-finances/farm-sector-income-forecast)). {{due 2027-02-10 "Has USDA ERS published its February 2027 farm income forecast? ## What you buy

Nitrogen fertilizer makers were paid 19.1% more in September than a year earlier, after a 19.9% rise the year before ([BLS, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCU325311325311)). Fertilizer has been 33% to 44% of the operating costs of growing corn since 2020 ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/charts-of-note/111221)), and natural gas can be up to 90% of the cost of making the ammonia it starts from ([Oklahoma State University Extension](https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/causes-of-fertilizer-price-volatility)). A few companies make most of it, which is in [The Name on the Label](who-owns-the-farm.html) too.

Diesel averaged $6.13 a gallon at the pump in the week of November 9, up from $3.84 a year earlier; adjusted for inflation, that is higher than 97% of weeks since 1994 ([EIA, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/GASDESW)). Diesel used on a farm for farming is exempt from the federal fuel tax of 24.4 cents a gallon included in that price, so a farm pays less, but it moves with it ([IRS, Publication 510](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p510)). The department expects total farm production expenses to rise 4.5% in 2026, to $492.8 billion ([USDA ERS, farm income forecast](https://ers.usda.gov/topics/farm-economy/farm-sector-income-finances/farm-sector-income-forecast)).

## The Fed and the operating loan

An operating loan carries the costs of seed, fertilizer, fuel and labor until the crop or the calves are sold. At commercial banks, farm operating loans averaged 6.66% in the third quarter of 2026, down 0.36 points from a year earlier but about 3.5 points higher than in 2020; machinery loans averaged 7.06% ([farmdoc daily](https://farmdocdaily.illinois.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/fdd102926.pdf)).

Land has gotten more expensive: cropland averaged $6,020 an acre in 2026, up 21.8% since 2022, while cash rent averaged $160 an acre ([Farm Policy News, on USDA's Land Values report](https://farmpolicynews.illinois.edu/2026/08/us-cropland-tops-6000-per-acre-for-first-time-in-2026/)). Rising values add to an owner's wealth on paper and raise the price of buying more land.

## Exports, the dollar and trade

About 85% of the U.S. cotton crop was exported in the 2025–26 marketing year, and 38% of the winter wheat ([Gigafact, from USDA data](https://gigafact.org/fact-briefs/is-about-80-of-the-cotton-and-40-of-the-winter-wheat-raised-in-the-united-states-exported/)). A stronger [dollar](../chart.html?id=dollar) makes those crops cost more abroad, and trade disputes reach farms through the buyers who stop buying. In December 2025, citing trade disruptions and higher production costs, the department announced $12 billion in aid, $11 billion of it for row-crop farmers; livestock was not included ([Holland & Knight](https://www.hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2025/12/usda-releases-details-of-long-awaited-farm-aid-package)). [Tariffs, and who pays](tariffs-and-who-pays.html) covers the other side of the trade fight.

## Off the farm

For most farm households, the farm isn't the main income. The department forecasts the median farm household's income at $108,460 in 2026, the median household's income from farming itself at a loss of about $467, and the median off-farm income at about $94,000 ([USDA ERS](https://ers.usda.gov/topics/farm-economy/farm-household-well-being/farm-household-income-forecast)). So the jobs report, the wage numbers and the health insurance costs on the rest of this site reach most farm families as directly as the price of corn does. [You work for a wage](you-work-for-a-wage.html) follows those numbers.

## Where you come out ahead

Crop prices are up from a year ago, and cattle prices, though down 7.0% this year, rose 32.2% the year before. Land owners have gained on paper as values rose. And a crop farm can change what it plants from one year to the next, which a factory can't. Against that: fertilizer and diesel cost far more than a year ago, operating loans cost more than they did in 2020, most of what a farm sells is priced by markets and buyers it doesn't control, and the department's forecast has net farm income falling after inflation. In 2024, 71% of farms had operating profit margins below 10%, which the department calls its high-risk zone ([USDA ERS](https://www.ers.usda.gov/media/20834/eib-299.pdf)).

## What people argue about

**Farm aid.** The department says the 2025 aid answers trade disruptions and higher costs. The American Farm Bureau Federation's president said it was needed but far short of losses he put in the tens of billions of dollars over two years ([DTN](https://www.dtnpf.com/agriculture/web/ag/news/article/2026/01/12/farm-bureau-president-says-bridge)). The Environmental Working Group, an advocacy group, estimates that because payments follow planted acres, nearly 40% of the $11 billion goes to farms growing 1,000 acres or more of commodity crops, and that the largest 5% of farms received 41% of the 2018–19 trade aid ([Environmental Working Group](https://oembed.ewg.org/news-insights/news/2026/02/trump-tariff-bailout-sends-billions-mega-farms-speeding-consolidation)).

**The companies on either side.** Whether a few seed, fertilizer and meatpacking companies squeeze farms, or bring savings that reach them, is argued in [The Name on the Label](who-owns-the-farm.html), with each side's evidence.
