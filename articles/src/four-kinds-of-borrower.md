## In one sentence

A household and a city have to pay back what they borrow and can go broke trying; a state has to balance its operating budget but has no way to go bankrupt; and the federal government borrows in a currency it issues, so the question for it isn't whether it can pay but how much debt the economy can carry before interest costs or inflation force a change.

The last row is the one most people never hear. When a recession hits, cities and states have to cut spending or raise taxes to keep their budgets balanced, just as a household tightens its belt. That pulls money out of the economy at the worst moment. The federal government is the level that can borrow more to fill the hole, which is why Congress sent aid to states in each of the last three recessions, though not in the 1980s or early 1990s.

## A household

American households owe about $19.1 trillion, and about 70% of it is mortgages. The rest is auto loans, student loans, credit cards and other debt. (These are the New York Fed's figures for the third quarter of 2026.)

A household's debt has a natural end. You borrow against a working life that will stop, so the loans have to be paid off before then. That's why the household rule of thumb is about **good debt and bad debt**: borrowing for something that lasts and pays you back over time, like a home or an education, can make sense; borrowing for everyday bills usually means trouble. The [Money Traps](money-traps.html) series is about the second kind.

## A city or county

Local governments borrow mostly by selling bonds, and they borrow for things that last: a school, a water treatment plant, a bridge. State and local governments together owe about $3.8 trillion, mostly in long-term bonds, according to the Federal Reserve. Bonds for a project are often repaid over 20 or 30 years, so the people who use the school pay for it, not just the taxpayers in the year it was built.

This is the household's good-debt rule, applied with a schedule. A 30-year bond for a 50-year water plant is a lot like a mortgage. Borrowing year after year to cover payroll is like putting groceries on a credit card.

Cities can go broke. Under Chapter 9 of the bankruptcy code, a city or county can ask a court to restructure its debts, but only if its state allows it, and only about half do. It's rare: about 700 cases since 1937, and since 2001 most filers have been small special districts, such as hospital or utility districts, rather than cities. The largest was Detroit in 2013, with about $18 billion in debt.

**Where the analogy breaks:** a city can raise taxes, which a household can't do to its employer. But the people and businesses being taxed can move away, and in many states the voters have capped how far taxes can go. California's Proposition 13, from 1978, limits the basic property tax rate to 1% of a home's assessed value, which can rise no more than 2% a year until the home is sold; Colorado's Taxpayer's Bill of Rights requires a public vote for tax increases and for most new borrowing.

## A state

States have the same constraints as cities, at a larger scale, with one big difference: there is no bankruptcy for states. The bankruptcy code doesn't cover them, so a state in trouble has to raise taxes, cut spending or negotiate with its lenders. (Puerto Rico, a territory, needed a special law from Congress in 2016 to restructure about $70 billion in debt, plus more than $40 billion in unfunded pensions.)

Almost every state has some rule requiring a balanced budget. The National Conference of State Legislatures counts 49, every state but Vermont, though the count depends on how strictly you define the rule. The rules mostly cover the operating budget, the money for salaries, services and programs, and allow borrowing for buildings and roads.

States also carry a debt that doesn't look like debt: promises to pay pensions and retiree health care. State and local pension plans are an estimated $1.13 trillion short of what they've promised in 2026, by one count, their best funding level since 2009; measured with a lower assumed rate of return, as the Federal Reserve does, the gap was about $2.4 trillion in mid-2026, roughly twice as much. Lenders watch all of it. Credit agencies rate every state's bonds; the lowest-rated state, Illinois, is in the single-A range at all three big agencies after years of pension shortfalls.

**What a recession does:** from 2008 to 2012, state and local governments cut about 700,000 jobs while they balanced their budgets, and in April 2020 they shed more than 900,000 in a single month. Federal aid covered roughly a third of state budget gaps after 2008; after 2020 it was large enough that most states' revenues recovered quickly, though many of the job cuts had already happened.

## The federal government

Washington is the borrower that breaks the household comparison. The federal government owed about $40.6 trillion in all in early November 2026. The part held by the public, the debt it sold to investors, including the Federal Reserve, was {{v debt fmt=pct0 else="97%"}} of the economy's annual output in {{v debt period else="the second quarter of 2026"}}. It doesn't have to pay it off: as bonds come due, it usually sells new ones. Because the debt is in dollars, which the U.S. issues, it can't be forced into default the way a city or a family can. The one way it could miss a payment is by its own choice: if Congress let the debt ceiling stop the Treasury from borrowing to pay bills it already owes.

That doesn't make the debt free. In the 2026 fiscal year, the federal government spent about $1.14 trillion on net interest, more than it spent on the military, and more than Medicare after the premiums people pay into it. Every dollar of interest is a dollar that can't go to anything else, and if interest rates rise, the bill rises with them. The real limits for a government like this are those interest costs and inflation: if it borrowed or printed far more than people want to lend or hold, prices would rise.

There's also a legal limit Congress sets on itself, the debt ceiling. Raising it doesn't authorize new spending; it lets the Treasury pay for spending Congress already approved. The ceiling was raised by $5 trillion in July 2025, to about $41.1 trillion, and outside analysts expect it to be reached again in 2027.

This level has its own Plain words page: [Why the Government Is Not a Household](government-is-not-a-household.html) works through the comparison in more depth, and [the deficit and the debt](what-are-the-deficit-and-the-debt.html) explains the numbers on the almanac.

## What people argue about

Most economists agree on the mechanics in the table. The argument is about how close the federal limits are.

**One side** says that because the federal government borrows in its own currency, its debt matters less than the headlines suggest. It can't run out of dollars, the question is only whether its borrowing drives up prices, and cutting spending or raising taxes in a weak economy does more harm than the debt. A St. Louis Fed economist put the core of it this way in 2021: a government like this can, in principle, roll its debt over indefinitely; it can default only if it allows it; and the debt is cheap to carry as long as interest rates stay below the economy's growth rate.

**The other side** says that debt is already high and climbing, and that "can't default" is not the same as "no cost." The Congressional Budget Office projects federal debt held by the public rising from about 101% of the economy this year, by its fiscal-year measure, to about 175% by 2056 under current law. Interest is already one of the biggest items in the budget, more borrowing can crowd out private investment, and higher rates make all of it worse. High debt also leaves less room to borrow in the next emergency, and repeated debt-ceiling fights have raised the risk of a default by choice. Moody's cut the federal credit rating in May 2025, the last of the three big agencies to do so.

Both are describing the same mechanics; they disagree about where the limits are and how soon they arrive. At the state and local level, the argument is more practical: how much to set aside for pensions now, and how much to leave to future taxpayers.
