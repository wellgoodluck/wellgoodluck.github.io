## Short version

Student loans can be a reasonable investment, but they're the largest debt most young people take on, and they're hard to escape: they generally can't be wiped out in bankruptcy. The traps are borrowing more than your likely first job can repay, letting interest pile up while you're in school, and falling behind once payments start. Federal repayment rules changed substantially on July 1, 2026, so advice from even a year or two ago may be out of date.

## The trap in one sentence

You sign for the loan at 18, and you find out what it costs at 23.

## How it works

**Federal loans versus private loans.** Federal student loans come from the U.S. Department of Education, have fixed rates set each year by law, and come with repayment options tied to your income. Private loans come from banks and other lenders, can have variable rates, and usually have far fewer options if you struggle to pay. Most advisers suggest using federal loans first.

**[This year's federal rates](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/fsa-print/publication/1007224)**, fixed for the life of loans first paid out between July 1, 2026, and June 30, 2027:

**Interest while you're in school.** On subsidized undergraduate loans, the government pays the interest while you're enrolled. On unsubsidized loans, interest starts building the day the money is paid out, even though you don't have to make payments yet.

**Repaying since July 1, 2026.** Anyone who takes out a new federal loan (or consolidates) on or after that date chooses between two plans:

- **The Tiered Standard Plan:** [fixed payments over 10 to 25 years](https://library.nclc.org/article/major-july-changes-federal-student-loan-repayment), depending on how much you owe. Under $25,000 is 10 years; $25,000 to $49,999 is 15 years; longer terms apply to larger balances.
- **The Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP):** [payments set at 1% to 10% of your income](https://thecollegeinvestor.com/79015/how-the-repayment-assistance-plan-rap-works/), depending on your income level, minus $50 a month for each dependent, with a $10 minimum. If your payment doesn't cover the month's interest, the unpaid interest is waived, so your balance doesn't grow as long as you pay on time. Any remaining balance is forgiven after 30 years of qualifying payments.

Borrowers with older loans keep some existing options for now. The SAVE plan has ended, and borrowers who were in it were given 90 days to pick a new plan, or be placed in one.

## A worked example

**Interest that builds before you graduate.** You borrow $7,500 a year in unsubsidized loans for four years at 6.52%, with no payments until six months after graduation. By the time repayment starts, about $5,900 in interest has built up on top of the $30,000 you borrowed. (This assumes each year's loan is paid out at the start of the school year.)

The longer plan lowers the payment by about $79 a month and costs about $6,200 more. You can always pay more than the required amount to finish sooner.

**What RAP might look like.** With an income of $45,000 and no dependents, RAP payments would be 4% of income: $1,800 a year, or $150 a month. That's less than the roughly $163 of interest a $30,000 loan builds each month, so the whole payment goes to interest and the government waives the rest. RAP also takes up to $50 a month off the principal on top of your payment. It's affordable, but the balance shrinks slowly. RAP is built to keep payments manageable, not to pay the loan off quickly.

## How to avoid it

**Borrow with your likely first salary in mind.** A common rule of thumb is to keep total student debt below what you expect to earn in your first year of work. [The federal College Scorecard](https://collegescorecard.ed.gov/) shows typical earnings and debt for graduates of specific programs at specific schools.

**Take subsidized loans before unsubsidized ones, and federal before private.**

**Pay the interest while you're in school if you can.** Even small payments keep the balance from growing before you've started repaying.

**Don't ignore the loan.** If you can't afford the payment, contact your servicer and switch to an income-based plan before you miss payments. Missed payments are reported to the credit bureaus (see [how credit scores work](how-credit-scores-work.html)), and federal loans go into default after about nine months without a payment.

**Know the cost of default.** [More than 9.3 million federal borrowers were in default as of June 30, 2026](https://fsapartners.ed.gov/knowledge-center/library/electronic-announcements/2026-09-22/federal-student-aid-posts-updated-reports-fsa-data-center). Defaulted loans can lead to the government taking tax refunds and up to 15% of wages, without a court order. The Education Department [paused those involuntary collections in January 2026](https://advorahq.com/student-loan-wage-garnishment/) and had announced no restart date as of October, though [officials have signaled collections may restart](https://aascu.org/news/aascu-federal-highlights-october-2026/); the pause is not forgiveness, and collections can resume after written notice.

**Look into public service forgiveness if it fits.** Public Service Loan Forgiveness can cancel remaining federal debt for people who work full time for government or qualifying nonprofits and make ten years of qualifying payments. [RAP payments count toward it](https://library.nclc.org/article/major-july-changes-federal-student-loan-repayment); Tiered Standard payments don't.

## The bigger picture

Federal student lending has swung back and forth in recent years. The 2025 budget law created RAP and the Tiered Standard Plan, ended Grad PLUS loans for new borrowers, and [capped graduate borrowing at $20,500 a year](https://www.npr.org/2025/12/23/nx-s1-5630504/2026-federal-loans-student-changes-save-plan) ($50,000 for professional programs like law and medicine; [which programs count is being fought in court](https://www.ncsl.org/news/details/new-rules-take-effect-for-student-loan-borrowers-and-universities)) and Parent PLUS borrowing at $65,000 per student over a lifetime. Undergraduate limits didn't change. The earlier SAVE plan, which had offered lower payments and faster forgiveness, was blocked by courts and has been shut down.

**Supporters of the changes** argue that unlimited graduate and parent borrowing let schools raise prices without consequence, that the new limits and simpler plans will hold down costs and protect taxpayers, and that RAP still protects low earners with small payments and an interest waiver.

**Critics** argue that the borrowing caps will push students toward private loans with fewer protections, that 30 years to forgiveness is too long, that new borrowers no longer have a $0 payment for very low incomes, and that ending unemployment and economic hardship deferments for new loans made starting July 1, 2027, will mean more defaults.

These rules are still being phased in and may change again. Check StudentAid.gov for the current details before you borrow or choose a plan. This page reflects federal law as of November 9, 2026.
