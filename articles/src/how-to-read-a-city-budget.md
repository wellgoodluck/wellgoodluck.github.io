## The short version

A city budget isn't one pot of money. It's a set of separate accounts, called funds, and most of the money in most of them is spoken for: water bills pay for the water system, a gas-tax share pays for streets, a grant pays for what the grant says. The one account a council has real choices about is the general fund, which pays for police, fire, parks and the city's offices, and in most cities is supported mainly by property and sales taxes. So the headline total ("City adopts $90 million budget, up 12%") usually mixes together things that have little to do with each other, such as a one-time road project, a water rate change and the year's raises. To see what's really changing, find the general fund, compare it with what was actually spent last year, separate one-time money from money that repeats, adjust for inflation and population, and check the city's reserves and its promises that don't show up in this year's numbers. Budgets adopted in 2026 and 2027 face a particular test: the last of the federal pandemic aid has to be spent by December 31, 2026.

## The analogy

Think of a family with several bank accounts. There's the household checking account for groceries and the electric bill. There's a college fund a grandparent set up that can only pay tuition. There's a rental house with its own account, where the tenant's rent pays the mortgage and the repairs. And there's a savings account for emergencies. If you added all four together and said "this family's spending went up 20%," you'd be right, and you'd have learned nothing. Maybe the rental needed a new roof. Maybe tuition came due. The checking account is the one that tells you how the family is doing, and the savings account tells you how long it could ride out a bad year.

## What's in the document

**Funds.** Governments keep their books in separate funds, because much of their money comes with legal strings. The common ones:

- **The general fund:** the main operating account, and the one with the most local choice in it.
- **Special revenue funds:** money that by law or by grant can only be spent on one thing, such as a state share of the gas tax for streets, a hotel tax for tourism, or a federal grant.
- **Enterprise funds:** services run like businesses and paid for by the people who use them, most often water, sewer, trash and sometimes an airport or golf course. Their rates are supposed to cover their costs.
- **Capital project funds:** money set aside for building or replacing things that last decades, often from borrowing.
- **Debt service funds:** money set aside to repay bonds.

**Operating and capital.** The operating budget pays for the year: salaries, fuel, contracts, utilities. The capital budget pays for things that last, and many cities publish a separate multiyear capital improvement plan listing projects for the next five or more years. A big road or water project can make the total budget jump in one year and fall the next, with no change in how the city runs day to day.

**The budget and the audit.** The budget is a plan, adopted before the year starts, usually after at least one public hearing. What actually happened is in the city's annual audited financial statements, in larger cities usually the ACFR (annual comprehensive financial report), published several months after the year ends. Most cities' fiscal years start on July 1, but some start on October 1 or January 1, so "the 2026 budget" can mean different 12 months in different places.

## Where the money comes from

It varies by state, because states decide which taxes their cities and counties may levy and how high. Nationally, for all kinds of local government together:

- **Property tax** is the largest local tax by far, about [72% of local tax revenue](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/state/local-tax-revenue-local-sales-taxes-local-income-taxes/) in 2021, and [nearly half (48%) of local governments' own revenue](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/how-do-state-and-local-property-taxes-work) in 2021. That share ranges from more than three-quarters in five New England states and New Jersey to [19% in Alabama](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/how-do-state-and-local-property-taxes-work), where local sales taxes do more of the work. The site's [property tax series](how-a-property-tax-rate-is-set.html) explains how the rate gets set.
- **Sales taxes, charges and fees** fill in much of the rest: local sales taxes where a state allows them, plus charges such as water bills, permit fees and recreation fees.
- **Aid from the state and federal governments:** in 2021, [31% of local general revenue came from the states and 7% from the federal government](https://taxpolicycenter.org/briefing-book/what-are-sources-revenue-state-and-local-governments), much of the state share for schools.

A city that depends on sales tax feels a slowdown within months; one that depends on property tax feels it a year or two later, after reassessments catch up. [What a Town Gets From a Drive-Thru](what-a-town-gets-from-a-drive-thru.html) follows how that difference shapes which businesses a city courts.

## Where it goes

Most of a general fund is people: officers, firefighters, crews, clerks, and their health insurance and pensions. In the National League of Cities' 2025 survey of city finance officers, [public safety (police and fire) took about 60% of budgeted general fund spending](https://www.nlc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025-City-Fiscal-Conditions-ReportwebA.pdf) in fiscal 2025, up from 54% two years earlier; recreation and culture took about a tenth to an eighth. Because so much is salaries and benefits, the biggest decision in many budgets is the raise, and it repeats every year after.

## Seven questions to ask

**1. Which fund?** Before reacting to a total, find the general fund. If the headline total rose 12% and the general fund rose 4%, the story is somewhere else: a capital project, a utility rate change, a grant.

**2. Budget or actual?** Compare this year's budget with what was actually spent last year, not only with last year's budget. Some cities budget generously and underspend every year; others budget tight and amend midyear.

**3. One-time or recurring?** A one-time windfall (a land sale, a grant, a legal settlement, leftover reserves) can pay for one-time things. Using it for recurring costs, such as new positions, builds a gap into next year's budget. Many budget documents label which is which; if not, ask.

**4. Restricted or flexible?** Water money can't pay for police, and a street grant can't pay for a park. When someone says "the city has $10 million sitting in the bank," the first question is which fund, and whether that money is restricted.

**5. Per resident, after inflation?** A budget that grew 6% in a town whose population grew 2% while prices rose 3% grew about 1% per resident in real terms. The site's [inflation page](what-is-inflation.html) and the almanac's [prices chart](../chart.html?id=cpi) give the second number.

**6. How much is in reserve?** The general fund's unrestricted fund balance is the city's cushion for a bad year, a disaster or a lawsuit. The Government Finance Officers Association recommends that a general-purpose government keep [at least two months of regular general fund revenues or spending](https://www.gfoa.org/materials/fund-balance-guidelines-for-the-general-fund) unrestricted, about 17%, and says many should keep more depending on their risks. A balance well below that leaves little room; one far above it raises the question of what it's for.

**7. What's promised but not in this year's numbers?** Bonds that must be repaid, pension and retiree health promises, and repairs that have been put off all show up in later budgets. The audited annual report lists the debt and the pension liability; the capital plan shows what's been deferred.

The 18% is real, but four-fifths of the $7.2 million increase is a one-time repaving project paid for by a grant ($4.6 million) and the water rate increase ($1.1 million), which water customers pay and which generally stays in the water fund. Next year's headline may well say the budget fell, for the same reason.

## Why budgets feel tight right now

The American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 sent $350 billion to state and local governments. They had to [commit it by the end of 2024](https://www.nlc.org/article/2025/09/24/how-localities-are-planning-for-the-end-of-the-american-rescue-plan-act/) and must [spend it by December 31, 2026](https://canons.sog.unc.edu/2026/01/2026-the-final-countdown-has-begun-for-arp-slfrf/), or [return what's left](https://www.ey.com/en_us/insights/forensic-integrity-services/navigating-the-expenditure-deadline). Programs started with that money now need another source or an end date. In a 2025 National League of Cities survey, [69% of respondents said the end of the money would hurt their budgets](https://www.nlc.org/article/2025/09/24/how-localities-are-planning-for-the-end-of-the-american-rescue-plan-act/). In the league's annual fiscal survey, [general fund spending, adjusted for inflation, grew 7.5% in fiscal 2024 and was budgeted to grow 0.7% in fiscal 2025](https://www.nlc.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/2025-City-Fiscal-Conditions-ReportwebA.pdf). Of the finance officers surveyed, 52% said they were better able to meet their city's needs in fiscal 2025 than a year earlier, down from 64% in the previous survey, and 45% were optimistic about fiscal 2026. Watch question 3 in any budget adopted this winter: programs that were one-time federal money last year show up as recurring local costs, or as cuts.

## Where to find it

Most cities post the adopted budget and the annual financial report on their websites, usually under Finance. Many states' auditors or comptrollers publish every local government's audited numbers in one place. Budgets that have won the Government Finance Officers Association's Distinguished Budget Presentation Award follow a common format that's easier to read. Many states require at least one public hearing before a budget is adopted, and the proposed budget is a public record before the vote.

## Where the analogy breaks

A family can't raise its income by vote; a council can, within the limits its state sets, by raising a tax rate or a fee. A family's college fund was set up by one grandparent; a city's restrictions come from state law, bond covenants, grant agreements and voter-approved measures, and changing them can take a lawsuit or an election. And a family's savings belong to the family, while a city's reserves came from its residents, which is why how much to keep is a public argument.

## What people argue about

**How big a reserve should be.** One side, including rating agencies and finance officers, says a healthy fund balance earns better credit ratings and lower interest costs, lets a city ride out a recession or disaster without emergency tax increases or layoffs, and that the [GFOA's two-month figure is a floor, not a target](https://www.gfoa.org/materials/fund-balance-guidelines-for-the-general-fund). Taxpayer groups and some council members say large balances mean residents were taxed more than the city needed, and that surpluses should go back as a lower rate or to deferred repairs rather than sit. The GFOA itself recommends that each government adopt a written fund balance policy, so the question is settled in advance rather than every budget season.

**Transfers from utility funds to the general fund.** Many cities move money each year from their water, sewer or electric funds into the general fund. Defenders say it pays for real shared costs, such as city hall staff and the use of city streets for pipes, and is like the property tax a private utility would pay. Critics say it turns a utility bill into a hidden tax, one that falls hardest on households for whom water is a large share of their spending, and some states limit it; in California, voters required in 1996 (Proposition 218) that property-related fees [not exceed the cost of the service](https://lao.ca.gov/1996/120196_prop_218/understanding_prop218_1296.html), which the Legislative Analyst said would bar moving such fee revenue into a city's general fund.

**What a budget should start from.** Most budgets start from last year's and adjust, which is predictable but tends to carry old spending forward unexamined. Some cities try budgeting that starts each program from zero, or ranks every service by priority, which forces the questions but takes far more staff time and can turn every line into an annual fight.
