## Short version

Money you're saving for a goal or an emergency belongs somewhere safe that you can reach in a day or two: usually a savings account at a bank or credit union, separate from checking. Up to $250,000 is insured by the federal government if the bank or credit union fails. The interest rate matters more than most people think. The average savings account pays well under 1%, while many online banks and credit unions pay around 4%, ten times as much, on the same insured dollars.

## The idea in one sentence

A savings account is a safe place to park money, and the rate it pays decides whether that money slowly loses ground to prices or keeps up with them.

## How it works

**Interest and APY.** The bank pays you interest for keeping money there, usually added monthly. Because each month's interest then earns interest too, banks advertise the annual percentage yield (APY), which includes that compounding. [Federal rules require banks and credit unions to state the APY](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1030.4), figured the same way, so it's the number to compare. Savings rates are variable: they can change at any time, and they tend to follow the Federal Reserve's rate up and down.

**The rate gap.** In October 2026, the FDIC's [national average savings rate was 0.38%](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/SNDR). At the same time, many online banks were paying [about 3.9% to 4.35%](https://www.bankrate.com/banking/savings/10-best-savings-account-rates/?pid=pt8) on ordinary savings accounts, close to what [3-month Treasury bills](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/DTB3) paid. Many of the largest banks [pay close to nothing](https://www.nerdwallet.com/banking/learn/average-rates-for-deposit-accounts), which pulls the average down. Both kinds of account carry the same federal insurance.

**What's insured.** The FDIC insures deposits at banks up to [$250,000 per depositor, per bank, for each ownership category](https://fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits) (an account in your name alone, a joint account and a retirement account are separate categories). The NCUA [does the same at federally insured credit unions](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage). Insurance covers checking, savings, money market deposit accounts and certificates of deposit. It [doesn't cover](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/financial-products-not-insured) stocks, bonds, mutual funds (including money market *funds*), crypto, annuities, life insurance, or what's in a safe deposit box, even when you buy them at a bank. Treasury bills and savings bonds aren't FDIC-insured either, but they're backed by the federal government itself.

**Apps that aren't banks.** Many money apps aren't banks; they hold your money at a partner bank. FDIC insurance can pass through to you, but it protects only against that bank failing, and only if the records show which dollars are yours. When Synapse, a company that connected apps to banks, went bankrupt in 2024, customers of several apps [lost access to their money](https://www.fdic.gov/system/files/2024-09/bc-npr-on-requirements-for-custodial-deposit-accounts.pdf) even though no bank failed. Many went months without it, some were [still waiting in 2025](https://abc7chicago.com/15985826), and some got back only part of it. FDIC insurance doesn't protect against an app company failing.

**Other places to keep savings.** A certificate of deposit (CD) locks in a rate for a set time, with a penalty for taking money out early. A money market deposit account is a savings account with a few check-writing features. Treasury bills, bought through TreasuryDirect or a brokerage, pay close to the Fed's rate.

**Getting to it.** Until 2020, federal rules limited most savings accounts to six online, phone or automatic withdrawals and transfers a month. The [Federal Reserve dropped that limit](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/bcreg20200424a.htm), but banks can still set their own limits and fees, so check the account terms.

**Taxes and prices.** Interest is taxable income; banks send a [Form 1099-INT](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1099-int) if you earn $10 or more in a year. And prices keep rising: consumer prices were [up 3.7% over the year to September 2026](https://www.bls.gov/news.release/archives/cpi_10142026.htm), the latest 12-month figure. At 0.38%, a savings balance loses about 3% of its buying power a year. At 4%, it roughly keeps pace (see [real vs. nominal](real-vs-nominal.html)).

## What to do with it

**Find your rate.** It's on your statement or in the app. If it starts with 0.0, you're earning almost nothing.

**Check that it's insured.** Look for "Member FDIC" or "federally insured by NCUA," and look the institution up on FDIC BankFind or NCUA's credit union locator. For an app, find out which bank holds your money.

**Keep the emergency cushion in insured savings,** not in stocks or crypto, which can be down just when you need the money ([the $400 surprise](emergency-cushion.html)).

**Compare APYs, and read the fine print:** minimum balances, monthly fees, and promotional rates that drop after a few months.

**Make it automatic.** A transfer on payday does more than a good intention.

**Know the difference between saving and investing.** Saving is for money you'll need within a few years. Money for decades away is a different decision, with risk (see [the employer match](employer-retirement-match.html)).

## What's still unsettled

After Synapse, the FDIC in 2024 proposed a rule that would require banks holding money for app companies' customers to keep daily, auditable records of who owns each dollar. As of November 2026, it [hadn't been finalized or withdrawn](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eAgendaViewRule?pubId=202510&RIN=3064-AG07); the federal regulatory agenda lists it as a long-term action with no date for a final rule.

**Supporters,** including the [state bank regulators' association](https://csbs.org/print/pdf/node/522246), argue that the rule would close the gap Synapse exposed and let customers count on the insurance the apps advertise.

**Opponents,** including the [American Fintech Council](https://fintechcouncil.org/advocacy/notice-of-proposed-rulemaking-on-recordkeeping-for-custodial-accounts--rin-3064-ag07), an industry group, argue that the proposal was rushed, relied on too little data, and would put heavy costs on banks and apps, and that it should be withdrawn.

These rules may change. This page reflects them as of November 12, 2026.
