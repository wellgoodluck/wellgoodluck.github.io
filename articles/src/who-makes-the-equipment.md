## The short version

The names on American air conditioners, furnaces, water heaters and refrigerators outnumber the companies that make them. Carrier, Bryant, Payne, Heil and Day & Night are all Carrier. Goodman, Amana's air conditioners and Daikin are all Daikin, a Japanese company. York and Coleman are now made by Germany's Bosch. A handful of makers has supplied most central heating and cooling equipment for decades; what changed is who owns them, as American makers were bought by Japanese and German ones. Prices went two ways. What makers charge for heating and cooling equipment has risen about 60% since January 2020; the makers cite materials, supply chains, a required change of refrigerant and tariffs, and a lawsuit filed this year alleges that they also coordinated price increases, which those that have commented deny. Kitchen appliances got cheaper: their consumer prices are lower than in 1997, while LG and Samsung grew into the largest sellers. Ductless mini-splits, a much smaller part of the market, are led by Asian makers' brands and joint ventures, among them China's Midea and Gree, and are sometimes sold under other companies' names. Water heaters are the most concentrated of the three: three makers sell most of them.

## The analogy

The cereal aisle. Dozens of boxes, each with its own name, mascot and price, nearly all made by a few companies. Shoppers who switch brands to get away from one maker often end up buying from the same one. The variety is real, in flavor, price and box size; the choice of maker is narrower than it looks.

The boxes are the brands on the equipment. The companies behind the shelf are a handful of manufacturers, many of them headquartered abroad.

Figures below are as of early November 2026.

## One company, many names

Some of the most common brand families:

- **Carrier** (Florida): Carrier, Bryant, Payne, Heil, Day & Night and others ([Carrier, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783180/000178318026000008/carr-20251231.htm); [ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/152239-hvac-wholesale-distributor-adds-two-carrier-brands-to-portfolio)).
- **Daikin** (Japan): Goodman, Amana heating and cooling, and Daikin, since Daikin bought Goodman for $3.7 billion in 2012 ([Daikin, via Kyodo](https://kyodonewsprwire.jp/release/201211018035)). Amana refrigerators are a different company's: Goodman bought Amana in 1997 and sold the appliance business to Maytag in 2001, keeping the name for heating and cooling ([Amana](https://amana-hac.com/about-us/amana-history)).
- **Bosch** (Germany): York, Coleman, Luxaire and Hitachi-branded residential lines, since Bosch bought Johnson Controls' residential and light-commercial heating and cooling business and the Johnson Controls–Hitachi joint venture, in a deal valued at $8.1 billion that closed in mid-2025; the York and Hitachi names are licensed ([Johnson Controls](https://www.johnsoncontrols.com/media-center/news/press-releases/2024/07/23/johnson-controls-to-sell-residential-and-light-commercial-hvac-businesses); [ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/165008-accelerating-our-growth-bosch-closes-jci-hvac-deal)). Bosch also owns BSH, maker of Bosch, Thermador and Gaggenau kitchen appliances, outright since buying out Siemens in 2015 ([Bosch](https://www.bosch-press.nl/pressportal/nl/en/press-release-621.html); [BSH](https://www.bsh-group.com/about-bsh/company-portrait/)).
- **Paloma** (Japan): Rheem and Ruud, plus Nortek Global HVAC, bought in 2024, which makes furnaces and air conditioners sold under Intertherm and Miller and, under license, the Frigidaire, Maytag and Gibson names ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/155152-rheem-to-acquire-nortek-global-hvac); [Nortek, 2007 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1216596/000121659608000017/dec3107_ntk.htm)). A furnace with a Frigidaire or Maytag badge comes from neither of the appliance companies that use those names in kitchens.
- **Trane Technologies** (incorporated in Ireland, North American headquarters in North Carolina): Trane and American Standard ([Trane Technologies, 2020 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1466258/000146625821000027/tt-20201231.htm); [2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1466258/000162828026005731/tt-20251231.htm)).
- **Lennox** (Texas): Lennox, sold directly to contractors, and Armstrong Air, Ducane, AirEase and Concord, sold through distributors ([Lennox, 2024 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1069202/000162828025004859/lii-20241231.htm)).
- **Whirlpool** (Michigan): Whirlpool and KitchenAid, plus Maytag, JennAir and Amana, which came with its March 2006 purchase of Maytag ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2006/March/06_at_187.html); [Whirlpool](https://whirlpool.mediaroom.com/2006-03-31-Whirlpool-Corporation-Completes-Acquisition-of-Maytag-Corporation)).
- **Haier** (China): GE Appliances, bought from General Electric for $5.6 billion in 2016 ([GE](https://www.ge.com/news/press-releases/ge-completes-sale-appliances-business-haier)).
- **A. O. Smith** (Wisconsin): A. O. Smith, State, American and Lochinvar water heaters (see below).

## Heating and cooling: the same few makers, new owners

A few makers have long supplied most central air conditioners, furnaces and heat pumps. In its annual reports Goodman, then an independent American company, estimated that the five largest domestic makers had about 80% of 2008 unit sales and the six largest about 90% ([Goodman, 2008 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1314655/000119312509053772/d10k.htm); [2009 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1314655/000119312510055049/d10k.htm)). Watsco, the largest distributor, now says eight makers ship about 90% of the units sold in the U.S. each year: Carrier, Daikin, Rheem, Trane, York, Lennox, Nortek, which Rheem now owns, and Mitsubishi Electric Trane, a joint venture of Mitsubishi Electric and Trane ([Watsco, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/105016/000119312526082486/wso-20251231.htm); [Mitsubishi Electric Trane](https://www.mitsubishicomfort.com/about-us)).

The bigger change is who owns them:

- **2012:** Daikin bought Goodman.
- **2023:** Blackstone bought control of Copeland, which it called "the market leader in supplying critical components" such as compressors, from Emerson at a $14 billion valuation, and bought the rest in 2024 ([HPAC Engineering](https://www.hpac.com/technology/article/21267262/blackstone-completes-14b-acquisition-of-emerson-climate-technologies); [Emerson](https://www.emerson.com/en-us/news/2024/emerson-completes-sale-to-blackstone)).
- **2024:** Carrier bought Germany's Viessmann Climate Solutions for about €12 billion ([Carrier, via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783180/000095014223001205/eh230352031_8k.htm); [closing](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783180/000114036124000034/ex20016935_ex99-1.htm)) and sold its fire, security and commercial-refrigeration businesses ([Carrier, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1783180/000178318026000008/carr-20251231.htm)); Rheem bought Nortek Global HVAC; Lennox and Samsung formed a joint venture for ductless systems ([Lennox](https://investor.lennox.com/news-releases/news-release-details/samsung-and-lennox-announce-establishment-joint-venture-ductless)).
- **2025:** Bosch's purchase of the York business closed, and Paloma's Rheem bought Japan's Fujitsu General, maker of ductless systems ([Cooling Post](https://www.coolingpost.com/world-news/paloma-completes-fujitsu-general-tender-offer/)).

Of the makers Watsco names, Carrier and Lennox are American companies; Trane Technologies is incorporated in Ireland; Daikin and Rheem's owner Paloma are Japanese; York's owner Bosch is German; and the Mitsubishi venture is half Japanese. Ownership moved abroad more than the factories did: in 2008 domestic makers supplied more than 97% of U.S. shipments ([Goodman, 2008 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1314655/000119312509053772/d10k.htm)), and Watsco says Carrier and Rheem now assemble much of their equipment in Mexico ([Watsco, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/105016/000119312526082486/wso-20251231.htm)).

## Ductless: a different set of names

Ductless systems, often called mini-splits, pair an outdoor unit with one or more wall- or ceiling-mounted units inside, with no ducts. They're used in houses that never had ducts, in additions and converted garages, and for single rooms. They're still a small part of what's installed: in 2020, the most recent federal survey, about 1.8 million of 123.5 million American homes used a ductless heat pump as their main air conditioning, against 82.7 million with central air conditioners or central heat pumps and 21.4 million with window or wall units ([EIA, Residential Energy Consumption Survey](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2020/hc/pdf/HC%207.1.pdf)). Their share of sales is larger: in 2017 a Fujitsu executive put ductless at 10% of the residential market and a Trane executive at about 10% of the industry, and ACHR News reported double-digit annual sales growth ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/135471-improved-efficiency-connectivity-propel-residential-hvac-ductless-market)); in 2024 the research firm BSRIA estimated that U.S. mini-split sales grew 4.5% in units, against 10% for ducted systems ([BSRIA](https://www.bsria.com/uk/news/article/global_hvac_market_outlook_20242025_highlights_/)). We found no free public count of U.S. ductless sales; the industry's monthly shipment figures cover central air conditioners and heat pumps ([AHRI, via the International Institute of Refrigeration](https://iifiir.org/en/news/united-states-december-2025-heating-and-cooling-equipment-shipment-data)), and Watsco's 90% figure doesn't say whether it includes ductless. In 2020 ductless was most common as a main system in the Northeast, with about 770,000 homes, and the Pacific states, with about 410,000; the Mountain South (Arizona, Nevada and New Mexico) had about 60,000 ([EIA](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2020/hc/pdf/HC%207.7.pdf); [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/data/2020/hc/pdf/HC%207.8.pdf); [EIA, definitions](https://www.eia.gov/consumption/residential/terminology.php)).

Most of the big names in ductless are Asian companies or joint ventures with them:

- **Mitsubishi Electric** sells in the U.S. through Mitsubishi Electric Trane, a 50-50 joint venture with Trane since 2018 ([Cooling Post](https://www.coolingpost.com/world-news/mitsubishi-electric-trane-now-in-operation/)).
- **Samsung** and Lennox formed Samsung Lennox in 2024, 50.1% Samsung, selling under Samsung's name and as "Lennox powered by Samsung" ([Lennox](https://investor.lennox.com/news-releases/news-release-details/samsung-and-lennox-announce-establishment-joint-venture-ductless)).
- **Fujitsu General**, a longtime ductless brand ([Fujitsu General America](https://www.fujitsugeneral.com/shared/us/pdf-fcus-pr-2016-fujitsu-general-america-names-matt-peterson-president-01.pdf)), was bought in 2025 by Paloma Rheem Holdings and has traded as GENERAL Inc. since January 2026 ([Cooling Post](https://www.coolingpost.com/world-news/fujitsu-general-to-trade-as-general-inc/)).
- **Carrier** bought substantially all of Toshiba's stake in their joint venture, Toshiba Carrier, in 2022 ([Carrier](https://corporate.carrier.com/news/news-articles/202208_carrier-completes-acquisition-toshibas-global-residential-light-commercial-hvac-business.html)), and in 2017 formed Carrier Midea North America, a residential ductless joint venture with China's Midea in which Carrier holds the majority ([Carrier](https://www.carrier.com/carrier/en/worldwide/news/news-article/carrier--midea-launch-residential-ductless-hvac-joint-venture-in-north-america.html)).
- **Bosch**'s 2025 purchase included all of the Johnson Controls–Hitachi venture, whose products include ductless systems, and a long-term license to the Hitachi name; Hitachi kept a ductless factory in Japan ([Bosch](https://www.bosch-homecomfortgroup.com/en/news-and-stories/news/question-and-answers/); [Johnson Controls, via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/833444/000083344425000058/a8-k2025x08x01ex991.htm)).
- **Daikin** sells ductless systems under its own name ([Daikin](https://daikincomfort.com/products/heating-cooling/single-split-ductless)).

**Midea and Gree.** Two Chinese companies sell air conditioners under their own names and others'. Midea cites Euromonitor research ranking it the world's largest air-conditioner company by retail volume in 2024 and 2025 ([Midea](https://www.midea.com/my/news/why-midea-is-considered-the-no-1-air-conditioner-brand-globally)). Listed in Shenzhen and, since 2024, Hong Kong ([Reuters](https://www.reuters.com/business/midea-group-shares-rise-95-hong-kong-after-4-bln-listing-2024-09-17/)), it had 458.5 billion yuan in revenue in 2025, about $64 billion at that year's average exchange rate, with about 43% from outside China ([China Daily](https://www.chinadaily.com.cn/a/202604/01/WS69cc7bd4a310d6866eb4118e.html); [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/releases/g5a/current/default.htm)). In 2016 it bought 80.1% of Toshiba's home-appliance business, with a 40-year license to the Toshiba name ([Japan Times](https://www.japantimes.co.jp/news/2016/03/30/business/corporate-business/toshiba-agrees-sell-white-goods-unit-chinas-midea-%C2%A553-7-billion/)). Gree, based in Zhuhai, calls itself the world's largest specialized air-conditioner company ([GREE Comfort](https://www.greecomfort.com/about-us/)). Its mini-splits are sold in the U.S. as GREE Comfort through Tradewinds Distributing, a Watsco subsidiary that is their exclusive U.S. distributor ([GREE Comfort](https://www.greecomfort.com/news-and-events/tradewinds-is-pleased-to-announce-a-partnership-with-tom-barrow-company/)), and Watsco lists Gree among its significant suppliers ([Watsco, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/105016/000119312526082486/wso-20251231.htm)). In 2021 two Gree companies agreed to pay $91 million in what the Justice Department called the first corporate criminal enforcement action under the Consumer Product Safety Act, for delaying for months reports that their dehumidifiers were defective and could catch fire ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/gree-appliance-companies-charged-failure-report-dangerous-dehumidifiers-and-agree-91-million)).

**The maker behind the name.** Recalls show how one factory can supply many brands. Gree's 2013 dehumidifier recall covered units sold under 12 names, among them Frigidaire, Kenmore and Gree ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2013/gree-recalls-12-brands-of-dehumidifiers)); Midea's 2016 recall, about 3.4 million units in the U.S. and 850,000 in Canada, covered about 50 brand names, including GE and Frigidaire ([CPSC](https://www.cpsc.gov/Recalls/2017/Dehumidifiers-Made-by-Midea-Recalled)). Those were dehumidifiers, not mini-splits, and we found no public count of how often the name on a mini-split belongs to a company other than its maker. MRCOOL, a Kentucky company founded by the Ingram family whose line includes do-it-yourself mini-splits ([Kentucky Cabinet for Economic Development](https://newkentuckyhome.ky.gov/Newsroom/NewsPage/20251104_MRCOOL)), said in 2018 that tariffs and shipping costs had led it to study making ductless units in Kentucky "rather than outsourcing it to overseas producers"; it didn't name its makers ([MRCOOL, via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/mrcool-exploring-domestic-manufacturing-expansion-in-kentucky-opportunity-zone-300752135.html)).

**Tariffs.** Split ductless systems made in China have paid an added tariff under Section 301 of the Trade Act since September 2018: 10% at first, 25% since May 2019 ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2019/05/09/2019-09681/notice-of-modification-of-section-301-action-chinas-acts-policies-and-practices-related-to); [Customs ruling N324892, via TariffLens](https://www.tarifflens.ai/rulings/N324892)).

## Equipment prices: up, and why

The producer price index for air-conditioning, refrigeration and heating equipment, which tracks what makers charge for both home and commercial equipment, rose from 130.6 in January 2000 to 204.2 in January 2020, about 56% in twenty years. It reached 293.1 in January 2024, up 44% in four years, and 327.6 in September 2026, another 12% ([BLS, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/PCU333415333415)). What makers charge for major kitchen and laundry appliances rose far less: about 13% from January 2000 to January 2020 and about 25% since ([BLS, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/WPU1241)). The causes the makers and trade press cite differ by period:

- **2020–2023:** raw materials, supply-chain disruption, strong demand and new federal efficiency standards that took effect in 2023 ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/163729-equipment-prices-keep-rising-as-r-410a-inventory-decreases)).
- **Refrigerant.** Under a federal rule phasing down refrigerants that warm the climate, makers stopped producing home systems using the long-standard R-410A after 2024. The Environmental Protection Agency first gave installers until the start of 2026 to use up existing stock, then in May 2026 dropped that deadline, so equipment made before 2025 can be installed until it runs out ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/166226-epa-removes-r-410a-installation-deadline); [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/hfcs/technology-transitions-hfc-restrictions-sector)). The replacements need redesigned equipment. Carrier said its new equipment would cost about 10% more at first, rising to 15–20% over two years; Lennox said more than 10%, and Trane high single digits ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/163729-equipment-prices-keep-rising-as-r-410a-inventory-decreases)). The new refrigerant ran short in 2025, and Honeywell, a major supplier, added a 42% surcharge ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/164475-honeywell-announces-42-surcharge-on-r-454b)).
- **Metals and tariffs.** Aluminum cost 34% more, copper 21% and steel 15% in March 2026 than a year earlier ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/166089-ppi-report-signals-renewed-hvac-cost-increases-amid-global-uncertainty)). Carrier expected about $800 million in higher costs in 2026, about half from tariffs, and announced an 8% increase in residential prices for late in the year ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/166938-carrier-residential-hvac-rebounds-but-price-hikes-loom)).

Sales fell meanwhile: shipments of central air conditioners and air-source heat pumps dropped 20% in 2025, air conditioners 26% and heat pumps 12%, partly because buyers had bought ahead of the refrigerant change in 2024 ([Contracting Business](https://www.contractingbusiness.com/industry-news/news/55358865/heat-pumps-hold-steady-air-conditioners-slide-breaking-down-ahris-2025-year-end-shipment-numbers)).

**The lawsuit.** In March 2026 a homeowner sued seven makers, Bosch, Carrier, Trane, Daikin, Lennox, Rheem and AAON, in federal court in Michigan, alleging that they coordinated price increases through public statements, industry meetings and shared data, and charged about 8% more than costs justified since 2020. Carrier and Trane called the claims baseless; Bosch declined to comment ([Facilities Dive](https://www.facilitiesdive.com/news/consumer-sues-manufacturers-for-conspiring-to-inflate-hvac-prices/816056/)). The case has since been combined with others, and an amended complaint was filed in July ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/166440-amended-complaint-filed-in-hvac-price-fixing-lawsuit); [amended complaint](https://www.locklaw.com/wp-content/uploads/HVAC-Amended-CPT.pdf)). ## Appliances: the other direction

Kitchen and laundry appliances went the opposite way. The consumer price index for major appliances stood at 87.8 in September 2026 against 100 in December 1997, a drop of about 12%, while consumer prices overall more than doubled ([BLS, major appliances](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SEHK01); [BLS, all items, via FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/CPIAUCNS)). In September 2026 major appliances cost 2.3% less than a year earlier, while all items cost 3.7% more (same sources).

**Who sells them.** When the Justice Department reviewed the Whirlpool–Maytag merger in 2006, it noted that newer brands such as LG and Samsung had "quickly established themselves" ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2006/March/06_at_187.html)). In the second quarter of 2026 LG had the largest share of U.S. unit sales of major appliances, 19.6%, followed by GE at 17.1%, Whirlpool at 12.5% and Samsung at 11.4%, according to the market researcher OpenBrand; its Whirlpool figure covers the Whirlpool brand only ([OpenBrand](https://openbrand.com/newsroom/blog/us-major-appliance-industry-market-share-trends-rankings-infographic)).

**Two mergers, two outcomes.** In 2006 the Justice Department let Whirlpool buy Maytag, citing competition from LG and Samsung imports, the bargaining power of big retailers, and cost savings the companies documented ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2006/March/06_at_187.html)). A later study by three economists, one of them at the FTC, found that for models introduced after the merger, prices rose about 14% for Whirlpool dryers and about 7% for Maytag dishwashers, with no price effect for washers or refrigerators; the merged company's market share fell in all four ([Ashenfelter, Hosken and Weinberg, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17476.pdf)). In 2015 the department sued to block Electrolux's purchase of GE's appliance business, arguing that it would leave a "duopoly" selling ranges, cooktops and wall ovens to home builders and other large buyers; the companies abandoned the deal after a four-week trial ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/electrolux-and-general-electric-abandon-anticompetitive-appliance-transaction-after-four-week)), and GE sold the business to Haier the next year.

**Under strain.** Whirlpool calls itself the only major U.S.-based manufacturer of kitchen and laundry appliances, and says about 80% of the appliances it sells here are made here ([Whirlpool, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/106640/000010664026000009/whr-20251231.htm)); GE Appliances is based in Louisville but owned by Haier. Whirlpool's adjusted earnings per share fell by about half in 2025 ([Whirlpool](https://whirlpool.mediaroom.com/2026-01-28-Whirlpool-Corporation-Announces-Fourth-Quarter-and-Full-Year-Results-Provides-2026-Guidance)), and in October 2026 it cut its 2026 forecast to an adjusted operating margin of 2.0–2.2%, from 5.5–5.8% in January, citing weak demand, higher steel and resin costs and tariffs ([Investing.com, earnings call](https://www.investing.com/news/transcripts/earnings-call-transcript-whirlpool-q3-2026-miss-sparks-premarket-drop-93CH-4975793)). Since June 2025, imported refrigerators, washers, dryers, dishwashers, ranges and freezers have paid a 50% tariff on the value of their steel ([Geodis](https://www.geodis.com/us-en/commerce-expands-50-section-232-tariffs-steel-based-household-appliances-starting-june-23)). Electrolux, owner of Frigidaire, agreed in April 2026 to form North American ventures with China's Midea for refrigerators and laundry, which began operating in September and October ([Electrolux](https://www.electroluxgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2026/04/electrolux-Press-release-Midea-partnership-April-23-2026-Eng-final-260423.pdf); [Electrolux, third-quarter 2026 report](https://www.electroluxgroup.com/en/electrolux-group-interim-report-q3-2026-47822/)).

## Water heaters: three makers

Water heaters have long been the most concentrated of the three. When A. O. Smith bought State Industries in 2001, it said it and three competitors supplied more than 90% of the market ([A. O. Smith, via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000091142/000089706902000168/pdm263a.txt)). It went on to buy GSW, parent of American Water Heater, in 2006 and Lochinvar in 2011 ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/101400-april-26-2006-a-o-smith-completes-acquisition-of-gsw); [A. O. Smith, via SEC](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000091142/000119312511236275/dex991.htm)). In a 2018 lawsuit against Rheem over advertising, A. O. Smith said it, Rheem and the privately held Bradford White sold about 88% of the water heaters in the market ([BizTimes](https://biztimes.com/battle-heating-up-between-a-o-smith-rheem-over-top-water-heater-label/)). In 1979 the Justice Department sued six water-heater makers, among them Rheem, State, Bradford-White and A. O. Smith, alleging price fixing; the case was settled by a consent judgment in 1980 ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/atr/case/us-v-rheem-manufacturing-co-et-al)). Tankless water heaters, a smaller part of the market, are sold by Rheem and A. O. Smith as well as Navien of South Korea and Rinnai and Noritz of Japan ([PHCP Pros](https://www.phcppros.com/articles/15598-2022-tankless-water-heater-report-no-sign-of-slowing-down)).

## Between the factory and the house

Most heating and cooling equipment reaches a house through a distributor and then a contractor. The largest distributor, Watsco, with about 695 locations, runs much of its business through joint ventures with Carrier, which made up 53% of its 2025 revenue; Carrier supplied 62% of what Watsco bought. Some of its supply agreements bar selling competing brands at locations that carry a maker's line ([Watsco, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/105016/000119312526082486/wso-20251231.htm)). So the contractor a homeowner calls, and that contractor's supplier, often decide which maker's equipment is offered.

## Where the analogy breaks

Cereal costs a few dollars and is bought every week, so a bad choice is cheap to fix. A furnace or air conditioner costs thousands and lasts about 8 to 20 years, and replacements, not new houses, make up 80% to 90% of home sales ([Watsco, 2025 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/105016/000119312526082486/wso-20251231.htm)), often when the old unit has failed. And cereal makers sell the same box in every store; equipment makers sell some brands only through certain contractors or distributors, so which names a homeowner sees depends on whom they call.

## What people argue about

**Costs or coordination.** The makers attribute the rise in equipment prices to materials, supply chains, efficiency and refrigerant rules, and tariffs, in their public statements and filings ([ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/163729-equipment-prices-keep-rising-as-r-410a-inventory-decreases); [ACHR News](https://www.achrnews.com/articles/166938-carrier-residential-hvac-rebounds-but-price-hikes-loom)). The plaintiffs argue that a concentrated industry used those cost increases as cover to raise prices further, and quote Lennox's chief financial officer telling investors in September 2024, "shares don't shift a lot in our industry" ([amended complaint](https://www.locklaw.com/wp-content/uploads/HVAC-Amended-CPT.pdf)). The complaint calls the industry "highly concentrated"; the 2020 concentration score it cites, "over 1,588," falls in the range federal antitrust agencies call moderately concentrated, 1,000 to 1,800 ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/atr/herfindahl-hirschman-index)), and predates the Bosch and Rheem purchases.

**Whether mergers help.** In approving Whirlpool–Maytag, the Justice Department accepted that imports and big retailers would keep prices in check and that the merger would save costs ([Justice Department](https://www.justice.gov/archive/opa/pr/2006/March/06_at_187.html)); appliance prices have kept falling since. The economists who studied that merger found price increases for some products afterward ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17476.pdf)), and in 2015 the department argued that a further appliance merger would harm builders and other buyers.

**Brand names.** Makers say they use several brands to reach different contractors, distributors and buyers; Lennox sells its own brand directly to dealers and its other brands through distributors ([Lennox, 2024 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1069202/000162828025004859/lii-20241231.htm)), and Goodman said its Amana line had features its Goodman line lacked, such as higher efficiency and quieter operation ([Goodman, 2008 annual report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1314655/000119312509053772/d10k.htm)). For a homeowner, the brand on the box doesn't say which company made it.
