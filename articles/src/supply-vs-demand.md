## In one sentence

Demand-driven inflation is too much spending chasing what the economy can make; supply-driven inflation is the economy making less, or paying more to make it; and the Fed's only tool works on the first.

## The analogy

A restaurant raises its prices for two very different reasons. One: the dining room is packed every night, there's a line out the door, and the owner can charge more because people will pay it. Two: the fish supplier doubled its prices, or the sous-chef quit and the replacement costs more, and the owner charges more because the meal costs more to make. The menu shows the same new price either way.

The responses are opposites. In the first case, a cover charge thins the line and prices settle; that is what the Fed does with interest rates. In the second, a cover charge doesn't bring the fish price down or the chef back. It just empties the room, leaving high costs and no customers: the stagflation signature, prices up and activity down. That is why raising rates into a supply shock is a judgment call rather than a fix.

The way to tell which kitchen you're in is to look at what's rising. Demand inflation shows up everywhere, in haircuts and rents and restaurant tabs, because everyone has more to spend. Supply inflation shows up in the specific thing that got scarce: oil in 2022 and again in 2026, eggs in 2023, cars in 2021. On the almanac that is the gap between headline and core inflation. When headline runs far above core, the kitchen's costs are the story. When core climbs with it, the room is full too.

## Where the analogy breaks

A restaurant owner knows which problem she has. The economy doesn't, not in real time, which is what the whole of 2021 was about, and most episodes are both at once: the supply chains broke the same year the relief money arrived. A supply shock can turn into a demand problem if wages chase the prices and the chase becomes expected, which is why the Fed sometimes raises rates into a shock it can't fix: not to lower oil, but to keep oil from becoming everything. And the kitchen's cost can be the Fed's doing in the other direction: higher rates raised mortgage costs and slowed building, which made the housing part of inflation worse before it made it better.

## What people argue about

**Which kitchen 2021–24 was.** Supply started it and demand kept it going is the closest thing to a consensus, and each side takes the half it wants: supply, so the hikes were unnecessary; demand, so they were why it ended. The San Francisco Fed publishes a monthly split of inflation into supply-driven and demand-driven parts; the split is itself a model, and the camps argue about it too.

**Whether to raise rates into the 2026 oil shock.** The Fed raised in September and held in October, with two members wanting another increase. The case for: core prices and producer prices are showing early signs of the shock spreading, and the 1970s show what waiting costs. The case against: core is near 2.5%, the job market is already soft, and rates cannot produce oil. The chart shows headline against core. Which side is right will be visible in core, later. {{check core_cpi.value <= 3.0 "core near 2.5%"}}

**Whether government can fix the kitchen.** When the problem is supply, the argument moves from the Fed to Congress: ports, housing permits, immigration, tariffs, energy leases. One side says these are the only tools that work on a supply shock and the Fed should hold while they act. The other says they take years, most are contested on their own merits, and in the meantime something has to keep expectations anchored. Both agree that rates don't make fish. They disagree about what to do while there is no fish.
