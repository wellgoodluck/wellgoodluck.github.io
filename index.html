    How to read this page

    The big number is the latest official reading. The date beside it is the period it describes, which is usually a few weeks behind today, because that is how long the agencies take to count.

    The bar underneath ranks that reading against everything the indicator has done since records began. It spans the typical range, the middle 90 percent of that history, and the dot sits at the reading's rank: halfway along, at the small notch, means half of all past readings were lower, which is what the sentence under it says in words. That middle reading is the median, and the label above the notch gives it. A dot near either end means today is unusual; a dot in the middle means it is not, whatever the headlines say. A hollow dot pinned at either end, with a gray arrow pointing outward, means the reading is outside the typical range altogether, which is rarer still. The numbers at the ends are the readings at the edges of that middle 90 percent. Because the bar shows rank rather than amount, the same distance along it can stand for a small change in one place and a large one in another.

    At a glance, at the top, puts every number's bar in one list so you can scan them all before reading any one in full. Each row links to its tile. Prefer the tiles alone? Hide the overview to leave the list out.

    In short, under each section heading, says what that section's numbers show in a few plain sentences. It is written by the same code that fetches the figures, from the readings and their ranks you see on the tiles, so it changes when they do and never says more than they show.

    The line in plain terms restates the reading as something a household would feel: what last year's $100 buys, the payment on the median home at this rate, how many people the unemployment rate represents. It is computed from the same data each time the figures refresh.

    The 2015–19 average is the last five full years before the pandemic. It is not a target, just a reasonable stand-in for what "normal" felt like recently. Its tick on the bar sits at that average's own rank in the history.

    What things cost shows prices as charged at the store and the pump, but compares them with the past after taking out inflation, because a raw price almost always sets a record eventually. Their typical range and 2015–19 average are in today's dollars; the chart and the year-ago figure are the prices people actually paid.

    The chart shows the full history, with recessions shaded as dated by the National Bureau of Economic Research. Use the buttons to zoom in.

    Add context opens one indicator at full size, with context you can turn on: reference lines such as the Fed's target, companion series such as PCE inflation or the U-6 rate, and bands showing who held the presidency and Congress, who chaired the Fed, and when major events happened. The main page stays plain on purpose; the context is there for readers who want it, each piece with its source and selection rule.

    Revisions. Agencies revise their numbers as more data comes in, sometimes substantially. This page always shows the current figures, so a month's reading here can differ from the headline that was reported at the time.
