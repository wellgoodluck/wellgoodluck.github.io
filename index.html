    How to read this page

    The big number is the latest official reading. The date beside it is the period it describes, which is usually a few weeks behind today, because that is how long the agencies take to count.

    The bar underneath shows where that reading sits in the indicator's typical range: the middle 90 percent of everything it has done since records began. A dot at the far end means today is unusual. A dot in the middle means it is not, whatever the headlines say. A small arrow at either end means the reading is outside the typical range altogether, which is rarer still.

    The line in plain terms restates the reading as something a household would feel: what last year's $100 buys, the payment on the median home at this rate, how many people the unemployment rate represents. It is computed from the same data each time the figures refresh.

    The 2015–19 average is the last five full years before the pandemic. It is not a target, just a reasonable stand-in for what "normal" felt like recently.

    The chart shows the full history, with recessions shaded as dated by the National Bureau of Economic Research. Use the buttons to zoom in.

    Full view opens one indicator at full size, with optional overlays: reference lines such as the Fed's target, companion series such as PCE inflation or the U-6 rate, and bands showing who held the presidency and Congress, who chaired the Fed, and when major events happened. The main page stays plain on purpose; the overlays are there for readers who want them, each with its source and selection rule.

    Revisions. Agencies revise their numbers as more data comes in, sometimes substantially. This page always shows the current figures, so a month's reading here can differ from the headline that was reported at the time.

    Articles explain each number in plain words, follow the numbers to five kinds of household, and take up what is argued about. They are linked from the top of the page.
